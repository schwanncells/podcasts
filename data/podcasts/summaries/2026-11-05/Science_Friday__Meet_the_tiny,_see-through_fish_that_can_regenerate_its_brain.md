# Science Friday — "Meet the tiny, see-through fish that can regenerate its brain"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guests:** Dr. Dennis Ottebye (neuroscientist, MIT's Whitehead Institute for Biomedical Research; studies regeneration in the nervous system); Dr. Alejandro Sanchez Alvarado (president and chief scientific officer, Stowers Institute for Medical Research; 30+ years studying planarian flatworm regeneration).

## TL;DR

- **Micro glassfish discovered in 2019 from a fish keeper magazine** — Dennis Ottebye found the species through an article about glassfish being raised in a museum in Bolton, UK, then obtained a starter culture of roughly 40 fish from labs in Utah and Berlin.
- **Complete transparency allows observation of internal regeneration** — the fish's organs are on full display, including its brain, making it unlike opaque model organisms such as salamanders and zebrafish that are not built for observation of internal regeneration.
- **First complete cellular atlas of a vertebrate body at single-cell resolution** — researchers sequenced hundreds of thousands of cells from across the fish's body (internal organs, fins, distinct body parts), identifying the complete set of cell types and their gene expression patterns in a single organism.
- **Fish exhibits extreme brain regeneration** — when brain tissue is damaged, the fish can regrow it; neuron-producing niches are distributed throughout the brain, and the cellular atlas revealed these stem cell locations prior to regeneration experiments.
- **Howard Hughes Medical Institute investing a billion dollars** — the organization is funding large-scale resource development to establish the micro glassfish as a major new model organism for regeneration research.
- **Key caveat: the fish is an adult larva, not fully developed** — progenesis (skipping developmental steps) left the fish devoid of bones and scales, so findings must be framed in the context of an adult larva, and it remains unclear how much will apply to adult human brain regeneration.
- **Cutting-edge research tests whether differentiated cells can change identity** — instead of introducing new cells or killing damaged ones, researchers are testing whether existing cells can reprogram themselves to promote regeneration; nature provides examples including the salamander retina's pigmented epithelium re-differentiating into a lens, and the immortal jellyfish reversing its life cycle when stressed.
- **Male glassfish produce audible vocalizations** — males vocalize during aggressive encounters and mating; researchers recorded these sounds and played them back to attempt to stimulate egg-laying.

**Discovery and Establishing a New Model Organism**

Dennis Ottebye was searching for a regenerative organism where he could directly observe regeneration happening in real time. His previous PhD work had been limited to snapshots of regeneration in flatworms, which are master regenerators but not transparent. During a late-night search through a fish keeper magazine in early 2019, Ottebye discovered an article about micro glassfish being maintained at a museum in Bolton, UK. He reached out to the two labs then working with the species—one in Utah and one in Berlin—and they generously sent him a starter culture of roughly 40 fish in plastic bags.

Establishing the glassfish in the lab required becoming, as Ottebye notes, part fish biologist and part aquarium expert. Everything was a variable: water conditions, pH, conductivity, temperature changes. Male fish produce audible vocalizations—sounds Ottebye recorded and attempted to play back to stimulate breeding, with limited success in his hands. After extensive experimentation, Ottebye and his team achieved consistent egg-laying and grew the colony, making further research possible.

**Brain Regeneration and the Cellular Atlas**

The glassfish's regenerative abilities quickly became apparent. When researchers clipped the tail or damaged the brain, the fish recovered extremely well. This extreme regeneration capability opened the door to studying the molecular and cellular basis of the process.

Ottebye's team took advantage of the fish's diminutive size and complete transparency to generate something unprecedented: a complete cellular atlas of an adult vertebrate body at single-cell resolution. They sequenced hundreds of thousands of cells from internal organs, fins, and distinct body regions, identifying which genes were active in each cell type and how much they were expressed. The atlas revealed roughly 20,000 genes expressed in humans; in each cell, a specific subset turns on to define that cell's type. The researchers discovered that across the body, there are hundreds of distinct cell types, each with its own genetic signature. Critically, the atlas was spatially resolved—they could observe which cell types neighbor each other in the brain and throughout the body, revealing the organizational architecture of a complex animal. This organizational information proved invaluable: the atlas showed neuron-producing niches—stem cell locations—distributed throughout the brain, providing a roadmap for understanding how regeneration actually proceeds when the brain is injured.

**Why the Glassfish Complements Existing Model Organisms**

The glassfish's major advantage is its complete transparency. Most other vertebrate systems used to study regeneration—salamanders, zebrafish, killifish—are opaque. Their internal organs are hidden, making it difficult to observe the cellular action where regeneration occurs. The shift to the glassfish mirrors a pivotal moment in biology history: in the 1970s, scientists moved from the relatively opaque Drosophila fruit fly to the transparent nematode C. elegans (Caenorhabditis elegans), allowing direct visualization of cellular behavior when molecular changes were introduced. Ottebye's glassfish represents a similar leap—a transparent vertebrate system where regeneration research can happen in real time with clear visual observation.

The Howard Hughes Medical Institute's billion-dollar investment in developing the glassfish as a model organism has validated this potential and is rapidly generating tools and resources to expand the field's capacity.

**The Developmental Context: Progenesis as a Caveat**

Alejandro Sanchez Alvarado raises an important caveat. The glassfish's transparency is the result of progenesis—a developmental process where the fish skipped several developmental steps on its way to adulthood. Unlike fully developed vertebrates, the glassfish is devoid of bones and scales, making it essentially an adult larva, similar to the axolotl. This raises a critical question: will the regeneration mechanisms discovered in an adult larval brain apply to understanding adult human brain regeneration in a fully developed nervous system? Findings must be carefully framed within this developmental context. The answer is still unknown, but Sanchez Alvarado stresses that understanding this distinction is essential before translating findings to human medicine.

**Cell Identity and Plasticity: The Cutting Edge of Regeneration Research**

Sanchez Alvarado describes his current research focus: testing whether differentiated cells retain absolute terminal differentiation or whether they are "stably differentiated" with plasticity that allows reprogramming. When an adult brain regenerates, neurons are replaced. The question is whether neurons are completely locked into their fate or whether they retain some flexibility—whether they can temporarily stop being neurons to allow new cells to be produced and then integrate into existing circuits.

Sanchez Alvarado is testing the hypothesis that differentiated cells are far more plastic than previously believed. Rather than killing damaged cells or transplanting new ones, the goal is to understand whether existing cells can change identity, reprogram themselves, and make the environment conducive to regeneration instead of scarring.

Nature provides intriguing proof-of-concept examples. In the late 1800s, researcher Wolf discovered that in salamanders, when the lens is removed from the eye, the pigmented epithelium of the retina—a supposedly terminally differentiated cell type—actually de-differentiates, loses its pigmentation, enters proliferation, and re-differentiates into a lens. Another striking example is the immortal jellyfish (Turritopsis dohrnii), which when stressed undergoes complete life-cycle reversal, with supposedly terminally differentiated adult cells reverting to a polyp state through a process not yet understood. These examples demonstrate that nature has already solved the identity-switching problem in certain contexts; the challenge is understanding how and whether those principles can be harnessed for regeneration research in other systems.

**Future Directions**

Ottebye's larger research goals are to understand the dynamics, molecular basis, and cellular basis of regeneration—the structural mechanics of how missing brain components get replaced—and how brain activity changes during regeneration. He is investigating whether regeneration in the adult brain involves developmental-like neural activity patterns, similar to what occurs when neural circuits first form during embryonic development. Understanding these processes at a fundamental level is a prerequisite for eventually translating findings to human medicine, particularly for neurodegenerative diseases where specific cell types are selectively lost. While direct clinical applications remain distant, Sanchez Alvarado emphasizes the importance of expanding the diversity of organisms scientists study. The broader biological world likely holds regenerative capabilities we have not yet sampled or understood, and the glassfish is just one of many organisms that may reshape what we know is possible.

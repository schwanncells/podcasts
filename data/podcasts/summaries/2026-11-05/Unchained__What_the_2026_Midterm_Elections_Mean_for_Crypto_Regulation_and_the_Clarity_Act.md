# Unchained — "What the 2026 Midterm Elections Mean for Crypto Regulation and the Clarity Act"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guests:** Mike Katz (partner, Frontier Tech, Monat; cryptocurrency and tech policy advisor); Justin Slaughter (senior advisor, Paradigm; board member, Long-Term Stock Exchange; former House Financial Services Committee staffer and congressional oversight investigator).

## TL;DR

- **Democrats swept Congress in a historic wave, winning by 13 percentage points** — the largest Democratic midterm victory since 2006. This is the end of the "long 2016" where politics was dominated by Donald Trump, and new millennial members in Congress may have more open minds on crypto than expected.
- **The Clarity Act is dead; Democrats are writing their own crypto market structure bill** — House Financial Services staff is drafting legislation with four components: stricter ethics rules for government officials' crypto holdings, much stricter DeFi requirements, 24-hour payout requirements and mandatory audits, and jurisdiction shifts from the CFTC to the SEC for some activities.
- **Elizabeth Warren will chair the Senate Banking Committee and Sherrod Brown likely leads Agriculture** — Warren is expected to launch intense oversight efforts targeting executives for investigations and hearings, while Brown is focused on reelection in 2028 and unlikely to aggressively pursue crypto issues.
- **Investigations into Trump family crypto deals are the crypto industry's target** — Democrats are expected to investigate World Liberty and other Trump crypto enterprises for self-dealing, quid pro quo, and foreign influence, particularly from the UAE and Saudi Arabia.
- **Industry super PAC spending proved ineffective; Fair Shake's $56 million in Ohio did not sway Sherrod Brown's election** — money matters in politics but is not decisive. The crypto industry's strategy must shift from showing "war chest" power to engaging Democrats in good faith on merits they understand.
- **SEC and CFTC rules are unlikely to change despite Democratic control** — agency leadership (SEC Chair Atkins and CFTC Chair Selig) will likely continue their current approach. The real risk is riders on appropriations bills that could pause rulemaking on stablecoins, DeFi, or derivatives.
- **The crypto industry faces an urgent two-year window to rebuild relationships with Democrats** — the industry needs a "reset" that explains crypto's benefits in terms Democrats care about (affordability, community banking, financial inclusion) rather than confrontational messaging, before the 2028 presidential race.

**A Historic Election and the End of the Trump Dominance Era**

The 2026 midterm elections delivered what Katz calls "a blue wave tsunami." Democrats won Congress decisively, winning the popular vote by 13 percentage points — compared to 8 points in 2006, the previous great Democratic midterm wave. Texas, Kansas, South Carolina, and potentially Mississippi flipped Democratic, upending the political landscape of the past decade.

Slaughter frames this as the end of the "long 2016," where political narratives were shaped primarily by Donald Trump and a Republican base that consistently supported him. For the first time in years, politics is not dictated primarily by Trump's actions and Democratic antagonistic responses. The new Democratic majorities include a cohort of young millennial members in their late 30s and 40s, many of whom have crypto exposure or know people who do. Several new senators—Annie Andrews, James Tallarico, Adam Hamilton, and Roy Cooper—have not yet publicly staked crypto positions, creating what Katz describes as an opportunity to "write a different kind of story" rather than assume all new Democrats are hostile to the industry.

**The Clarity Act's Death and the House's New Crypto Market Structure Bill**

Slaughter confirms that the Clarity Act is dead and will not pass in the lame-duck session before the new Congress convenes. The industry's failure to pass clarity despite controlling both houses of Congress under a Republican majority represents a significant strategic miscalculation.

In its place, House Financial Services Committee staff are reportedly writing their own crypto market structure bill with four key components. First, much stricter ethics requirements for government officials' crypto ownership and involvement in crypto companies—a direct response to the Trump family's crypto dealings. Second, significantly stricter rules on DeFi operations, going well beyond the DeFi-adjacent provisions in Clarity. Third, consumer protections similar to those Maxine Waters proposed during Clarity markup, including 24-hour payout requirements and mandatory independent audits. Fourth, a shift of regulatory jurisdiction from the CFTC to the SEC for some asset classes and activities.

According to Slaughter's sources, roughly 80% of the Clarity Act provisions are acceptable to Democrats on staff, but the parties remain in conflict over 20% of the bill—and that contested 20% includes provisions the industry particularly cares about. Slaughter warns that the industry should be watching DeFi regulation closely, as it is likely to face stricter requirements than the CFTC's approach.

**Warren, Brown, and the Democratic Party's Internal Fights**

Elizabeth Warren is poised to lead the Senate Banking Committee. Contrary to some misreadings of her positions, Slaughter notes that Warren is "a scourge of large banks" but favors smaller community banks and credit unions, not a ban on banking itself. Sherrod Brown, who won reelection in Ohio, likely will chair the Agriculture Committee, which has jurisdiction over stablecoins and prediction markets.

However, Slaughter argues Brown is unlikely to be a major obstacle for the crypto industry. Brown won his race by only 4 percentage points—less than Annie Andrews won in South Carolina—despite a Democratic +13 national environment. He underperformed significantly in his home state and faces a tough reelection battle in 2028 in an unfavorable state. Slaughter's analysis suggests Brown will prioritize his reelection campaign over pursuing aggressive crypto investigations.

Warren is expected to be more aggressive. Reports indicate she has reviewed a 181-page dossier of potential oversight targets across executive suites, prediction markets, Wall Street, manufacturing, and crypto. Slaughter anticipates "a flurry of letters and hearings" aimed at many industries, with crypto among them. However, he notes that Warren's power is constrained by "down dais" Democratic members—fresher, junior senators on the Banking Committee who are unlikely to want to spend political capital on a "holy war against all of corporate America" heading into a 2028 presidential race.

**The Trump Family Crypto Investigations**

Katz and Slaughter predict that Democratic-controlled committees will prioritize investigating Trump family crypto dealings. They expect investigations into World Liberty, meme coins, and other Trump crypto enterprises, with particular focus on questions around self-dealing, quid pro quo arrangements, foreign funding from the UAE and Saudi Arabia, and any political donations in exchange for pardons or dropped cases.

Slaughter notes that within hours of the Binance hack, Maxine Waters tied it to the Trump pardon of CZ and the dismissal of Binance cases, signaling that Democrats see an investigative opportunity. However, he cautions that there is "not much they can do" to unring the bell on the dropped cases themselves; the SEC under Chair Atkins operates with a different enforcement philosophy.

Katz sees an opportunity in this targeting dynamic. While the oversight spotlight is focused on Trump family crypto businesses, other crypto companies and projects can work with pragmatic Democrats to find "more productive solutions." He argues the industry should explicitly embrace investigations into Trump family crypto enterprises as a corruption issue separate from supporting sensible market structure legislation.

**Agency Rules and Regulatory Continuity**

Despite Democratic control of Congress, Slaughter does not expect the SEC or CFTC to reverse or significantly change their recent rules. SEC Chair Atkins and CFTC Chair Selig are understood to be aware of past chairs' mistakes in attempting too many simultaneous rulemakings; Atkins is reportedly focused on doing "a few rules" well rather than 30 or 40 rules broadly.

The real risk is indirect: riders attached to appropriations bills that could pause or defund rulemaking on stablecoins, DeFi derivatives, or other areas. Slaughter notes that Democrats at leadership level "do not by and large care that much about crypto," and the incoming freshmen have limited focus on it. However, he flags that some subset of Democratic staff—maybe 30 or 40 people in circles around figures like Warren—do want to vindicate the Biden administration's crypto crackdown and seek "revenge" for its perceived failures.

**The Fair Shake Spending Debacle and Strategic Reappraisal**

Fair Shake, the crypto industry's primary super PAC, spent $56 million in the 2026 cycle, focusing heavily on Ohio (targeting Sherrod Brown) and one other race. Sherrod Brown won reelection. Slaughter notes two overlooked facts about Fair Shake's strategy. First, they played in only two races, not broadly across the country, despite pressure from both Republicans (to expand spending) and Democrats (to spend in races like Maine). Second, the industry failed to recognize that there are diminishing returns to super PAC spending in high-profile races. In Texas, total outside spending exceeded $500 million—orders of magnitude more than Fair Shake spent anywhere—and Democratic candidates still won decisively.

Slaughter emphasizes his core takeaway: "You cannot think of super PAC spending as your first weapon. It is your last weapon of last resort." The belief that a large "war chest" would intimidate Democrats into supporting the industry's preferred legislation has proven false. Money in politics matters, but "it is not the end all be all."

Katz agrees that the industry must shift strategy. He notes that late-stage Clarity negotiations involved 12 Democrats who were not simply looking for "a fig leaf" to move the bill—they are an "earnest party" that refuses to sign onto legislation they cannot justify on the merits. Saying "we have this big money cannon" will not persuade them and may actively hurt the industry's position. Engagement must be "in good faith on the merits in ways they understand."

**The Path Forward: A Two-Year Reset**

Both Katz and Slaughter emphasize that the crypto industry faces a critical two-year window before the 2028 election. The industry's current strategy of waiting out Democratic control and hoping for a sympathetic administration in 2028 is a "gigantic mistake." Democrats are unlikely to wait; banks and other players opposing crypto are not waiting; and industry must engage now.

Slaughter advocates a complete reframing of the industry's political messaging. Rather than arguing from first principles about "crypto's revolutionatary potential" or showcasing a "war chest," the industry should explain benefits in terms Democrats and their constituents understand: cheaper, faster payments; better ways of moving money; more open financial infrastructure; benefits for community and regional banks in states that have been left behind; and financial inclusion and affordability for regular voters.

The industry must also engage with good faith and acknowledge legitimate criticisms. Slaughter notes that the "extremism" on both sides—anti-crypto purists like Elizabeth Warren and zealous industry maximalists—share more similarities than differences and alienate centrist Democrats who could be persuaded. He points to Austin Campbell, a crypto commentator, as someone who was "ostracized and criticized" for critiquing the Trump family's crypto involvement while remaining pro-crypto, but he is likely vindicated by the Democratic focus on those investigations.

Katz stresses that the industry cannot "sit around and wait it out." Humility, strategic engagement, and a reset focused on "how this helps Democratic constituents" is the path to building political credibility over the next two years.

**Data Centers and Texas Politics**

Governor-elect Gina Yosafat, a Democrat who defeated Republican Greg Abbott in Texas, has proposed a moratorium on new data center approvals pending new rules on electricity and water costs to protect Texas residents. Slaughter notes that the governor's office in Texas has significant power over data center approvals.

However, Slaughter's reporting suggests the moratorium will likely not halt already-approved construction or projects already underway—it will prevent new facilities from beginning. The impact is more significant for hyperscalers pursuing AI infrastructure than for Bitcoin miners. He notes that roughly 15,000 gigawatts of data center power is coming online in other countries in the near term, and he would advise companies heavily concentrated in America to diversify internationally. Other countries see data center investment as a way to influence AI development, though the Trump administration is reportedly opposed to offshore concentration of AI infrastructure.

Slaughter characterizes Yosafat as "a bit more moderate" than headlines suggest and notes that Abbott himself moderated his data center stance as "political winds shifted" in 2026.

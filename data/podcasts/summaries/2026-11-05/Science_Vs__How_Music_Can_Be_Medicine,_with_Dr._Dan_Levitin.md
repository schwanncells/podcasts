# Science Vs — "How Music Can Be Medicine, with Dr. Dan Levitin"

**Host:** Wendy Zuckerman. **Guest:** Dr. Dan Levitin (Emeritus Professor, neuroscientist; musician who has worked with Santana, Sting, and Steely Dan; author of *I Heard There Was a Secret Chord: Music as Medicine*).

## TL;DR

- **Music activates more regions of the brain than anything else studied.** It engages rhythmic centers, visual cortex, and the default mode network (the brain's restorative state), with millions of neurons synchronizing to a song's tempo.
- **Listening to music releases endogenous opioids in the brain** — the same type of natural painkiller found in morphine or oxycodone. Music listening raises pain thresholds measurably, though the effect is not pharmaceutical-level. Disliked music does the opposite: releasing cortisol and adrenaline.
- **Music memory is exceptionally strong and durable** because melodies, rhyme schemes, and repeated exposure create mutually reinforcing memory pathways. Songs from ages 10–12 are particularly resistant to forgetting and are often the last memories to degrade in Alzheimer's disease.
- **For Alzheimer's patients, music from their teen years reconnects them with their sense of self** — a 20-minute session can restore speech and orientation for about a week, even in individuals who no longer recognize loved ones.
- **Music helps Parkinson's patients walk better by providing an external beat.** After about four seconds of synchrony with a song matching their natural walking speed, patients bypass degraded basal ganglia circuits and can walk in rhythm. Weeks of practice can create new neural pathways, allowing walking without music afterward. A metronome alone does not work because music engages the whole brain.
- **The "Mozart makes you smarter" claim is bad science.** The original study conflated correlation with causation (paper-folding ability with intelligence), lacked proper controls, and did not test actual intelligence. No music genre improves cognition unless the listener already enjoys it.
- **Fetuses can hear music in the womb from week 20 onward** (though amniotic fluid filters out high frequencies). Infants recognize and prefer songs heard prenatally, showing that in-utero exposure creates durable memories.
- **A Gallup poll found Gen Z's most common coping strategy for emotional distress was listening to music** — more common than social media or spending time with friends.

**The Reach of Music Across Brain Regions**

Music engages a wider network of brain regions than any other stimulus studied. Levitin explains that millions of neurons synchronize to a song's tempo in the rhythmic centers, causing listeners to move or dance involuntarily. The visual cortex also activates during music listening and composition. In some cases, people report vivid visual imagery accompanying music—trees, rivers, cityscapes—appearing as a guided mental journey. Levitin notes that composer Sting visualizes music as architecture, describing "different levels of a house or a building and buttresses and arches and a number of stories and rooms and filigrees" when composing. (Levitin himself has aphantasia, the inability to form mental images on command, and so does not experience this visual accompaniment, though he can dream in images.)

**The Default Mode Network and Mental Restoration**

One of the brain's key networks is the default mode network, the daydreaming state that activates when attention is not focused on a task. Music reliably triggers this restorative mode, which opposes the central executive network—the focused, task-oriented state. After prolonged concentration, neural resources become depleted, and the brain needs restorative time to restore oxygenated glucose. A true restorative break is not scrolling social media but going for a walk in nature, napping, daydreaming, or listening to music. Levitin describes his personal practice: when he feels his attention flagging, rather than reaching for coffee, he closes his eyes and meditates or puts on music, and within five to ten minutes feels refreshed. This practice is particularly important for knowledge workers whose jobs demand sustained focus.

**Music and Dopamine: Pleasure and Motivation**

Music listening triggers dopamine release in different brain regions for different functions. In the limbic system, dopamine creates the sensation of warm pleasure. In the frontal lobes, it generates motivation to pursue healthy behaviors. This is why people can push through a painful exercise workout when music is playing—the steady dopamine supply sustains them past the point their muscles would otherwise give up. Music also modulates serotonin, which stabilizes mood.

**The Pain-Relieving Mechanism: Opioids and Beyond**

Levitin's lab was the first to demonstrate that listening to music one enjoys triggers the release of endogenous opioids in the brain—the same compounds present in morphine and oxycodone. The caveat is critical: the music must be music the listener enjoys. Disliked music not only fails to release these natural painkillers; it releases cortisol and adrenaline, increasing stress sensitivity and slowing critical thinking.

In laboratory experiments using the cold pressor test (subjects immerse their arm in ice water to the elbow until pain becomes unbearable), music listening extends pain tolerance. Most people can last one to two minutes; with music, they last longer, demonstrating that music raises pain thresholds by acting as an analgesic. Levitin notes that the historical example of Manuel Noriega's surrender in Panama illustrates this principle in reverse: U.S. forces blasted him with Van Halen music he despised, and combined with the removal of water, air conditioning, and food, it drove him to surrender.

However, Levitin cautions that the analgesic effect, while significant and measurable, is not powerful enough to replace surgical anesthesia. The dental field has discovered that music can minimize or eliminate the need for Novocaine or nitrous oxide in many patients, suggesting the effect is real but modest. The main mechanism appears to be opioid release, though music's distracting and engaging properties likely contribute as well.

**Why Music Memory Is Uniquely Durable**

Music memory occupies a privileged position in the brain. While only about 10% of people dislike music, most remember songs vividly throughout life. Several factors reinforce music memory: melody and rhyme schemes create mutually reinforcing patterns that lock together in specific ways, and the pieces of a song can only be arranged in certain combinations. Additionally, over the past 70 years, pop culture has exposed listeners to repeated plays of songs during limited windows (weeks or months), after which older songs drop from rotation. This creates strong associations between a song and a specific time or place in the listener's life, and a single musical cue can unlock a flood of memories from that period.

Most people develop their musical tastes around ages 10 to 12. This timing is crucial: early memories are the last to fade in neurodegenerative disease. Gallup research found that when Gen Z feels upset, listening to music is the single most common coping strategy—more common than social media or seeing friends.

**Music and Alzheimer's Disease**

A general principle of memory states that first-encoded memories are last to deteriorate. Because people typically begin attending carefully to music around age 10–12, songs from that period persist even as Alzheimer's strips away more recent memories. Patients may not recognize their spouses or even themselves in mirrors, yet they retain memories of their fourth-grade classroom, seventh-grade bedroom, or music from their teens.

Tony Bennett famously continued singing his entire repertoire of hundreds of songs in advanced Alzheimer's disease because he learned those songs as a child. He no longer knew what day it was and sometimes sang the same song three times in a row without realizing, yet the music remained intact.

In care facilities and assisted living, playing music from a resident's teen years (rather than recent pop music) can have profound effects. Someone who does not recognize loved ones or themselves may become agitated or withdrawn because nothing makes sense. Playing familiar music from their formative years allows them to reconnect with the self they thought they had lost. A single 20-minute session can restore speech and orientation for approximately a week. Levitin suggests that individuals should consider creating Alzheimer's playlists for themselves now, drawing from music heard between ages 12 and 18—the songs most likely to survive into late-stage cognitive decline.

**Music Therapy for Movement Disorders**

Research has shown that music helps patients with Parkinson's disease and multiple sclerosis walk more smoothly and with better strides. When people listen to music, their brains synchronize with the beat. In Parkinson's disease, circuits in the basal ganglia degrade, impairing the brain's internal clock that coordinates smooth, sequential movements. Patients often "freeze" and cannot walk because this internal timing mechanism is damaged.

When music with a tempo matching the patient's natural walking speed is introduced, something remarkable occurs: after about four seconds of synchrony, Parkinson's patients can walk in rhythm to the music. They effectively bypass the broken basal ganglia circuit by using music as an external clock for the motor system. This approach has been described as a "game changer" for treating Parkinson's symptoms. The protocol involves listening to music (typically 15 minutes daily) while walking, and after weeks or days of this training, patients can often dispense with the music and walk normally for several weeks, suggesting that new supplementary motor circuits have been strengthened or created.

Critically, a metronome alone does not produce this effect. The initial hypothesis—that only the beat matters—has been tested and disproven. Metronomes fail because they do not engage attention or captivate the listener the way music does. The therapeutic effect requires the whole brain's involvement.

**The Mozart Myth**

The claim that listening to Mozart (or classical music generally) makes people smarter is, in Levitin's words, "balderdash"—a result of poorly conducted science. A foundational study proposed that 20 minutes of daily Mozart listening would enhance intelligence without any need for reading, math, or other intellectual effort. This study suffered from multiple methodological flaws: it measured intelligence by a paper-folding task (similar to simple origami), which correlates with some measures of spatial reasoning but is not a measure of general intelligence. More fundamentally, the control condition was "do nothing," not "listen to a different genre or activity." Subjects who listened to Mozart improved relative to sitting in a dimly lit room doing nothing, but that says nothing about Mozart's unique cognitive benefit.

In fact, no music genre improves cognition unless the listener already enjoys it. The effect of music on motivation and mood varies entirely by individual preference. As Levitin notes, citing his grandfather: "If we all liked the same thing, everybody would want to get with your grandma."

**Prenatal Music Exposure and Fetal Development**

By week 20 of gestation, a developing fetus's auditory system is fully functional. Sound travels through amniotic fluid, which filters out high frequencies (similar to hearing music underwater in a bathtub, where primarily bass and rhythm remain audible). A study by Levitin's colleague Alexandra Lamont played specific songs to pregnant women around 20–24 weeks gestation, then tested the infants one year later. When played either the same songs or different songs by the same artist from the same album, infants showed a clear preference for the songs they had heard in the womb—demonstrating recognition and memory formation that persisted from fetal life into infancy.

The commercial market for fetal music speakers makes optimistic claims about intelligence enhancement. One experimental study conducted by researchers testing whether musical prenatal exposure affected fetal behavior used a novel approach: comparing music played to the fetal belly via speaker against music played intravaginally during ultrasound. When music was played intravaginally, fetuses showed specific mouth and tongue movements; when played to the belly, they did not. This suggests some effect of direct sound transmission, though the practical implications remain limited.

**Putting Music to Practical Use**

Levitin emphasizes that the evidence supports music as a genuine therapeutic tool—not magic, but neuroscience. The takeaways for practical use are clear: listen to music you enjoy to gain dopamine and opioid benefits; use music as a restorative break between focused work rather than reaching for caffeine; create a personal Alzheimer's playlist from formative years; and for those with movement disorders or depression, consider music listening as a complement to other treatments. The key variable is always individual preference: the music that works is the music that resonates with each listener.

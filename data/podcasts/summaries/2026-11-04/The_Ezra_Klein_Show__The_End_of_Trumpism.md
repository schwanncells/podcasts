# The Ezra Klein Show — "The End of Trumpism"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Aaron Redica (colleague and editor at Ezra Klein's publications; conducts this episode as an interview of Klein).

## TL;DR

- **The "blue tsunami" redrew the electoral map** — Democrats won in states Republicans considered locked (Kansas, Texas, Iowa, South Carolina, Mississippi, Ohio), with the last Democratic senator from Kansas winning in 1932 before Adam Hamilton's 2026 victory.
- **Trump betrayed his mandate through political malpractice** — he promised to lower costs, end wars, and bring order at the border; instead, he raised costs through tariffs, launched new wars (Iran), and increased border disorder through ICE raids, never pivoting when public support collapsed.
- **Democrats won by running candidates who fit their states** — Adam Hamilton (Methodist megachurch pastor, pro-life Democrat in Kansas), James Tallarico (seminarian revival in Texas), Josh Turek (Paralympian with spina bifida in Iowa); this broke the partisan geographic sorting that had locked states as safe red or blue.
- **Historic voter realignment across multiple groups** — young voters aged 18–29 swung 32 points toward Democrats, male voters +17 points, Hispanic voters +28 points; the specific cause was voter turnout and real-world consequences (war, inflation, state force on immigration) rather than abstract persuasion.
- **Democrats now control governorships in states worth 392 electoral votes** — a historic shift after winning governorships in Texas, Ohio, Iowa, and Georgia; gubernatorial control matters for redistricting and state-level power.
- **Trumpism as ideology has collapsed — there was only ever Trump** — right-wing intellectuals tried to extract coherent policy (tariffs, foreign policy restraint) from Trump's impulses, but Trump himself never held to any of it; infrastructure is already fracturing into infighting ("the neocons did this") with no clear successor (J.D. Vance underperformed in Ohio and lacks popular support).
- **Trump's motive is loyalty and profit, not ideology** — he cares about keeping his family out of jail and their companies profitable; he doesn't mind Republicans losing when he's not on the ballot because it proves his individual importance; a second term under exhausted staff will be more erratic.
- **A religious left is rising with moral language** — Tallarico, Hamilton, El-Sayed, and Ossoff are speaking in biblical cadence and the language of virtue in response to Trump's moral depravity; this "Love Thy Neighbor Coalition" represents a thermostatic response and a new Democratic opening.

**The Historic Democratic Midterm and the Destruction of the Red-Blue Map**

Ezra Klein opens by describing the 2026 midterm as the destruction of the political map people had believed in. Democrats won in places "nobody thought they had any chance even to compete in a year ago"—Kansas, Texas, Iowa, South Carolina, and Mississippi (where the Senate race remained unclear as Klein recorded). Democrats won governorships in Ohio, Texas, and Iowa. The most striking historical marker: the last Democratic senator from Kansas was George McGill, who won in 1932—92 years prior to Adam Hamilton's 2026 victory.

Klein attributes this victory to two forces: what Trump did wrong and what Democrats did right, and both came down to listening. Trump dismissed what voters were angry about and never pivoted. Democrats, historically unwilling to listen to voters rather than themselves, found candidates who fit their states: seminarians, Paralympians, and others who could put previously off-the-table places into play.

**Trump's Political Malpractice and Betrayal of His Mandate**

Trump was elected promising to lower costs, end wars, and bring order at the border. Instead, Klein states, he "raised costs through tariffs and through new wars, and he created disorder after closing the border and sending in masked ICE agents to harass and upend settled communities." Trump never changed course or admitted what was happening. His party paid the price.

Klein argues this represents catastrophic political malpractice: Trump had a mandate from voters but betrayed it entirely. Donald Trump led his party to defeat—or created the conditions for the party to go to defeat. Aaron Redica notes that Trump, somewhat mysteriously, does not appear upset by the results. Klein explains that this is because Trump doesn't mind when Republicans lose when he is not on the ballot; Republican losses when Trump is not present actually serve his interests by demonstrating his individual importance. His post-election True Social post—"if I had been on the ballot, we would have won in a landslide"—crystallizes this logic.

**The Strategy That Won: Candidates Who Fit Their States**

Democrats succeeded by running a diverse coalition of candidates tailored to their regions rather than ideologically homogenous to the national party. Adam Hamilton in Kansas is a Methodist megachurch pastor and a pro-life Democrat; James Tallarico led a "religious left revival in Texas" against Ken Paxton; Josh Turek, a Paralympian with spina bifida, ran in Iowa. Mary Patola in Alaska ran on "Faith, Freedom, and Fish." Dr. Annie Andrews in South Carolina ran progressive healthcare positions while fitting the state.

Klein emphasizes that "fit the state" does not mean "moderate"—Tallarico is fairly progressive on some issues (populism, building) while moving right on oil and gas and border security. The common thread is that these candidates either had records of appealing to Trump voters or understood their states deeply enough to navigate cross-party appeal.

This strategy vindicated Howard Dean's 50-state strategy, which held that even in unwinnable states, investing in candidate recruitment and development builds party infrastructure and surfaces talented politicians who strengthen the coalition. Klein notes that even candidates who ran in less favorable conditions—Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, Sarah Jackson in Maine, Angie Nixon in Florida—did not perform terribly; the lesson is not that progressive candidates hurt Democrats, but that the strongest overperformers were those who fit their regions.

**The Historic Voter Realignment**

Klein and Redica examine the specific voter swings. As Klein reports from 2026 House results, male voters swung 17 points toward Democrats. Voters aged 18–29 swung 32 points. Hispanic voters swung 28 points. These swings are historic in scale.

However, Klein cautions about interpreting these shifts as pure persuasion. Roughly 150 million people vote in presidential elections; midterms draw 110–120 million. Many Trump voters from 2024 may have simply not turned out in 2026. Redica notes that turnout and persuasion cannot be cleanly separated from voting data, but Klein's point is that the true swing voters—people who vote inconsistently—are often the decisive group.

**Historical Parallels and Their Limits**

Klein draws parallels to prior wave midterms. In 2006, Democrats won big and then Obama won the presidency in 2008 with a Democratic Senate supermajority. In 2018, Democrats won the House and then won the presidency in 2020. But 1994 brought a huge Republican midterm victory followed by Clinton's 1996 presidential win. And 2010 brought a Republican wave before Obama's 2012 re-election.

Klein's point is that wave midterms are not a straight line to victory in the next election. The party that wins must be thoughtful about governance, candidate selection, and strategy. Midterms reflect current discontent; they do not guarantee future success unless the party uses the mandate wisely.

**How Democrats Lost and Why Musk and Others Failed**

Klein notes that where Democrats ran more left-wing candidates in contested races—Michigan (Abdul El-Sayed), Maine (Troy Jackson, after moderate Jared Golden retired from the reddest House district), Florida (Angie Nixon)—those candidates did not overperform the environment. El-Sayed won by roughly 7-8 points in a Democratic +13 environment; that is not an electorally impressive performance. This does not mean progressive candidates are bad members of Congress or lack important ideas; it means the electoral lesson from 2026 is that state-fit candidates outperformed ideologically sorted national candidates.

Klein and Redica discuss how Elon Musk and Trump-backed candidates collapsed in particular. Vivek Ramaswamy lost Ohio's gubernatorial race to Amy Acton, running a campaign that included an ad by Acton's estranged mother attacking her for alleged sexual abuse—"grotesque" and "reprehensible," Klein says. Ken Paxton in Texas lost. Roger Marshall in Kansas lost after imposing brutal 18% interest rates on patients.

Musk spent roughly $500 million, with a large plurality going to Texas specifically. Yet Texas collapsed for Republicans—the dream of a "Blue Texas" that seemed fantastical for decades has materialized. Musk's money, Klein argues, didn't just fail to help Republicans; it likely hurt. Klein speculates that frontloading "unabashed cruelty"—Paxton's treatment of immigrants, Ramaswamy's personal attacks, Marshall's medical predation—may have been electorally poisonous.

**Governors and Electoral Math**

Klein notes that Democrats now control governorships in states representing 392 electoral votes—a historic concentration of Democratic executive power. This matters not only for 2028 but for redistricting and state-level governance heading into the 2030s.

**Trumpism Has Collapsed; Only Trump Remains**

Klein argues that "Trumpism" was never a coherent ideology—there was only ever Trump. Right-wing intellectuals and pseudo-intellectuals tried to extract coherence from Trump's impulses: a structured tariff regime to bring manufacturing home, an end to neocon Middle East wars, a rearrangement of foreign policy away from liberal internationalism. Trump himself never held to any of it.

His tariffs were, Klein says, "insane." He did not end neocon wars; he got into Iran with Benjamin Netanyahu, not because of some principled foreign policy shift. Trump himself is already blaming neocons for his failures ("the neocons did this to us"), but Trump got into Iran—the neocons didn't. Marco Rubio and Bill Kristol are convenient scapegoats for Trump's own decisions.

The fracturing has already begun on the right. J.D. Vance is being positioned as what "real Trumpism" could have been—an intellectual nerve center of coherent populist nationalism without war. But Vance is not popular; he underperformed badly in his own Ohio Senate race. No one finds "Vanceism" appealing.

Trump's actual motive is loyalty and profit. He wants to keep his family and associates out of jail, maintain their corporate profits, and ensure loyalty from Republican officials. He has no coherent ideology. He is "not an ideas guy." The Republican Party "all knows it to be true." And so the movement will fracture into infighting over what comes next, with Trump focused purely on maintaining control.

**Trump's Likely Trajectory in a Second Term**

Klein raises the specter of Trump's second term. Trump is eighty years old, increasingly "discursive and all over the place," in tenuous contact with reality, and facing an exhausted staff less able to challenge him. Trump does not care about Republicans winning in 2028; he wants to maintain control. Redica suggests that Trump may actually prefer Republicans to lose in 2028 while embracing him to winning while running away from him.

Klein lists the converging crises: the economy is shaky, built on an AI CapEx boom that could easily collapse; the Iran war could escalate; AI and cyber threats; China and Taiwan; Russia and Ukraine; Trump's health. He invokes Percy Bysshe Shelley's poem "England in 1819"—"An old, mad, despised and dying king"—to capture the historical moment. Shelley asks what "glorious phantom" will emerge from this wreckage. Klein worries less about the glorious phantom than about whether Trump, who doesn't adapt to events and doesn't listen, can navigate cascading crises.

**Investigation and Corruption**

Klein argues that Democrats must investigate Trump administration corruption not out of revenge but because it is genuinely vast. USAID, the Department of Education, the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau—agencies were destroyed. Immigration policies created mass upheaval. Klein and Redica want Democrats to "follow the money": Who bought what from the Trump administration? What did the Qatari jet cost? How many billions has the Trump family made during the crisis people lived through?

John Ossoff has been effective at corruption messaging by focusing narrowly—one small thing at a time—allowing citizens to conceptualize a coherent whole. The challenge is that the corruption is so vast that without focus, it becomes incoherent. Klein suggests starting with financial flows into and out of the Trump ecosystem: contracts to Trump family companies, Jared Kushner's Gulf-funded private equity fund, crypto deals, and the like.

**Democratic Governance: Resistance, Compromise, and Agenda-Setting**

Klein outlines three governance challenges for Democrats. First is resistance: fighting Trump, investigating him, holding him and his family accountable, sending bills that create visible contrasts. Second is compromise: potentially passing permitting reform, AI regulation, and immigration bills, working with Trump where interests align. Third is power-building and agenda-setting: hammering out Democratic positions on healthcare reform (Medicare for all policies), AI, pre-K, and child care for the 2028 platform and 2029 governing agenda.

Hakeem Jeffries and Chuck Schumer have kept Democratic messaging focused on affordability and ending the Iran war, not distracted by secondary issues. This discipline was part of why the election message landed.

**The Rise of the Religious Left and the "Love Thy Neighbor Coalition"**

Klein sees an opening in the rise of a "religious left" and a "moralistic left" that speaks in the language of virtue. James Tallarico, Adam Hamilton, Abdul El-Sayed, and John Ossoff all deploy biblical cadence and moral language. Ossoff, Klein notes, is "really good at speaking in a biblical cadence, thick with language of virtue." This represents a thermostatic response to Trump's moral depravity and an opportunity to rebuild politics "explicitly moral within the moral wreckage of Donald Trump."

This coalition—Tallarico, Hamilton, El-Sayed, Ossoff—is what Klein proposes naming the "Love Thy Neighbor Coalition." It offers a new language for American politics that reclaims moral authority from the right and centers virtue, compassion, and faith-based arguments.

**Who Lost and Who Won**

Klein and Redica close by reflecting on who they are happy to see go and glad to see arrive. Klein is satisfied to see Susan Collins go; she wore "the mask of the moderate" but voted with Republicans 83-85% of the time and was the chief enabler of the destruction of reproductive rights through her Supreme Court votes.

Redica is happiest to see politicians fail when they front-loaded unabashed cruelty: Ken Paxton, Vivek Ramaswamy, Roger Marshall. Marshall, an OB-GYN, imposed 18% interest rates on his own patients.

On the flip side, Redica is optimistic about the rise of this religious left and its moral language. Klein agrees that this represents a genuine opportunity to rebuild a politics centered on virtue and moral seriousness in the face of Trumpian depravity.

# Bits + Bips — "Why the AI Rally Keeps Growing — and Why Circle Launched Arc"

**Host:** Laura Shin (Unchained). **Guests:** Austin Campbell (high scholar, Zero Knowledge Group), Ram Alawalia (Maester of Wealth, leader of Lumina), Chris Perkins (managing partner, 250 Digital Asset Management; venture investor; former Lehman Brothers associate).

## TL;DR

- **AI infrastructure picking and shovels dominating markets** — semiconductor companies (Micron, SanDisk, Lumentum) have delivered unprecedented returns; Micron alone is on a 10X run as a large-cap security, trading at 8x forward P/E with $800–900 billion in data center CapEx flowing this year.
- **Iran-Hormuz conflict recalibrated but unresolved** — 73+ days of tension with Israel; CENTCOM blockade effective; oil "higher for longer" but military confrontation unlikely to fully resolve; markets have normalized to $50–60/barrel baseline.
- **Institutions flooding into crypto via Circle's Arc launch** — Circle (a public company) raised $222 million for Arc tokens at $3 billion FDV; investors include BlackRock, Apollo, ICE, SBI, Janus Henderson, Goldman—marking first public-company token presale and legitimizing token investments as institutional securities. USDC is native gas token on Arc EVM L1.
- **Coinbase's layoffs framed as AI restructuring but driven by spot trading collapse** — 14% headcount cut (~700 people); Q1 showed $394M net loss, $1.41B revenue vs. $1.52B estimate; spot trading down 37% QoQ; derivatives and deposits (not spot) are the profitable paths forward.
- **Crypto industry maturity questioned over Consensus strip club after-party** — official event at Bitcoin-accepting Miami venue drew institutional backlash; crypto brands distancing themselves, signaling the industry is moving beyond the 2018 playbook toward institutional adoption.
- **AI bubble is in private markets, not public** — 63 unicorn AI labs with billion-dollar valuations (including Ilya Sutskever's company) generate zero revenue; Europe has zero comparable LLMs; public markets have exercised discipline while private VCs subsidize innovation.
- **Value accrual questions for Circle and Arc** — stock up 15% on Arc announcement despite token issuance being dilutive; market may be pricing in Arc's eventual spinoff as a standalone payment rail (Visa/Discover precedent) rather than permanent Circle subsidiary.

**Geopolitics: Iran-Hormuz and the Path Forward**

Austin opens the discussion with Iran's escalating demands and CENTCOM's effective physical blockade that effectively cut off oil transit. The conflict remains on "massive life support" (Trump's phrase), with 73 days of Israeli-Iranian sparring. Austin frames this as both a military and treasury market contest, with Iran needing to outlast the U.S. dollar system rather than defeat it militarily.

Ram dismisses the treasury-market threat as "a nothing burger," pointing out that U.S. sovereign debt remains the best-backed asset globally—backed by military might, AI leadership, productivity growth, and demographics far better than Europe or Japan. He notes that CENTCOM's initiation of the blockade turned the tide by becoming coherent policy; before then, China was brokering deals and shadow-fleet tankers were leaking through. Now China "is on its back heels," with even a Chinese tanker being seized by the IRGC.

Chris argues markets are "very, very resilient"—geopolitical shocks fade as other forces take over. He points to Milken Institute discussions where the dominant sentiment is optimism around data center stimulus and CapEx stimulus offsetting any Hormuz noise. However, China is materially exposed to energy markets, and Trump is going to the China summit with leverage.

Austin pushes back that the Iranians' demand to recognize sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz won't fly with Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Iraq, and Oman. Those cartel members won't accept Iran militarizing the strait after these attacks. This suggests oil remains "moderately higher for longer."

**The AI Rally: Unprecedented Semiconductor Run**

Ram crystallizes the current market narrative in three words: "It's just AI." The entire rally is explained by AI picks and shovels—semiconductor companies like Micron, SanDisk, Lumentum, and CN. Micron's 10X run as a large-cap security is unprecedented. Ram compares the capital flight to water draining from a beach before a tsunami—all capital is flowing from everywhere else into this one theme. Record call option activity and record 3X levered ETF activity are evidence of maximum FOMO and anxiety.

Chris notes that at Milken, the consensus was bullish on U.S. outperformance. The U.S. has three structural advantages: the AI lead, energy dominance, and demographic favorability. Europe has none. The CapEx figures—$800–900 billion this year—suggest the cycle has legs.

Ram acknowledges the anxiety is real—whipsaw risk, the question of whether to hold or buy now. Semiconductors are now 18% of the S&P (a record high; the previous record was 11% in 2014), which historically signals rotation time. However, he points to the dot-com era, when valuations were truly nutty (geo.com going 600% on IPO day). Today's situation is different: profitable large-cap companies (not startup-backed dark fiber) are the primary spenders, and they're proving ROI through cloud business growth (Google Cloud up 60%, Microsoft Cloud up 40%).

Chris adds that the lack of current regulation allows startups to jump in, but future regulation will consolidate the market toward the incumbents. He notes that there are 63 unicorn AI labs with billion-dollar valuations, none of which generate any revenue—the bubble is in private markets, not public. Europe has zero comparable LLMs, which is the scarier fact.

Austin argues that value creation and value allocation are two separate questions. The macro question—is AI writ large a bubble?—is separable from the allocation question—is value accruing to the right companies? He's bullish on AI overall but skeptical of whether capital is flowing to the right winners. Nvidia and the semiconductor complex are clear winners; the question is whether consultancies (Accenture) deploying AI, or the agentic solutions (Claude, Microsoft's newly rolled out agentic framework), will be priced in.

**Circle's Arc Blockchain and Institutional Legitimization**

Austin introduces Circle's $222 million Arc token presale at a $3 billion fully diluted valuation. The investor list reads like a TradFi roll call: a16z ($75 million), BlackRock, Apollo, ICE (owner of the New York Stock Exchange, not the immigration agency), SBI, Janus Henderson, Stanchart Ventures, General Catalyst. This is the first public company to run a token presale.

Arc is an EVM-compatible L1 with USDC as the native gas token. It's been running on testnet since October 2025 with hundreds of millions of transactions; BlackRock, Visa, Goldman, AWS, and Anthropic have been testing on Arc.

Chris frames the presale as legitimizing token investments for traditional finance—investors are buying tokens, not private equity. The fact that so many TradFi firms are investing signals a narrative shift toward acceptance.

Austin raises the central puzzle: Circle stock went up 15% (~$4.5 billion market cap increase) on the Arc announcement, even though the token issuance should be dilutive. He compares it to 3Com in the dot-com era, where a subsidiary was worth more than the parent company. The only way these valuations are coherent is if the market is pricing in a future Arc spinoff—a standalone payment rail (like Visa or Discover).

Ram agrees that the market is focused on the financial engineering ("how can they create value out of thin air?") rather than on what Arc actually does. He notes that Coinbase has a perpetual exclusive right on Circle's stablecoin revenue, so Arc's economics don't tap into USDC revenue directly.

Chris pushes on the value prop: is Arc going to preference USDC as the gas token, or will it be neutral and let other stables compete? Austin notes that Arc only supports USDC as the gas layer, which is a problem for institutional adoption. Banks like Chase, BoA, and Citi won't give up their net interest margin (NIM) to use Arc unless they can use their own deposit tokens. A real institutional rail would need to be multiasset—deposit tokens from various banks competing against each other.

Austin argues this is why Visa and Discover spun out of their original owners: you can't control the payments rail if you're also issuing the payment instrument. If Arc is going to work as a 24/7 fast-payments infrastructure for institutions, it probably needs to be spun out.

Regarding the retail exclusion: this token presale involved only institutional investors. Retail was left out in the cold unless they own Circle stock. This marks a shift where "the game's just in public markets now."

**Coinbase's Layoffs, Maturation, and Market Resilience**

Austin pivots to Coinbase's rough week: 14% headcount cut (~700 people), CEO Brian Armstrong framing it around AI-native pods and player-coaches. But the Q1 earnings were brutal: $394 million net loss, $1.41 billion revenue (vs. $1.52 billion estimate), EPS of –$1.49 (vs. +$0.27 estimate), spot trading volume down 37% QoQ, and a five-hour AWS outage on Friday that Armstrong called "completely unacceptable."

Chris notes that this is a pattern we've seen before in financial services: spot trading fees trend to zero over time (like in equity markets). The profitable businesses are derivatives and deposits. Coinbase acquired Deribit to address the derivatives gap.

However, Chris remains bullish on Coinbase due to clarity—the potential passage of the Clarity Act would trigger regulatory consolidation, and larger incumbents benefit from regulatory moats. Coinbase has diversified beyond spot (custody, derivatives), and the Circle perpetual stablecoin revenue contract is recurring revenue.

Ram adds that the stock was up 7% on the layoff news—cutting headcount boosts earnings. The main competition is from ETFs and spot fee compression, but recurring revenue from Circle and the custody business offer stability.

Austin agrees that Coinbase needs to own the customer end-to-end, like Schwab or Robinhood—banking, investing, payments—rather than chasing spot margins. Ram is bullish on this positioning and notes his own company Lumina is building an app that's "already better than Coinbase and Robinhood" (though without trading enabled yet).

**Crypto Industry Maturity and the Consensus Backlash**

Austin closes with the Consensus Miami after-party held at a Bitcoin-accepting strip club, with hours-long lines and Bloomberg headline: "Crypto industry throws lap dance party in middle of bear market." This was the second time (2018 Bitcoin conference also held a similar event at the same venue).

Chris draws the parallel to Lehman Brothers in 2006, where the associate orientation's first message from leadership was "no strippers." That era, Wall Street matured past these incidents. He argues that "this is not how you get to institutionalization"—the industry needs to grow up, especially now that it's trying to appeal to institutions like BlackRock and Goldman.

Austin notes that it's unclear whether the brands involved even knew they were being associated with the event. Chris agrees that brands are important and due diligence will tighten going forward.

**Market Summary and Forward Outlook**

The episode captures a market in an extraordinary state: an AI-driven bull market that has concentrated in semiconductors at historic levels, with institutional capital moving into crypto for the first time (via Circle's Arc), while geopolitical risks simmer (Iran-Hormuz) but don't derail risk appetite. The tension is between bubble fears in private markets (63 unfunded AI labs) and public market discipline, between the maturity that institutional buyers demand and the immaturity that still clings to crypto's brand in the broader culture.

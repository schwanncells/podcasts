# Time To Say Goodbye — "The Big Fishing Episode"

**Host:** Tyler (co-host). **Guests:** Jerry Audette (managing editor, Surfcasters Journal; host, Surfcast podcast; author of *Hooked: An Anthology of Fresh and Saltwater Game Fish*; accomplished surf fisherman in the Northeast).

## TL;DR

- **Surf casting is fishing from shore with lures into shallow waters adjacent to land**, a sport that encompasses many styles from passive beach fishing to "wetsuiting"—wading deep into the ocean at night in wetsuits to catch fish 20–50 pounds on 11-foot rods.
- **Striped bass are the obsession**: incredibly versatile prey that can be caught on fly rods, live bait, or artificial lures, drawing serious anglers into the sport because each individual fish demands different tactics and patterns.
- **Success requires obsessive commitment**: fishing 4–6 nights per week is necessary to master the sport's granular variables (wind direction, moon phase, tide, bait fish migrations), because fishing once or twice weekly yields almost no success.
- **Wetsuiting carries real life-or-death risk**: strong tidal currents, 20+ foot tide swings, and night conditions in the Atlantic create hazards; one angler was pulled 700+ yards offshore after becoming trapped in a tide rip, and Jerry nearly drowned crossing a jetty when his waders filled mid-current.
- **Coastal access is under siege from wealthy property owners and governmental retreat**: fishing clubs (once powerful advocates for public land access) are weakening, and Jerry fears private equity could soon acquire formerly protected national seashore land like Cape Cod's 33-mile stretch—though birder advocacy groups currently serve as a counterbalance.
- **Localism and secrecy protect fragile spots**: the fishing community guards specific locations fiercely—even close friends withhold spot names from each other to uphold ethics—because just a few disrespectful anglers (leaving garbage, using lights, parking poorly) can ruin an entire location.
- **The sport's brutality is its barrier and its appeal**: 90% of fishing is lonely, cold, boring, and unsuccessful; only 5–10% involves dramatic success. This inherent unpleasantness deters casual participation and requires a peculiar obsessive personality type, making it difficult to grow the sport despite public articles about it.
- **Writing about fishing requires honesty about rawness and drama**: unlike curated fly-fishing narratives or surfing media that carefully control story, surf casting's extreme conditions (night fishing in dangerous waters with heavy lures on rocky jetties) generate genuine drama and tension that "writes itself."

**What Surf Casting Is and Why It's Obsessive**

Jerry explains that surf casting fundamentally means fishing from shore with lures into shallow adjacent waters. Within this broad category exist many styles—from passive beach fishing with a rod wedged in a spike (a PVC tube) and a beer cooler, to the extreme variant Tyler and Jerry practice: wetsuiting, which involves wearing a wetsuit, deep wading sometimes into swimming depths, and casting from sandbars and rocks 70–80 yards offshore. The fishing happens almost exclusively at night between roughly 10 p.m. and 3 a.m., targeting striped bass weighing 15–50 pounds.

Striped bass are what elevate the sport from simple recreation into obsession. Jerry emphasizes that bass can be caught "any way you want"—on fly rods inside estuaries, live eels and bait chunks from the beach, or artificial lures (wood and plastic) cast from the water. This versatility means each angler can match their personality to their approach. The beauty, Jerry says, is that you can fish casually for two hours in bluebird skies with your son, or throw yourself completely into the extreme nocturnal hunting that Tyler and he practice.

To the question of how anyone catches anything in such a vast ocean when "you threw a hook out there and you're just sitting there with a cooler," Jerry acknowledges the apparent hopelessness. But he argues that with consistent patterning—working through all the variables systematically—anglers catch fish on many nights. Patterns become extremely specific: "the fish are going to be at this point on a northwest wind in these weeks in August when the squid are running." Success requires not luck but disciplined pattern recognition. Tyler adds that surf casting is nearly a pure meritocracy: hard work, time on the water, and willingness to fish during nasty conditions (front edges of hurricanes, extreme weather) are directly rewarded. There are few activities left in American life that work this way.

**The Demanding Commitment**

Jerry identifies a fundamental paradox of surf casting: it is an "all or nothing proposition." Fishing once or twice per week yields almost no success; you must fish 4–6 nights per week to even begin understanding the sport. This extreme commitment threshold naturally filters the participant pool to obsessives only. Unlike fly fishing, where weekend anglers can achieve reasonable competence, surf casting rewards only those willing to live the sport.

Tyler attributes much of the sport's addictive pull to this structural demand. The activity is inherently dramatic: you're wading deep into the Atlantic at night, holding a wild animal weighing 30–50 pounds while "chest deep," experiencing a "visceral wildness and nature that you just can't get standing back on the beach." He recalls his first serious wetsuit outing: paddling out to a famous rock during high tide on a rough night, spotting three silhouettes on the rock fishing in 3–4 foot surf, and realizing he had to attempt the same thing despite barely keeping his feet under him. The narrative tension is baked into the activity—you're fighting the ocean, the darkness, and your own limits simultaneously.

**Coastal Access: NIMBY Politics and Trespassing**

Both Jerry and Tyler identify coastal access as perhaps the sport's most acute tension. Wealthy property owners, especially in Massachusetts, Rhode Island, and Maine, attempt to block public beach access. Massachusetts technically guarantees a constitutional right to fish between mean high tide and mean low tide, and Rhode Island recently strengthened protections to permit public use up to mean high tide plus 10 feet. However, wealthy homeowners routinely confront anglers despite these rights, and they've installed cameras and fences to discourage access.

Tyler notes that trespassing has become routine necessity. Anglers strategize parking 15 minutes away, gear up in privacy, then execute "hit and runs"—pull in with lights off, out of the car in 10–15 seconds, and disappear into the background. They move "hypothetically" through backyards and under air conditioning units to reach fishing spots. Jerry observes that homeowners (not police, who are typically cooperative) are "the real obstacle." Tyler adds that this dynamic mirrors surf culture's NIMBY dynamics, where wealthy coastal residents claim to value the ocean's beauty while excluding ordinary people from accessing it.

Jerry fears a looming crisis. Fishing clubs, once powerful advocates for public lands access, are weakening and disappearing as younger people disengage from the sport. He specifically worries about the Cape Cod National Seashore—a 33-mile protected stretch—which could be vulnerable to private equity acquisition and development if public lands protections erode under current policy. The wealth at stake is enormous: waterfront property on this scale would be worth tens of millions. Jerry's hope rests on birder advocacy groups, who are fiercely protective of protected lands and actively push back against encroachment. Ironically, anglers often resent birders because they install piping plover fencing that cuts off fishing access, but these groups' aggressive advocacy currently prevents total privatization of critical coastal lands.

**Localism, Secrecy, and Community Ethics**

The fishing community operates under intense localism and secrecy, though paradoxically, anglers are simultaneously desperate to share. Jerry describes the old-school ethic: you must earn your stripes, prove yourself, and maintain secrecy about specific locations. Even close friends withhold spot names from each other. Jerry has a friend, Toby Lipinski, whom he's fished alongside for eight or nine years, yet Toby still refuses to name certain spots because he wants to uphold the ethics of the original angler who showed him those locations. Jerry's own code: "I have a spot" or "I was at the spot"—never naming the place, even with Tyler, his closest fishing partner.

The reasoning is pragmatic: a location can be "ruined by just a couple of anglers." The presence of disrespectful anglers—turning on lights, parking incorrectly, leaving garbage—destroys an entire spot. Jerry and Tyler are welcoming to newcomers (lending wetsuits, rods, and lures to anyone seeking to learn the sport), but they fiercely protect locations.

Interestingly, even the most hardcore old-school anglers, despite their reputation for secrecy, eventually "spill their guts" and share knowledge once conversation gets rolling. Tyler and Jerry try to honor this paradox through their writing and photography: they represent the pastime honestly and uphold its ethics while giving readers a genuine glimpse of the sport and its practitioners.

Jerry notes that the fishing community is not particularly welcoming in the old-school model (secrecy, earning your stripes), but this is gradually changing as new anglers with different values enter the sport. However, the secrecy around locations persists because the stakes are real.

**Life-or-Death Risk and Why People Stay**

Wetsuiting carries genuine mortal danger. Jerry describes two near-death experiences. In the first, he swam 700+ yards offshore after becoming trapped in a tide rip, lost orientation in the dark, and would have drowned had a boat not accidentally found him. In the second, more recent incident, Jerry was attempting to cross a jetty at night during a moon tide (when tidal currents are strongest due to lunar influence). He took one misstep, fell into dark water, lost his headlamp, and disoriented, could not determine which direction the jetty lay. His waders filled with water. Thinking through how drowning would feel—something he always prefaces his seminars with—saved him: he realized he was sinking, ditched his waders and belt (with $200–$500 worth of lures attached), jammed his rod tip down into rocks 10 feet below, held it like a flagpole to catch his breath, then pushed with the rod to turn and swim back with the current. His EMT friends watching from shore believed he was dead.

Despite these horrors, Jerry continues the sport. He emphasizes to newcomers that they must "think about what it feels like to almost die"—visualize water entering your mouth, how long you can hold your breath, when you'd abandon expensive gear. This preparation has saved his life. Yet the sport persists, suggesting the satisfaction outweighs the risk for those who commit to it.

Tyler notes that the sport's unpleasantness is actually protective: 90% of fishing is "lonely, boring, and excruciating," with only 5–10% involving "wildly exciting" success. The best fishing happens in terrible conditions. Hunting large trophy-class fish (as opposed to numerous small ones) is extraordinarily boring—you leave spots where you're catching many fish because big fish live elsewhere and behave differently. This inherent grimness discourages casual participation and wealthy bankers from simply buying a house in Montauk and joining in.

**Writing About the Sport With Honesty**

The hosts discuss a recurring tension in outdoor writing: balancing genuine transcendental experience against the risk of corniness. Tyler explains that transcendental surf writing often feels overwrought—narratives about how a Pacific storm creates energy that reaches California coasts, or about harnessing gravity and water physics. True but cliché. Yet at some point, the corn becomes truth. He admires Jerry's article because it walks this line carefully, refusing to claim false drama where none exists.

Jerry argues that surf casting avoids this trap: "There's a very raw nature to the whole thing." Unlike fly fishing or trout fishing, which in curated Eastern contexts can feel "a little curated sometimes," surf casting in the Atlantic is inherently wild. Night fishing with an 11-foot rod, 0.5-pound lures, heavy waders in 20+ foot tide swings, and genuine drowning risk—this isn't hyperbole. Writing about it feels honest because the drama is real. Tyler agrees: "fishing writing kind of writes itself" when the activity is inherently dramatic. The narrative tension (will you catch the fish? will you survive the current? will you find the spot in the dark?) is baked into the sport itself, requiring no embellishment.

**Other Fishing Obsessions and the Comparative Extremity**

Jerry situates surf casting within a broader landscape of fishing obsessions. Other communities achieve comparable intensity: Midwest and West anglers pursue sunfish and bluegill world records; musky fishermen—pursuing a fish called "the fish of 10,000 casts" because success comes roughly once per 10,000 casts—practice equally obsessive and hardcore pursuit. Fly fishing contains "elements which are even more intricate and intense" than surf casting. But Jerry and Tyler's specific niche differs in two ways: the environment (open Atlantic, not boats or rivers) and the timing (night fishing versus daytime). These factors compound the difficulty and the appeal.

**Conservation and the Future of Access**

Jerry expresses anger and frustration about public lands threats under current administration policies. While many anglers assume threats to public lands are Western issues, Jerry fishes extensively on Cape Cod's National Seashore—33 miles of protected shoreline—and fears what happens if private equity acquires such tracts. The financial incentive is immense. Historically, fishing clubs provided a powerful advocacy lever for protecting public lands. As these clubs weaken and younger generations disengage, that lever disappears.

The counterforce is birder advocacy groups, which Jerry describes as fiercely protective and unwilling to concede ground. Though many anglers resent birders (particularly for piping plover protection fencing that restricts fishing access), these groups currently stand between public coastal lands and privatization. Without their advocacy, places like the Cape Cod National Seashore would likely be developed.

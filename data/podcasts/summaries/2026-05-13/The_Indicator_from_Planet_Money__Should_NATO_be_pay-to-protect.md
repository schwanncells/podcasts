# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Should NATO be pay-to-protect?"

**Host:** Adrian Ma, Waylon Wong. **Guest:** Heather Cox Richardson (historian; author of *Letters from an American* newsletter with ~3 million subscribers; expert on how the U.S. reached its current moment).

## TL;DR

- **NATO born from World War II trauma** — designed to prevent another global conflict through three pillars: free trade + open seas, cultural/value exchange, and collective security. Article 5 (attack on one = attack on all) has only been invoked once, after 9/11.
- **Trump frames NATO as a protection racket** — says allies should "pay, and we'll protect," not as a strategic alliance but as a transaction. The Pentagon is pulling troops from Germany; Trump has also threatened to withdraw from Spain and Italy.
- **U.S. provides disproportionate high-end capabilities** — while European defense spending is increasing (now pledged at 5% of GDP vs. previous 2%), the U.S. supplies crucial infrastructure: advanced surveillance, mid-air refueling aircraft, and nuclear deterrence that European countries cannot independently provide.
- **Historical imbalance in spending** — until recently, the U.S. was nearly the only NATO member hitting the 2% defense spending target; other allies combined spent less than half what the U.S. spent. The U.S. accepted this as being in its own strategic interest.
- **Transactional security thinking breaks the alliance model** — the Trump administration views security as a fee-for-service contract ("if they don't pay, we don't protect") rather than collective defense designed to prevent world war.
- **Economic cost of NATO dissolution would be steep** — the U.S. currently spends $1 billion per day in Iran alone; NATO's common budget is only $5–6 billion per year. Historian Richardson argues the post-WWII system—of which NATO is central—enabled both American safety and prosperity.
- **European alternatives emerging** — some European leaders, including a former NATO secretary general, are already discussing creating a separate security alliance without U.S. participation.

**NATO's Strategic Origins and Article 5**

Heather Cox Richardson explains that NATO emerged from the World War II generation's determination to prevent another global catastrophe. After witnessing the horrors of two world wars, leaders wanted to create a safe world for future generations. NATO's foundation rests on three pillars. First, free trade enabled by freedom of the seas—goods and people can flow safely between countries without threat of plunder. Second, international cooperation through exchanging culture and values. Third, collective security, formalized in Article 5, which treats an attack on any member as an attack on all. This article has been invoked only once: when NATO allies rallied to support the U.S. after 9/11. Richardson emphasizes that Article 5 doesn't mean members never disagree or that war disappears entirely—it means they don't fight each other in ways that spiral into world war. The system, she argues, was central to American prosperity and power for nearly 80 years.

**Trump's Transactional Framing**

President Trump has reframed NATO as a protection racket, famously saying "if they're not going to pay, we're not going to protect." The Pentagon is pulling troops from Germany, and Trump has threatened similar withdrawals from Spain and Italy. The White House Press Secretary asserted that Europe benefits from U.S. military presence and that requests to use American bases for American interests were denied, claiming the president won't allow the U.S. to be "taken advantage of." Nikki Akani, a NATO Defense College fellow specializing in institutional warnings, describes this logic as converting NATO into a subscription service: "If you pay, you get protection. If you don't, you do not."

**NATO's Two Funding Mechanisms**

NATO funding has two components. The common budget—$5–6 billion annually—covers headquarters, communications, and organizational operations; members pay proportionally to their size. The second is national defense spending, where members commit to a percentage of GDP for their own defense. This spending is understood as contributing to the alliance's collective strength. Akani uses a school group project analogy: each student buys supplies for themselves that can also benefit the group. However, what matters is not just spending volume but how efficiently that spending translates to alliance capability. For example, if a country spends defense money on pensions rather than deployable assets, the money doesn't improve the alliance's crisis response.

**America's Outsized Defense Contribution**

Despite recent European increases in defense spending (pledged up to 5% of GDP, up from 2%), the U.S. provides a disproportionate share of high-end military capabilities essential to NATO's credibility. These include advanced surveillance systems, mid-air refueling aircraft, and nuclear deterrence. Historically, the U.S. was nearly alone in meeting the 2% GDP spending target; other NATO allies combined spent less than half what America spent. The U.S. accepted this imbalance because it believed strengthening Europe was in America's strategic interest. The Trump administration, however, views security spending as a balanced transaction where countries should exchange money for protection, rather than as a collective investment in preventing systemic conflict.

**The Cost-Benefit Case for NATO**

Richardson pushes back against the transactional logic by pointing to concrete economics. The U.S. currently spends approximately $1 billion per day in Iran alone. NATO's entire common budget is $5–6 billion annually—meaning the organization is extraordinarily "cheap" compared to military alternatives. More broadly, she argues, the post-WWII system of which NATO is the cornerstone demonstrably enabled American safety and economic strength. Dismantling it would reverse a settlement that has worked for nearly eight decades.

**European Contingencies**

Richardson notes that European allies are not waiting passively. Some European leaders, including a former NATO secretary general, are already exploring the creation of an independent European security alliance—a NATO without the U.S. Such a split would represent a historical rupture in the Western alliance forged after 1945.

# The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart — "Foreign Policy and American Hubris with Ben Rhodes"

**Host:** Jon Stewart. **Guest:** Ben Rhodes (national security analyst, author of *All We Say*; former deputy national security advisor and speechwriter to President Obama; co-host of Pod Save the World).

## TL;DR

- **Iran war cost $25 billion officially** — nearly $6 trillion total spent on post-9/11 counterterrorism globally over two decades. Trump achieved only what the JCPOA (Obama's 2015 deal) had already accomplished: Iran's uranium enriched at 3%, stockpile shipped out, international inspections of mines and facilities.
- **Iran now treats Strait of Hormuz like a toll road** — has closed shipping to charge transit fees after demonstrating it can inflict billions in damage on Gulf allies' infrastructure; Qatar's offshore gas field (world's largest) was bombed and taken offline, threatening food and power supplies across Europe.
- **U.S. military infrastructure in the Gulf is badly damaged** — UAE, Qatar, and other allies' facilities sustained billions in repairs, but damage is being suppressed; local governments forbid media documentation, signaling extent of Iranian capabilities.
- **Gulf states are hedging away from U.S. security guarantor model** — Saudi Arabia, UAE, and Qatar historically traded oil/investment returns in exchange for American military protection, but war has shattered that model; they're now exploring alternative partnerships with China and Russia.
- **Netanyahu's strategy has shifted from stability to managed chaos** — Israel now benefits from endless regional conflicts (Iran, Lebanon) that provide cover for West Bank settlements, ethnic cleansing in Gaza and Southern Lebanon, and territorial expansion; permanent war keeps Netanyahu in power.
- **Most heavily sanctioned countries (Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia) are worse, not better** — sanctions entrench authoritarian regimes and empower hardliners (like Iran's IRGC); lifting sanctions could reduce repression more effectively than maintaining them.
- **Congress never votes on these wars** — Libya intervention lacked congressional authorization (only UN Security Council resolution); constitutional requirement to seek congressional approval would sharply reduce military interventions because Americans and elected representatives oppose them.
- **$6 trillion spent on war on terror, but Americans killed by gun violence annually exceeds 9/11 death toll by orders of magnitude** — never triggers equivalent military response; reveals the "commander-in-chief test" where presidents must demonstrate willingness to bomb to appear credible.

**The Iran War and the Return to the JCPOA**

Rhodes explains that when the Obama administration evaluated Iran's nuclear program, two paths emerged: negotiated limits with inspections (what became the JCPOA) or bombing. War games consistently showed bombing could not destroy the program—knowledge and scientists cannot be destroyed from the air—and would trigger catastrophic costs: Iran would likely close the Strait of Hormuz (through which 20% of global fossil fuels flow), leading to global economic damage. Israel's Netanyahu repeatedly pitched bombing to Obama with identical presentations ("very consistent briefings"), but the analysis was clear.

Trump withdrew from the JCPOA and chose bombing. Now, a decade later, the best outcome he can achieve is an agreement "very much like" the deal he abandoned. The JCPOA had Iran's uranium enriched at only 3%, its stockpile shipped out, and allowed International Atomic Energy Agency inspections of mines, mills, enrichment facilities, and storage sites. A UN Security Council coalition (U.S., Russia, China, France, UK, Germany, EU) verified the deal. Under Trump's bombing, those verification mechanisms and international consensus are gone—and Iran is now further from accepting restrictions than before.

The war has cost $25 billion officially, though Rhodes suspects the true figure is higher. Trump is caught between the political cost of extending the conflict (Americans are suffering economically—oil, fertilizer, and gas prices spike) and his ego—he cannot accept merely returning to what Obama negotiated without claiming victory. Meanwhile, Iran has become more hawkish, with the Iranian Revolutionary Guard Corps (the most militaristic faction) now holding greater power and credibility.

**The Strait of Hormuz as a Toll Road and Regional Damage**

Iran has not just demonstrated it can close shipping; it has demonstrated it can *profit* from controlling the strait. After successfully hitting Gulf shipping and letting tankers through selectively, Iran is now collecting transit fees—operating the strait like the Suez Canal. Rhodes notes a parallel: Egypt nationalized the Suez Canal after war, then collected revenue for decades. Iran is doing the same.

Qatar's liquid natural gas field (the world's largest) was hit by Iranian strikes and is offline. This cripples Qatar economically and threatens LNG supplies to the UK, which depends on that gas for power—creating food shortages and cascading instability in Europe. The UAE's infrastructure also suffered extensive damage; officials forbid media documentation, a signal that Iranian missiles and drones penetrated defenses far more than public reporting admits.

Stewart points out the irony: Trump's war has likely flooded Iran's coffers beyond the $1.7 billion under the JCPOA, simply through higher oil prices and strait tolls. The Gulf states—especially Saudi Arabia and the UAE—are realizing their security guarantee from the United States is not what it once was. They are beginning to hedge, exploring partnerships with China and Russia.

**Israel's Shift Toward Managed Chaos**

Rhodes describes Israel's current strategy as fundamentally different from the stability-seeking approach of past decades. Netanyahu now benefits from perpetual regional conflict: wars with Iran and Lebanon provide political cover for expanding settlements in the West Bank, ethnic cleansing in Gaza and Southern Lebanon, and territorial annexation. Permanent war and fear-mongering keep hardliners like Ben Gvir (national security minister) and Smotrich (finance minister) in the coalition and Nathan Netanyahu in power longer than any previous Israeli premier.

These ministers openly call for West Bank annexation, ethnic cleansing of Gaza, and territorial grabs from Lebanon and Syria—"not fringe," Rhodes stresses. Israel is not just displacing Palestinians; it is rendering Southern Lebanon uninhabitable by destroying tens of thousands of olive trees, making return impossible. Some of this is pure territorial expansionism justified by religious claims ("this book says it's ours").

Stewart notes the fundamental contradiction: Netanyahu undermines Palestinian moderates while empowering Hamas, ensuring any Palestinian counterpart is too weak or delegitimized to negotiate. This prevents a credible two-state path. Rhodes observes that the pattern mirrors what happened with Hezbollah and Hamas in Lebanon—Israeli military operations *strengthened* those groups by giving them credibility as defenders of their populations. Killing Iranian military leadership and the political operator Lara Jani (a negotiation partner) leaves only the IRGC as the institution that can claim to stand up to the U.S. and control the strait, *increasing* their credibility and power in Iran.

**Structural Problems in American Foreign Policy**

Rhodes identifies a core issue: policymakers sit in Washington believing they can "move pieces around a board"—sanction or bomb a country and achieve a desired political outcome. This mindset has consistently failed. The heavily sanctioned countries (Cuba, Iran, North Korea, Russia) are worse now, not better. Sanctions entrench regimes, empower hardliners (Iran's IRGC thrives in black markets), and hurt ordinary citizens more than elites.

When it comes to choosing military intervention, Rhodes argues the infrastructure itself is self-sustaining. The U.S. has built bases, intelligence platforms, and special forces networks across North Africa, the Middle East, and South Asia over two decades. This machinery "constantly sees enemies"—a group gains territory in Somalia, a bad actor appears in Mali. Or Trump wakes up with an idea. The infrastructure becomes self-justifying.

Libya exemplifies the slip into endless intervention. It began as a humanitarian mission—save Benghazi from Gaddafi's army during the Arab Spring. A UN Security Council resolution authorized a no-fly zone. But once the military operation started, "mission creep" took over. Stopping Gaddafi at Benghazi's gates wasn't enough; they decided regime change was necessary. The lesson Rhodes emphasizes: "There's not a record of a regime change supported by the United States through military force that made a place better." Whether the rationale is terrorism, nuclear proliferation, or local politics, American military power cannot engineer foreign political outcomes.

**Congressional Authorization and the Constraint of Democracy**

Rhodes highlights a constitutional brake that has been ignored: wars require congressional authorization. Presidents have sidestepped this since 2002, operating under the Authorization for Use of Military Force (AUMF) passed after 9/11 for counterterrorism—now extended indefinitely.

When Obama faced pressure to bomb Syria after a chemical weapons attack in 2013 (the infamous "red line" moment), he submitted the question to Congress. Congress refused to authorize it—not because of ideology, but because members know war is unpopular (it costs money, American lives, and produces bad outcomes). Marco Rubio, a hawk who had criticized Obama for inaction on Syria, voted against authorization. If presidents had to ask Congress before each military action, Rhodes argues, military interventionism would drop sharply.

Stewart observes that every president who campaigned on ending interventions (Obama 2008 on Iraq, Trump 2016 on "forever wars") has continued or expanded them. Rhodes blames the media, think tanks, and the foreign policy "blob"—a discourse machine that generates pressure for "action." The media frames every global problem as "what could America do to help?" rather than simply reporting what happened. Jake Tapper in Libya yelled, "How many more must die before you put in a no-fly zone?" Stewart notes the strange assumption that it is the media's job to hold presidents accountable for *every* bad thing happening in the world.

The fix is structural: end the 2002 AUMF and require congressional votes. Follow the Constitution. But this requires political spine from both parties—Democrats especially resist being labeled "weak" on foreign policy.

**Sanctions as Entrenched Failure**

Rhodes proposes a controversial idea: lift sanctions on Iran. Sanctions have made the Iranian government more repressive and empowered the IRGC, which profits from black market trade. Ordinary Iranians are squeezed. Rhodes argues that engagement—trade, diplomacy, example-setting—works better over time than coercion. No country has been sanctioned into democracy.

Stewart pushes back: why do we do deals with terrible people (Saudi Arabia) while harshly sanctioning others? Rhodes notes the contradiction is by design. Military sales to Gulf states, crypto investments by Trump associates, defense contractor profits—these benefit powerful interests insulated from the instability they create. Arms dealers, fossil fuel companies, and financial speculators profit from chaos. The stock market has *risen* during the Iran war because these actors are unharmed by global instability; they trade it for profit.

**The Loss of Rhetoric and Argument**

Rhodes's new book, *All We Say*, traces American identity debates through 15 historical speeches from Benjamin Franklin to the present. He observes that America once had a culture of long-form argument—speeches printed in newspapers, speaker circuits (Frederick Douglass), radio speeches (FDR), and television charisma (JFK, Reagan). These allowed complex argument and persuasion.

The internet and social media have fractured this. Attention is infinite and competitive; algorithms polarize people into tribes; information arrives as bite-sized, anger-inducing content. No one sits through a full argument anymore. Politics has devolved into "jubilee sessions" where people raise flags and land one-sentence viral owns instead of actually persuading.

Rhodes argues this degradation of rhetoric has degraded our politics. When you can't make arguments, you can't persuade anyone—you only talk to people who already agree with you. Part of what needs to change is recovering storytelling and longer-form argument about who Americans are and where they're going. Without this, political discourse remains disoriented and driven by narrative momentum rather than actual analysis.

**Conclusion: A Foreign Policy Reset**

Stewart and Rhodes conclude that Americans are ready for a reset. They want leaders to speak to them "like grownups," acknowledge that the post-9/11 security state has failed, and redirect resources to schools, hospitals, and climate action. The war on terror cost $6 trillion—imagine what that could have funded. There is no "winning" condition for indefinite military sprawl. Prevention of catastrophic attacks (like 9/11) does not require a massive empire; it requires effective, targeted intelligence and special operations, not constant bombing campaigns that radicalize populations and create refugee crises.

Rhodes's bottom line: the solution is not isolationism but *engagement as equals*—trade, diplomacy, climate collaboration, AI governance. Stop assuming America can control events. Stop military interventions absent congressional approval. Let other powers build roads; America can still be a partner. This requires accepting that sometimes bad things happen in the world and America cannot prevent them—and that trying to do so, at vast cost, only makes things worse.

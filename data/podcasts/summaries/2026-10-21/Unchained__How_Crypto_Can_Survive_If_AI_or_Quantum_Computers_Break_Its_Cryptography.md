# Unchained — "How Crypto Can Survive If AI or Quantum Computers Break Its Cryptography"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guests:** Alex Pruden (co-founder and CEO, Project 11; Ethereum Foundation advisor on post-quantum roadmap); Connor Deegan (co-founder and CTO, Project 11; cryptographer).

## TL;DR

- **ECDSA (elliptic curve digital signature algorithm) is potentially vulnerable to both quantum computers and advanced AI**, unlike hash-based cryptography which relies on randomness rather than mathematical structure that AI could exploit.
- **AI-driven cryptographic breaks are happening now, not in the distant future**—OpenAI solved ~300 math problems in one paper, AI labs have already broken two non-production schemes (HAWK lattice-based and MACALIS schemes), and Justin Drake framed this as "A-Day" happening "maybe now, last month, sometime around now."
- **Quantum computers threaten ECDSA but we have timeline flexibility**—earliest realistic quantum attack estimated at 2030, with better probability window early-to-mid 2030s (2033–2035); recent Google and Quantinuum results on error correction suggest earlier timelines are more likely but engineering challenges remain substantial.
- **Public key encryption (not just signatures) faces an unsolvable problem in a post-AI/quantum world**—no hash-based replacement exists for key exchange encryption used in TLS and privacy protocols like Zcash; blockchains mostly use signatures (solvable) rather than encryption (not solvable), but privacy-preserving protocols do rely on encryption.
- **Elliptic curves have held for 30+ years with a "$1 trillion honeypot" testing them**—no breaks despite 30+ years of attacks, contrasted with hash functions being retired multiple times; the debate between lattice-based, hash-based, and elliptic curve advocates hinges on different risk tolerances and performance tradeoffs, not proof of vulnerability.
- **Crypto agility (supporting multiple algorithms, not betting everything on one) is the practical answer**—blockchains must build systems that can migrate to new cryptographic schemes as threats emerge, with "the boring 80% of the work" being the actual migration, not theoretical defenses.
- **Justin Drake's "bunker mode" proposal calls for storing assets behind hashed addresses** (hiding public keys) to mitigate immediate exposure if ECDSA breaks; Hasib Qureshi counter-proposed "cryptographic wartime" with ecosystem-wide governance, backup hash-based keys, and a validator kill-switch to force migration.
- **Project 11 launched StrongPoint, a crypto-agile custody platform supporting any post-quantum algorithm**, partnering with Zcash and Realm Insurance to explore pricing cryptographic risk and reducing insurance costs for proactively secured systems.

**The Cryptographic Foundations of Blockchain Security**

Connor explains that ECDSA (elliptic curve digital signature algorithm) is the cryptographic backbone of nearly all blockchains—it is the "one universal piece of tech that's in effectively every blockchain." When you send cryptocurrency, you are signing a message proving you control an address; ECDSA is the mathematical scheme that makes this work. Elliptic curves are used universally because they offer the "Goldilocks" balance: very performant, small, and run quickly on constrained devices. The security of ECDSA and all digital signature schemes rests on a "trapdoor function"—a mathematical function that is very hard to compute unless you know a specific secret (your private key). Cryptography has always assumed certain mathematical problems are hard to solve. The threat from both quantum computers and advanced AI is that these tools might discover hidden mathematical structure within schemes that humans have overlooked for decades.

**AI Attacks on Cryptographic Schemes**

Connor emphasizes that "as of today, there is no known attack" on elliptic curves, Bitcoin, or Ethereum—these schemes remain secure under current knowledge. However, recent evidence shows AI breaking non-production cryptographic schemes. Specifically, two schemes fell to AI attacks in the past two months: HAWK (lattice-based) and MACALIS (another structured scheme). Anthropic funded a $100,000 bounty that broke one of these schemes considered for future use but not yet deployed in production. AI's strength lies in finding patterns in mathematical structures. Elliptic curves, lattices, and other schemes with complex algebraic structure have "juicy mathematical structure, ripe for AI to attack" (in Ellie Ben Sassoon's phrasing). Hash-based cryptography, by contrast, minimizes algebraic structure—hash functions act "almost like random machines" that take data and output what appears to be random data. This fundamental difference means hash-based schemes are harder for AI to break because there is less mathematical structure for AI to exploit.

**The Quantum Threat and Timeline Estimates**

Q-Day (the day a practical quantum computer arrives) is a known threat. Shor's algorithm, a well-understood quantum algorithm, can exploit the mathematical structure in elliptic curves and other schemes. Google's recent result on "magic states" (crucial for quantum error correction) and Quantinuum's parallel result on error correction suggest quantum computing progress is real engineering, not merely theoretical. Alex gives his updated estimate: the earliest date a realistic quantum attack is possible is around 2030, with better-than-even odds in the early-to-mid 2030s (2033–2035). These results suggest the trajectory is toward earlier timelines, though substantial engineering work remains. By contrast, AI-driven cryptographic attacks are already happening. Connor and Alex frame this as "A-Day" (AI Day) occurring "maybe now, last month, sometime around now"—meaning the threat is immediate, not decades away.

**Hash-Based vs. Lattice-Based vs. Elliptic Curve Schemes**

A contentious debate emerged between cryptographers favoring hash-based schemes (Justin Drake, Vitalik Buterin proposing "bunker mode" and migration to hashes) and those favoring lattice-based post-quantum schemes (NIST's primary recommendation, A16Z, many lattice researchers). The tradeoff is real: hash-based signatures are larger, slower, and less performant than lattice-based ones. Lattice schemes are "the Goldilocks" of post-quantum cryptography—better performance than hashes while still offering post-quantum security. However, lattices carry algebraic structure that could theoretically fall to AI. NIST standardized both lattice (MLDSA, primary recommendation) and hash-based (SLHDSA) schemes, not because lattices are definitively safer, but because NIST balances post-quantum security, performance, and internet-scale deployment needs. Yehuda Lindell (Coinbase cryptographer) correctly pointed out that historically, hash functions have been broken more often than elliptic curves over 30+ years—no elliptic curve cryptosystem has ever been retired, while multiple hash functions have been retired. The strongest argument for hash-based schemes is that the sheer number of Bitcoin hashes computed (astronomically larger than ECDSA signatures) provides massive real-world testing; any pattern miners could exploit would be enormously valuable, yet none has been found. Alex notes that the answer is not choosing one scheme as "the perfect solution," but building systems with "crypto agility"—supporting multiple algorithms so that if one fails, others are ready.

**Encryption vs. Signatures: A Fundamental Asymmetry**

Matthew Green's warning about "potentially losing public key cryptography" specifically referred to encryption, not signatures. This distinction is crucial. For signatures, multiple solutions exist: lattice-based signatures, hash-based signatures (like SPHINCS), and others all work. For encryption (key exchange), the situation is dire. Techniques like ECIES (elliptic curve integrated encryption scheme) require establishing a shared secret between two parties without ever meeting. There is no known hash-based equivalent—you cannot build secure key exchange purely from hash functions. If the algebraic structures underlying all known key-exchange schemes break, there is no simple fallback. Fortunately, most blockchains do not rely on encryption; they primarily use signatures to prove ownership. Privacy-preserving protocols like Zcash do use encryption (for encrypted notes) and would suffer severe consequences if encryption broke. The broader internet infrastructure (TLS, secure communication, banks) also depends entirely on encryption. If elliptic curves broke, these systems could theoretically migrate to new post-quantum schemes. Blockchains cannot easily do this without retroactively exposing all encrypted activity (in Zcash's case) or abandoning privacy entirely.

**Community Reactions: Justin Drake, Yehuda Lindell, and Others**

Justin Drake's provocative "bunker mode" tweet sparked a debate. He proposed moving crypto assets to fresh addresses whose public keys remain hidden behind hashes—a defensive measure that does not directly solve the underlying cryptographic problem but reduces exposure if ECDSA breaks. The first part of the advice (storing behind hashed addresses) is sound; the second (implying ECDSA is in immediate danger) drew criticism. Yehuda Lindell countered that there is "no evidence whatsoever" of a break in elliptic curves and that "just throwing out AI is doing amazing math, so elliptic curve hardness is in danger, has no logical basis whatsoever." Alex frames this as a clash of perspectives: Justin, as a protocol designer, is thinking about Ethereum's 30–100 year longevity and the difficulty of decentralized upgrades (the ETH1-to-ETH2 transition took years). Yehuda, as Coinbase's cryptographer, is responsible for billions of dollars in assets *today* and cannot afford to discard the only thing known to work without concrete evidence of a replacement's safety. Both positions have merit. Dankrad Feist and Hasib Qureshi offered other perspectives. Hasib argued that blockchains are uniquely vulnerable compared to banks or TLS—if ECDSA breaks, there is no one to call and no second transaction authorization; the attacker simply takes the money. Blockchains could theoretically fork to a new chain, as Ethereum did with the DAO hack, but this is disruptive and not a viable recovery strategy if cryptography itself is broken.

**Proposed Solutions: Bunker Mode, Cryptographic Wartime, and Beyond**

Justin Drake's "bunker mode" is the simplest: store assets behind hashed addresses now, avoiding immediate exposure. Hasib Qureshi proposed a more systemic "cryptographic wartime" framework involving governance across protocols, wallets, exchanges, and apps. His plan includes: (1) every blockchain implementing a "cryptographic recovery mode" with a new hash-based signature scheme, (2) requiring all addresses to have a backup key using this scheme after a governance-set deadline, and (3) deploying a validator kill-switch allowing a coordinated vote to force all addresses into recovery mode without a protocol upgrade. Balaji Sreenivasan suggested that blockchains could implement on-chain consents to rewind the chain to a previous state if a cryptographic break is detected, allowing transaction reversals. He also proposed an on-chain AI with a token budget that immediately probes new AI models for vulnerabilities whenever a new model is released. Alex notes that the practical challenge is not generating creative solutions but achieving consensus across disparate ecosystems—getting everyone to "sing the same sheet of music" is much harder than proposing the idea. Connor, as a cryptographer, advocates for focusing energy on the "boring work" of actual migration rather than designing multiple creative fallbacks. Every chain will need some form of kill-switch and redundancy mechanism, but the core task is starting the migration itself.

**Crypto Agility and the Path Forward**

The common thread in all proposals is "crypto agility"—designing systems that assume schemes will break and building in the ability to migrate to new schemes without catastrophic loss. This is neither a new nor a surprising insight: cryptography has always evolved, and schemes have always been replaced as attacks improved or performance demands changed. The difference now is that both quantum computing and AI represent asymmetric threats: we know quantum computers would break schemes like ECDSA if they existed, but we do not know which AI approaches might break which schemes. Protocols should not assume any scheme is permanent. Instead, they should be designed to support multiple algorithms and allow seamless migration as evidence emerges. Connor notes that AI is not only breaking old schemes but creating new ones—the cryptography landscape will likely evolve constantly. If systems are designed properly, migration can become routine rather than catastrophic. The cost of crypto agility is real (supporting multiple schemes is more expensive than relying on one), but it is a form of insurance. Project 11's recent announcement of StrongPoint—a crypto-agile custody platform supporting any post-quantum algorithm—exemplifies this approach. StrongPoint partners with Zcash (as a design partner) and Realm Insurance to explore how proactive security measures (migration to post-quantum schemes, crypto agility) can reduce insurance costs by lowering quantifiable risk.

**Timelines and the Asymmetry of Information**

A critical dynamic underlies the debate: asymmetry of information. Justin Drake hinted that frontier AI labs might have already discovered breaks but are not disclosing them for security reasons. This is plausible—quantum computing companies are known to withhold results by agreement with governments. OpenAI published ~300 math problem solutions but had gaps in numbering (problems 44–47 missing), fueling speculation about undisclosed breakthroughs. Without public evidence, Yehuda is right to reject alarmism; with the possibility of hidden attacks, Justin is right to plan proactively. The consensus among both guests is that the work must begin now, regardless of exact timelines. Q-Day estimates of 2030–2035 are uncertain but credible; A-Day timelines are even more uncertain but undeniably closer. Waiting for definitive proof of a threat is not a sound strategy when the cost of preparation is bearable and the cost of being wrong is existential for a blockchain system.

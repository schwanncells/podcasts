# Time To Say Goodbye — "The Creativity Con with Thomas Frank"

**Host:** Jay Kristoff. **Guests:** Thomas Frank (political analyst, historian, founding editor of The Baffler magazine; author of *What's the Matter with Kansas*, *Listen Liberal*, and *The Creativity Con*).

## TL;DR

- **Creativity is a "lovable meritocracy"** — an ideology that justifies inequality without the ugliness of eugenics or IQ-based hierarchies; it claims everyone can develop creativity but defines creative people as members of the elite professional class.
- **Alex Osborne (BBDO) invented "brainstorming"** in the late 1940s–early 1950s as a business concept; the term and theory gained massive institutional support after Sputnik (1957), when American elites blamed low creativity for military lag.
- **Sputnik triggered a national mobilization** — Walter Lippmann, university presidents, the Rockefeller Brothers Foundation, and the entire political establishment agreed that America needed to become a "creative society"; this led to gifted-and-talented programs, SAT expansion, and the birth of "post-industrial economy" theory.
- **Creativity doctrine excludes working-class labor** — blue-collar manufacturing jobs are never described as creative or innovative, even though factory workers constantly solve problems and improve processes; de-industrialization was rationalized as shedding non-creative work.
- **Richard Florida's "creative class" theory** (2000s) used artists as shock troops for gentrification; cities from Detroit to DC to Portland lured creative professionals, raised rents, displaced ordinary residents, and called it urban revitalization.
- **Obama was the zenith of creativity ideology** — he ran as a populist in 2008 but governed as a pure neoliberal technocrat; he gave a speech in Osawatomie, Kansas mourning the death of blue-collar jobs but offered only one solution: go to college and get STEM degrees.
- **The "creative class" label applies to billionaires, not artists** — Steve Jobs, Elon Musk, and tech CEOs are celebrated as creative; actual writers, musicians, and journalists are dying out while creativity discourse ignores it entirely.
- **Populist opportunities now exist but Democrats won't take them** — oligarchy, political corruption, and anti-democratic gerrymandering are at historical peaks; the right has begun offering a (white nationalist) critique of innovation culture, while Democrats still push the same exhausted innovation messaging.

**The Origins of Creativity Theory**

Thomas Frank traces creativity as an ideology back to Alex Osborne, founder of the BBDO advertising agency, who popularized brainstorming and creativity theory in popular business books from the late 1940s onward. This doctrine became embedded in American institutions, but its true explosion came with Sputnik in October 1957, when the Soviet launch triggered an immediate, nationwide panic. Within days, Walter Lippmann and virtually every major American intellectual figure proclaimed that America's problem was lack of creativity. The Rockefeller Brothers Foundation released a study on education in 1958 centering on the need for more creativity in schools. This wasn't McCarthyism or blame for espionage—it was a collective pivot to the idea that nonconformity and individual creativity were essential to Cold War victory.

**Why Creativity Instead of IQ**

Frank explains that creativity solved a political problem for business elites. IQ-based theories of inequality—most infamously *The Bell Curve*—are viscerally repugnant to Americans, who believe in equal starting points and individual achievement. Creativity offers the same justification for hierarchy but with an optimistic gloss: it's unmeasurable, it's universal (everyone has some), and it's improvable. Yet when elites actually describe who is creative, they point to billionaires and tech CEOs, not artists or writers. This creates a sleight of hand: the ideology claims to be democratic and meritocratic, but in practice it's simply a modern form of "great man theory" that rationalizes the dominance of the professional-managerial class.

**Sputnik and the Cold War Education Push**

The Sputnik crisis led to a comprehensive mobilization. Universities, government, and business coalesced around building a creative intellectual infrastructure. The SAT expanded as a tool to find hidden talent in rural areas. Gifted-and-talented programs were created. The concept of America as a "post-industrial economy" or "knowledge economy" took root—the idea that manufacturing was obsolete and the future belonged to knowledge workers, innovators, and creative professionals. This myth persisted even as millions of actual jobs vanished.

**The Class Dimension: Who Is Excluded**

Frank's entire career has focused on social class, and it runs through every page of the creativity literature: blue-collar workers are systematically excluded from the definition of creativity. Manufacturing workers who solve problems on the shop floor, improve equipment, and innovate in practical ways are never called creative because they lack college credentials and white-collar status. This ideology was weaponized to justify de-industrialization; as factories closed, elites rationalized it by saying those jobs weren't essential because America had become a post-industrial, knowledge-based economy. The creativity doctrine provided cover for one of the biggest strategic disasters in American history.

**Richard Florida and the Gentrification Machine**

Richard Florida's "creative class" theory (early 2000s) weaponized the creativity ideal for urban gentrification. Cities from Detroit to DC used Florida's framework to argue that luring young, educated, creative professionals would spark economic revival. Artists were brought in as the vanguard; once neighborhoods became "creative districts," rents spiked and displacement followed. The Obama administration even formalized this with a "creative placemaking" program. Frank found only one instance of artists resisting—a German manifesto denouncing the scheme—while American artists largely accepted the narrative, unaware they were being used as gentrification shock troops.

**Obama: The Peak of Creativity Ideology**

Obama represented the apotheosis of creativity doctrine. He ran as a populist in 2008, and Frank believed it; the defeat of Clinton felt like the beginning of a new era. But Obama's actual presidency was pure neoliberal technocracy. In his Osawatomie, Kansas speech, he evoked the pain of dying blue-collar communities beautifully—and then offered one solution: everyone must go to college, get STEM degrees, and innovate. He used the word "innovation" in every State of the Union. He brought Google executives into the White House, tied Silicon Valley to DC policy, and spread innovation-speak throughout the executive branch. His defense secretary, Ashton Carter, used the word "innovation" 67 times in a 30-minute speech.

**The Mythology of Creative Work**

A central con in creativity discourse is that it promises non-alienated labor under capitalism. Frank's earlier book *The Conquest of Cool* explored how advertising and capitalism rebranded themselves as liberating and human-centered. But actual artists—writers, musicians, journalists—are dying out while the word "creative" is applied only to tech executives and billionaires. When someone works at Facebook deleting fake accounts, they're called a creative professional. When someone writes a novel, publishing is collapsing. The creative class concept applies to managers and tech workers, not artists, yet art is used as window dressing—a hood ornament on the car of capitalism.

**The Magazine Class and Educational Gatekeeping**

Frank expresses despair about the present state of intellectual culture. Magazine staffs are entirely drawn from Harvard, Princeton, Yale, and a few other elite schools; anyone from Wesleyan or another "lower-tier" school is treated as educational diversity. This class reproduction through educational pedigree is iron-clad. When Frank started The Baffler, he looked back to the Midwestern literary tradition—Sinclair Lewis, Hemingway, Theodore Dreiser, James T. Farrell, Richard Wright—writers who wrote about ordinary people without requiring Ivy League credentials. That tradition has disappeared. The entire intellectual apparatus is now calcified by elite school gatekeeping, making real dissent or outside voices nearly impossible.

**The Death of the Creative Class Myth**

Frank notes that the magic of creativity ideology has begun to wear off. When governors like Josh Shapiro in Pennsylvania tried to rebrand the state as an AI and innovation hub, public opposition killed the plan—nobody wants data centers, nobody wants to be obsoleted by AI. Tech workers are no longer seen as creative rebels. The ideology is losing currency, though it may be too late; the wealth has already been consolidated into the hands of a few billionaires meeting with Trump.

Yet creativity discourse hasn't died. It simply shifted: now the same elites casually announce that AI will obsolete millions of workers, and they'll give displaced workers UBI so they can "write poetry." The underlying con remains: some people are creative, most are not; creativity is the path to legitimate inequality; and everyone else should accept their obsolescence.

**Political Populism and the Missed Left Critique**

Frank closes by noting that genuine populist openings now exist. The original populists raged against oligarchy, political corruption, and lack of democracy—all of which are now at historical extremes. Bribery is nearly legal, gerrymandering is endemic, insider trading in Congress is rampant. Yet no major Democratic candidate is making this critique. The right—through figures like Josh Hawley and J.D. Vance—has begun articulating a (xenophobic, white nationalist) version of populist discontent, emphasizing community, dignity in manual labor, and skepticism of endless innovation. Frank expresses bewilderment that the Democratic Party hasn't seized on this opening, instead continuing to push the exhausted innovation agenda under different candidates.

He observes that his own criticism of Democrats in *Listen Liberal* ended his career as a mainstream pundit; his "pundit card got canceled." But he notes that the book was popular in Europe and Australia—places where the democratic elite could hear a critique without existential defensiveness. In America, you cannot critique the Democratic Party without exile. Yet the opportunities for a real left populism have never been greater, and the cost of missing them may be permanent.

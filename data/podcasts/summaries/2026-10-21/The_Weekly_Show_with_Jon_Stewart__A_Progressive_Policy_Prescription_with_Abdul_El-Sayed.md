# The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart — "A Progressive Policy Prescription with Abdul El-Sayed"

**Host:** Jon Stewart. **Guest:** Dr. Abdul El-Sayed (Democratic nominee for U.S. Senate in Michigan; physician and public health expert; former health director of Detroit and Wayne County; author of *The Sirens' Song: My Life with Boys*).

## TL;DR

- **$70 million spent against him in the Democratic primary alone** — a record for Michigan. After being up 20 points on July 4th, El-Sayed still captured 40% of the Black vote despite the onslaught, suggesting the narrative of his decline is "manufactured."
- **Three core issues resonate in Michigan town halls: care (healthcare, childcare, senior care), AI dread, and tariff pain** — voters report appendectomy bills covered only $395 by insurance, and the Canada tariffs are costing Michigan families $5,600 on average due to Michigan–Canada economic integration.
- **Medicare for All + money out of politics is his dual message** — El-Sayed emphasizes that the "affordability crisis" is not a crisis but the inevitable result of a rigged economy where billionaires can make a second billion more easily than working people can make their first $100,000.
- **He refuses to pander or triangulate** — He tells skeptical communities exactly where he disagrees (Israel policy, foreign military aid) and where he agrees, explicitly rejecting corporate and PAC money to maintain consistency.
- **His immigrant story is invoked against him, but he claims it as strength** — Parents fled Egypt for opportunity; his grandmother told him at 13 that the difference between his cousins and him was that he "got to go home to America." He views it not as a dual-loyalty liability but as a moral obligation to expand opportunity for others.
- **Tariffs, healthcare costs, and "paper patriotism" of Mike Rogers are the actual issues the race hinges on** — El-Sayed repeatedly pivots attacks on his name/faith back to his opponent's hypocrisy on 9/11 first responders and prescription drug prices.
- **Democratic fractures over Gaza support uncommitted votes, which he endorsed but walks back cautiously** — He endorsed "uncommitted" in the primary to send pressure to Biden on Gaza, endorsed Harris the next day (the first Arab Muslim leader to do so), and spent 106 days trying to convince Arab and Muslim leaders that Trump would be worse—a delicate balance he defends on principle rather than political expediency.

**The Campaign's Arc and El-Sayed's Decision to Run**

El-Sayed launched his campaign in April 2025, meaning he has been running for 18 months with two weeks left until election day. He previously ran for governor in 2018 and spent eight years in public health after that, eventually returning to electoral politics. He frames his entry into medicine and public service as rooted in his immigrant background: growing up with parents from Egypt, he witnessed his father's transformation from a vegetable-market-goer to an engineer in Detroit. An encounter with President Clinton at his University of Michigan graduation in 2007, where Clinton suggested he might one day run for office, planted the seed, though El-Sayed dismissed it as implausible given his name. Obama's 2004 DNC speech and 2008 victory changed his perspective—seeing someone who "looked like him" in politics felt clarifying. But even then, he pursued medical school and a PhD in public health, believing he could address systemic health problems that affect communities downstream of inequality.

**Why Medicine Alone Was Not Enough**

During his final year of medical school, El-Sayed realized that clinical practice treats symptoms while systemic issues—poverty, pollution, lack of access—keep people sick in the first place. He pivoted to public health and public service, serving as health director of Detroit and Wayne County. In those roles, he guaranteed free glasses for kids, took on corporate polluters, reduced prescription drug prices, eliminated up to $700 million in medical debt, and ensured lead safety in schools post-Flint. He wrote the book on Medicare for All. He describes his approach as treating society's "pathology" upstream rather than treating patients one at a time downstream. His campaign mantra—money out of politics, money in your pocket, Medicare for All—reflects this diagnosis: the system is rigged to extract wealth upward, and structural reform is the only cure.

**The Immigrant Obligation and Family Narrative**

El-Sayed's grandmother in Egypt was illiterate, bore eight children (two died in infancy), and worked in Alexandra. His father watched two siblings die before age five, which drove him to excel academically and eventually emigrate to America. At 13, during a visit to Egypt, his grandmother told him that while his cousins might be smarter or taller or nicer, "you have the best thing of all—you get to go home to America at the end of the summer." This framed his entire moral worldview: he has been given unearned access to opportunity that his cousins lack. He spent much of his childhood shuttled to Egypt, seeing directly the life he would have had without immigration. The "no A, no play" rule his father imposed—no sports if grades slipped—was not arbitrary hardship but the price of leveraging the opportunity his father's sacrifice had purchased. This debt animates his political work: he is trying to ensure that others born in America get the same opportunity he received by accident of birth.

**Three Issues Dominating Town Halls: Care, AI, and Tariffs**

When El-Sayed asks voters what keeps them up at night, three themes emerge consistently. First, care—healthcare, childcare, senior care. One woman reported having an emergency appendectomy covered only $395 by insurance, leaving her with a crushing bill. Others struggle to afford their parents' care while losing work hours to provide it. Second, AI dread: voters fear data centers invading their neighborhoods, their kids graduating into jobs that no longer exist despite $60,000 in student debt, and the abstract terror of terms like "Hugging Face" and "recursive self-improvement." Third, tariffs and the cost of living. The Canada tariff war with Michigan is particularly acute: Detroit is the only place in the country where you drive south to reach another country. Michigan–Canada trade is as integrated as crossing a state border. The tariff is costing Michigan families an average of $5,600. El-Sayed uses these concrete harms to frame the election: Trump's vanity and recklessness have directly translated into prices at the pump and grocery store.

**The Medicine-Politics Parallel and Diagnosis**

El-Sayed draws an explicit parallel between medicine and politics. In medicine, a patient describes pain, the doctor forms a differential diagnosis based on anatomy and pathology, runs tests, and applies the appropriate treatment. In politics, he "triages" society's ailments, listens to the pain people express, diagnoses the systemic illness behind it, and proposes the policy solution. The parallel breaks down, he notes with dark humor, because there are "Wario doctors" (MAGA) out there trying to worsen the illness. But his point is that most campaigns fail to listen deeply before proposing solutions. His 18-month engagement—holding intimate town halls early in small rooms, taking open questions without screening—was an extended diagnostic period. Now that he understands Michigan's core needs, he can point to policy prescriptions: Medicare for All addresses the healthcare crisis; tariff reversal addresses inflation; money out of politics addresses the rigged system enabling both.

**The Primary Drubbing and Narrative Manufacture**

In the primary, El-Sayed's opponents spent $70 million against him—a Michigan record—despite his leading by 20 points on July 4th. The ads weaponized a 15-year-old op-ed he wrote about Michelle Obama's Let's Move campaign, falsely implying he was disrespectful to women. El-Sayed notes that if advertising works (which it does), his ability to capture 40% of the Black vote despite the onslaught suggests his narrative of collapsing support is "manufactured." He argues that outlets covering the race are manufacturing a story of inevitable decline because it's more interesting than the truth: that voters are supporting him despite unprecedented spend against him. This connects to his larger point about media distortion—the press asks him to disavow Hassan Piker and the Muslim Brotherhood; the voters ask him about AI, healthcare, and his leadership style.

**Media Disconnect and the Power of Authenticity**

On his town hall tour covering rural, urban, and mixed communities, El-Sayed discovered a stark divide: voters ask substantive questions about policy, leadership, and rights; the press gaggle afterward poses gotcha questions designed for clicks. When CNN or MSNBC tries to pin him down on controversial associations, he simply pivots back to the core issue: his tax dollars are being spent on foreign military aid that should fund Michigan schools. Detroit public schools just received $700 million but still carry a $1.8 billion shortfall; meanwhile, Congress authorized $2.8 billion in 2,000-pound bombs for a foreign government. The 2,000-pound bomb "never gave a kid an education. It never fed somebody, never provided healthcare." This reframing is not evasion—it's answering the question most people care about, while the press hunts for clicks. El-Sayed believes this authenticity—refusing to pander, refusing to triangulate—is earning him support even from Trump voters and skeptical communities because he demonstrates he'll fight for the issues he prioritizes.

**The Gaza Question and Democratic Fracture**

The 2024 primary saw Michigan voters cast "uncommitted" ballots as a protest against Biden's handling of Gaza. The Palestinian American and Arab American communities in Michigan felt their tax dollars were subsidizing a genocide and wanted to send a message using tactics borrowed from the anti-apartheid South Africa movement. El-Sayed endorsed uncommitted for the same reason—to pressure his own party. When Biden dropped and Kamala Harris became the nominee, El-Sayed endorsed her the next day, becoming the first Arab Muslim leader to do so. He spent 106 days on calls with Arab and Muslim leaders, arguing that Trump's first-term Muslim ban demonstrated that he would be worse, and with the Harris campaign, trying to create daylight between Harris's position and Biden's Gaza policy. In the end, there was no budging. El-Sayed defends this balancing act on principle: he supported uncommitted on principle; he supported Harris on principle; he opposes unconditional foreign military aid on principle. The question is not whether every community agrees with him, but whether they trust that he'll fight for the principles he states.

**Addressing Skepticism in Jewish and Black Communities**

Pressed on whether black Michiganders and Jewish Michiganders have legitimate concerns about his candidacy beyond his name and faith, El-Sayed concedes the point but reframes it. He argues the choice is binary: stay home, support him, or support Mike Rogers (Trump's puppet). He cannot prove in advance what he will do over six years in the Senate, but he can show what he has done over six years in public health. He can also show what his opponent has done: Rogers voted against the Zadroga Act (healthcare for 9/11 first responders) while claiming to honor the heroes of 9/11. With Jewish Michiganders, El-Sayed was direct and specific: he disagreed with some of his audience on Israel policy but was honest about it. He committed to defending Jewish safety as sacrosanct—a commitment rooted in his opposition to attacks on any faith—and made clear that his critique is of unconditional foreign military aid to any country, not Israel specifically. The approach eschews the pandering he sees from other politicians who show up to Iftar dinners claiming solidarity while their actual policies harm Muslim communities.

**Why His Opponent Fears the Real Issues**

El-Sayed frames Mike Rogers as unwilling to defend his own record. Rogers voted four times against prescription drug reimportation yet aired ads (using footage of Debbie Stabenow) about high drug prices in Michigan versus Canada. His opponent does not want to debate healthcare, air quality, water safety, or tariffs because he lacks a record on any of them. Instead, Rogers and Trump resort to invoking El-Sayed's name and faith—the "dark man" fear Trump and J.D. Vance lean into. But El-Sayed refuses to shrink. He points out he was raised by his father and his stepmother, Jackie (daughter of the American Revolution, Jan and Judy Johnson's granddaughter). His younger brother's name is Osama—a coincidence of timing that turned tragic after 9/11, but also a reminder of how arbitrary such naming is. His brother is a direct descendant of Abigail Adams. "That's America," El-Sayed says. "And there are small-minded people who want to make America small like them. America has always been big, particularly in Michigan." Michigan is a microcosm—Detroit, Dearborn, Grand Rapids, tiny towns in Iron Mountain and Escanaba. Michiganders are gritty and earnest, unwilling to be exploited, bigger than what Trump and Rogers want to make them.

**The Electoral Math and Final Weeks**

With two weeks left, El-Sayed feels strong based on unexpected pockets of support and expected support from traditional Democratic areas. He does not trust polling (the primary shifted from a reported blowout for him to a close race at the end). He is "burning through" the final 14 days, fighting for every vote. He frames the race as a choice between a vision of a "big Michigan"—where people reach across divides and build shared opportunity—versus the small, fear-based vision offered by his opponents. Michiganders, he believes, deserve better than being exploited by a "billionaire from New York or a millionaire from Florida." The campaign has been true to its ideals, and he plans to keep fighting until the results are in.

**Post-Interview Reflection: Media, Democracy, and Reality**

After El-Sayed exits, Stewart and the producers reflect on the interview, noting the contrast between El-Sayed's authenticity and the clickbait media environment. They discuss how national media creates false narratives divorced from what voters actually care about, how that disconnection undermines trust in both media and government, and whether there will be any electoral price for Republicans' passivity in the face of Trump's recklessness. Stewart offers an anecdote from a diner in "middle America" where an older man said, "I don't like anything Trump's doing and none of them stand up to him, so I can't vote for the party anymore." This moment—described as if perfectly cued, like a dolphin appearing during a sunset—gives Stewart brief hope that some Republicans may defect. But the closing tone is darkly anxious: the national media is so disconnected from reality that no one can predict the outcome. After January 6th, the explicit dystopian goals of Trump and his allies should have been disqualifying, yet he may win again. Stewart and the team close in a state of existential dread, hoping listeners have questions that might pull them out of the "morass."

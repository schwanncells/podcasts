# Code Switch — "What Trump's language has in common with cult language"

**Hosts:** B.A. Parker and Gene Demby. **Guest:** Amanda Montell (author of *Cultish*; host of the podcast *Sounds Like a Cult*; self-described "linguistics groupie" who takes academic linguistics concepts and makes them accessible and urgent for general audiences).

## TL;DR

- **Trump's language is "loaded language"** — he attaches strong emotional charges to buzzwords like "fake news," "witch hunt," and "the deep state" to produce reflexive feelings of anger, fear, or righteousness in followers, a technique cult scholars have documented in leaders like Jim Jones.
- **"Thought-terminating clichés" are a key warning sign** — the term was coined by psychiatrist Robert J. Lifton in the early 1960s and describes catchy, repeatable stock phrases designed to shut down independent thinking; calling a bad headline "fake news" functions as a "semantic stop sign."
- **MAGA is derivative by design** — Montell notes that Hitler used a nearly identical phrase ("Make Germany great again") and that this type of slogan romanticizes a fictional golden age to weaponize fear of change and create a sense of persecution.
- **Trump's language operates as a branding strategy** — unlike typical politicians, Trump treats his rhetorical repertoire as a suite of "repeatable linguistic products," from "MAGA" to chantable imperatives like "Drain the Swamp," which Montell attributes to his background as a businessman and reality TV star.
- **Language does not just describe reality — it creates it** — Montell cites the linguistic theory of performativity: words like a justice of the peace pronouncing a couple married produce real material changes. When Trump takes on a quasi-religious "Christian nationalist prophet role" for followers, his loaded terms acquire religious significance and can lead people to take actions they otherwise would not.
- **Red flags for cult-like language include:** thought-terminating clichés, high exit costs (loss of identity, social network, or safety), and charged nicknames that cast outsiders as unilateral villains and insiders as a special, chosen group.
- **This is "the most cultish era of all time"** — Montell attributes it to the combination of late-stage capitalism, ideological polarization, social media, and a widespread loneliness epidemic.

**Trump's Loaded Language and the Cult Playbook**

Montell explains that Trump's rhetorical toolkit exemplifies what cult scholars call "loaded language" — attaching strong emotional charges to specific words or phrases, either existing ones or new neologisms, to produce reflexive feelings in a following. Terms like "fake news," "witch hunt," and "the deep state" function as reliable emotional triggers. Montell draws a parallel to Jim Jones, who used the phrase "paper idol" for the Bible and would stage theatrical moments ripping it apart to incite laughter and contempt from followers. Trump, she says, has a similar populism and theatrics, amplified by his background as a former reality TV star, which produces a "filterlessness" and "zealotry" that makes him riveting to watch.

She distinguishes Trump from ordinary politicians on the "cultish spectrum" in two key ways. First, he treats language explicitly as a branding strategy, deploying a suite of repeatable linguistic products. Second, he leans heavily on thought-terminating clichés — a term Robert J. Lifton coined in the early 1960s for zingy, memorable expressions aimed at shutting down independent thinking and alleviating the cognitive dissonance that might lead followers to question leadership. Calling a bad headline "fake news" is a textbook example: it functions as a "semantic stop sign" that ends inquiry rather than inviting it.

**MAGA as Derivative Slogan and Euphemistic Reframing**

Demby asks how "Make America Great Again" differs from slogans like Reagan's "Morning in America" or George W. Bush's "Compassionate Conservative." Montell argues MAGA is distinct because it is derivative — similar phrases have been used by populist and fascist politicians throughout history, including Hitler's use of "Make Germany great again." Slogans in this mold, she explains, do three things: they romanticize a past golden age that never existed, weaponize fear of change and progressivism, and construct a sense of persecution that demands a return to a time before that persecution. The Trump administration and its followers also understand, she argues, the power of euphemistic reframing — language that makes "deeply unpleasant or violent truths seem acceptable or even morally righteous" and encourages followers to commit actions they might otherwise refuse.

**Linguistic Performativity and Political Religion**

Demby asks about "linguistic performativity" — the theory that language actively creates reality rather than just describing it. Montell explains that English-speaking culture conditions people from childhood to discount language's power ("sticks and stones"), but linguists have established that speech produces real material change. Classic examples are an umpire calling someone out or a judge pronouncing a couple married: after those words, something is physically different in the world. When a political figure like Trump takes on a quasi-religious role — what Montell describes as a "Christian nationalist prophet" identity for some followers — his terms extend beyond the earthly world to followers' sense of purpose and afterlife, making loaded language like "witch hunt" or "crooked Hillary" carry genuine religious weight.

**Red Flags and the Cultish Era**

Montell offers practical red flags: thought-terminating clichés that meet any pushback with a zingy stock phrase; high exit costs, where leaving a group means losing your sense of identity, close friendships, or personal safety; and the use of charged nicknames that paint outsiders as villains and insiders as a special, chosen group. She declines to position herself as the definitive authority on what constitutes a capital-C cult, preferring instead to invite people to examine cultishness in their own lives — noting that she herself was humbled to discover she wasn't as skeptical as she thought.

On the broader cultural moment, she argues the combination of late-stage capitalism, ideological polarization, social media, and a loneliness epidemic has made this the most cultish era in history. Her practical advice: focus on noticing when you yourself are slipping into cultishness rather than trying to change others' minds, because behavioral economics research shows people are much better at changing their own thinking than influencing someone else's. The goal, she says, is to be part of communities you can participate in and exit with dignity — borrowing English scholar Gary Eberle's concept of "ritual time" — rather than groups that colonize your identity completely.

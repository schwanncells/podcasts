# Unchained — "Why Aave's Unified Pool Turned a Bridge Hack Into $193M in Bad Debt"

**Host:** Unchained host. **Guests:** Luke Leasure (head of research, Blockworks Research); Shaundra Devans (research analyst, Blockworks Research); Carlos Gonzalez-Campo (researcher, Blockworks Research).

## TL;DR

- **$193 million in bad debt from a single exploit** — an attacker exploited LayerZero's DVN bridge to mint 116,000 unbacked rETH on mainnet, deposited it into Aave as collateral, and borrowed stablecoins and ETH at 93% LTV, leaving Aave short nearly $200 million.
- **A one-of-one signature scheme enabled the hack** — KelpDAO used LayerZero's default single-signature configuration (recommended against by LayerZero itself), making it possible for one compromised key to authorize the fraudulent cross-chain transaction; about 40% of unique LayerZero OFT deployments still use the same risky setup.
- **Aave's unified pool model amplified the damage** — 98% of borrowed ETH was collateralized by liquid staking tokens involved in "looping" strategies, where traders borrow and reborrow the same asset for leverage; this concentration made the protocol vulnerable to cascading liquidations once utilization hit 100%.
- **$8 billion in assets fled Aave after the exploit** — markets froze at 100% utilization (zero available liquidity), stranding lenders and forcing leveraged traders into underwater positions as borrowing rates jumped from 1-2% to 10%, turning a 0.5–1% positive-carry trade into a -7% loss when leveraged 5–10x.
- **The umbrella module (Aave's backstop) is dangerously undercapitalized** — the insurance pool of roughly $250 million, accumulated from a 2–4% risk premium on deposits, covered historical bad debt under 5 million but is insufficient to cover the $193 million loss; it will likely be wiped out even in the best-case resolution scenario.
- **KelpDAO can choose who bears losses** — they can socialize the loss across all rETH holders globally (17% haircut but spreads risk) or isolate losses to the Layer 2 instance (catastrophic haircuts to L2 holders, triggering massive looping liquidations that could cascade to mainnet lenders).
- **Aave responded within 85 minutes but the loss was already crystallized** — the team froze the rETH market in time to prevent new exposure, but the attacker completed the deposit and loan origination within minutes, making the bad debt irreversible; Layer Zero's DVN network blocked a second exploit attempt.
- **This mirrors historical leverage crises like LTCM** — traders betting on stable spreads in leveraged positions faced margin calls as rates spiked, forcing rapid deleveraging with no liquidity to exit; the parallel extends to questions of who backstops systemic risk when markets freeze.

**The Exploit: LayerZero Bridge Vulnerability and KelpDAO's Risk Choices**

An attacker exploited a vulnerability in LayerZero's Delegated Validator Network (DVN) by signing a malicious cross-chain transaction claiming that 116,000 rETH had been burned on the Layer 2, entitling them to mint the same amount on Ethereum mainnet. The fraudulent transaction succeeded because KelpDAO used LayerZero's default single-signature configuration. While LayerZero recommends using a more sophisticated multisig setup, the one-of-one signing scheme is the out-of-the-box standard, and roughly 40% of unique LayerZero OFT deployments still operate with this single-signature structure. The vulnerability was exacerbated by the fact that Aave's systems were never breached—the protocol's oracles correctly reported the pre-exploit price of rETH, and the attacker simply deposited the unbacked tokens as collateral and borrowed against them within minutes. By the time Aave's security council could act, the bad debt was crystallized.

Luke Leasure notes that responsibility is distributed across multiple parties: KelpDAO, with over a billion in client deposits, could have defended itself with a multisig; LayerZero could have pushed back harder against single-signature deployments or secured their RPC infrastructure more robustly; and Aave could have flagged the one-of-one signer as a collateral risk when listing rETH. Shaundra Devans adds that circuit breakers or rate limiting on rapid borrows could have limited exposure, though none would have caught this in real time.

**Aave's Unified Pool Model and the Looping Problem**

Aave operates a monolithic, unified pool on mainnet where all approved assets are commingled, and any user with sufficient collateral can borrow any asset from the pool—a design choice that maximizes capital efficiency and liquidity but distributes risk across all participants without individual consent. In contrast, competitors like Morpho allow lenders to customize which collateral types are allowed to borrow their deposits.

The problem emerges from the structure of what traders actually use: while Aave V3 theoretically allows borrowing of ETH, there is no organic demand for ETH as a borrow asset—traders short ETH occasionally, but most borrow stablecoins. Instead, the borrowed ETH serves a specific leveraged strategy called "looping," where traders deposit liquid staking tokens (like stETH or rETH), borrow the passive wrapped version (wETH), convert it back to the liquid staking token, and repeat. This looping generates synthetic yield, turning a ~1% positive carry into a 5–10x leveraged return. Historically, these were among the highest-conviction trades on Aave, and as Devans explains, they account for 98% of collateral backing the borrowed wETH. The top looping wallet is EtherFi's vault product, which abstracts the strategy and presents it to users as a high-yield ETH product.

The unified pool model creates a perverse dynamic: by forcing all ETH lenders into a single pool where looping demand drives high utilization and interest rates, Aave was able to offer lenders a competitive 1.7% APY (compared to 0.01% on Bitcoin, where there is no looping demand). Morpho's modular approach—allowing lenders to opt out of looping collateral—resulted in lower liquidity, higher borrowing costs, and less interest in the looping trade. The efficiency of Aave's model enabled the strategy to flourish, but it also meant that ordinary deposit holders were unknowingly taking on concentration risk in a leveraged carry trade.

**Aave's Crisis Response and the Frozen Markets**

Aave's security council (a five-of-nine multisig) froze the rETH market 85 minutes after the exploit was detected, preventing new deposits from becoming collateral. However, the loan had already been originated—the attacker had completed the deposit and borrow in minutes, before the freeze. As Luke Leasure explains, the bad debt was crystallized before Aave could intervene. The freeze prevented additional exposure but did not recover the funds.

Following the freeze and the subsequent collapse of rETH's value, the wrapped ETH market spiked to 100% utilization—all available liquidity was exhausted, and neither lenders nor borrowers could transact. Ordinarily, high utilization triggers higher interest rates, attracting new lenders and incentivizing borrowers to repay. But uncertainty about the bad debt resolution created a binary dynamic: either you get your money out or you don't. Interest rates became irrelevant. Lenders faced an illiquid deposit with no defined maturity and no clear path to recovery. Aave governance lowered the ceiling rate from an initial ~10% to ~3%, further reducing incentive for new suppliers to enter the market, leaving utilization stuck at 100%.

**The Bad Debt Scenario and the Looping Cascade**

The $193 million loss must be borne somewhere, and KelpDAO governance effectively has two scenarios to choose from. In the first, they socialize the loss across all rETH holders globally, across all chains. This results in approximately a 17% markdown on rETH, concentrated among the largest holders but distributed widely. The umbrella module—Aave's insurance pool funded by a 2–4% risk premium on deposits—was sitting at roughly $250 million at the time of the exploit, which seemed adequate given historical bad debt of less than $5 million. However, close to $50 million of that sits in the ETH umbrella, which will likely be depleted entirely by the loss. Even after the umbrella exhausts, a 17% rETH haircut remains manageable for looping positions because they are diversified across many lenders.

In the second scenario, KelpDAO isolates losses to the Layer 2 instance, making all mainnet holders whole but imposing catastrophic haircuts to L2 holders. This triggers a cascade: looping positions on Aave become underwater. As Devans describes, what was a positive-carry trade at 0.5–1% yield became a -7% loss when rates spiked to 10% and stayed elevated due to 100% utilization. A trader holding a 5–10x leveraged position faces a margin call. They must unwind their positions rapidly, but there is no liquidity available to close them. Health factors crater, and liquidations flood the market. The result is that looping equity—the buffer between collateral value and borrowed amount—gets wiped out, and losses flow backward to lenders.

Luke Leasure notes that while the nominal size of the bad debt ($193 million) is less than the umbrella module, the actual losses to traders from forced liquidation and rate spikes will be "orders of magnitude bigger" if handled poorly. Spark, a competing lending protocol, avoided catastrophic exposure partly by removing rETH from its collateral set in January, but more importantly by implementing supply caps and borrow caps that rate-limit how quickly users can accumulate exposure. Even if rETH had remained on Spark, its losses would have been far smaller due to these circuit breakers.

**Broader Questions: Decentralization, Risk Management, and the Future of DeFi**

The incident raises uncomfortable questions about what decentralization means when centralized actors—like Aave's governance and security council—make unilateral decisions affecting all users. Arbitrum froze $30 million in stolen USDC; Circle chose not to freeze USDC stolen by the Lazarus group; and Aave's security council froze rETH markets without a vote. Luke Leasure suggests that certain functions, particularly crisis response and risk management, may need to be gatekept to a "select few of highly competent and responsible parties that are also easily identifiable and then in turn liable in the event of an exploit or mishap." This moves DeFi closer to a CeFi model with trusted intermediaries, raising the question of whether decentralization is binary or a spectrum.

Carlos Gonzalez-Campo points to vertically integrated platforms like Maple Finance, which manage risk end-to-end and maintain legal enforceability and clear accountability, as a model that may attract institutional capital seeking certainty. For retail users prioritizing permissionless access, he notes that "battle-tested" protocols like Maker—which has been live longer than almost any other DeFi protocol—may see increased flows.

Gonzalez-Campo also highlights that Aave itself was not exploited; the contagion resulted from risk management failures in the choice of collateral. As systems become more composable and fragmented, the attack surface expands. However, many of the attack vectors remain unchanged from five years ago: bridges, oracles, RPC infrastructure, and risk-taking wrappers. Defender and attacker capabilities alike will accelerate with AI, but the most competent teams will remain "security first" and may largely avoid these issues.

The unified pool model is not inherently flawed, Gonzalez-Campo argues, but it works best with a restricted, high-quality collateral set like BTC, ETH, USDC, and USDT. Spark's conservative approach—tight collateral restrictions, lower caps, and careful risk parameters—may represent the future direction for unified pools, while Morpho's modular approach offers users more control at the cost of lower liquidity and higher costs.

**Implications and Unresolved Questions**

The episode concludes with lingering uncertainty: Who will ultimately backstop the loss? Will an external party inject capital, or will some combination of rETH holders, Aave governance, and upstream protocols like LayerZero absorb the hit? The Drift hack, which occurred a week or two prior and was partially addressed by Tether's intervention, introduces questions about whether protocols can rely on white-knight rescues. Gonzalez-Campo does not expect Aave to issue a recovery token (as Bitfinex did in 2016), but external capital may yet arrive. The question of Lazarus group attribution is largely settled by consensus on-chain forensics, but the broader question remains: Is this an opening salvo in a new wave of attacks, or a sign that defenders have woken up to systemic vulnerabilities?

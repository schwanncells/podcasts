# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Who's afraid of private credit?"

**Host:** Waylon Wong. **Guests:** Vito Emanuel (Indicator intern, co-host); Natasha Sarin (economist and president of the Budget Lab at Yale).

## TL;DR

- **Private credit is a $3 trillion "black box"** — firms like Blackstone, Apollo, Blue Owl, and Aries pool money from investors and lend it to businesses, functioning like banks but largely exempt from post-2008 banking regulations.
- **Over 50% of riskier corporate loans once made by banks are now done by private credit firms** — Dodd-Frank made risky lending expensive for banks, pushing it into the unregulated shadow banking sector.
- **Investors are trying to get out** — some funds received redemption requests so large they were forced to cap payouts. BlackRock honored only 5% (its contractual limit) when investors sought 9% back; Aries and Apollo investors collectively asked for over $1 billion more than they'll receive this quarter.
- **The Trump administration proposed opening 401(k)s to private credit** — the rule was announced on the same Monday investors were racing to exit the asset class.
- **Four reasons for investor jitters:** bank-run contagion dynamics, heavy AI-sector exposure (including Blue Owl funding a Meta data center in Louisiana), systemic interconnections with insurers and investment banks, and near-total opacity about underlying loan portfolios.
- **Natasha Sarin is watching for systemic fraud and big blowups** — she hasn't seen clear signs yet, but says private credit's opacity means "all of that is something that only with time we're really going to appreciate."

---

**How Shadow Banking Was Born**

Natasha Sarin was a college economics student when Lehman Brothers collapsed in 2008. She describes her professor turning on CNBC, telling the class: "You don't know it yet, but this day and this moment is going to fundamentally change the way that the American economy works." Congress responded with Dodd-Frank in 2010, making risky lending more expensive and tightly regulated for traditional banks. Policymakers at the time already anticipated a side effect — that financial activity would migrate out of regulated banks into what's called shadow banking. That's exactly what happened. Private credit is a form of shadow banking: the firms aren't technically banks and most banking regulations don't apply to them, so loans can be made more easily and on more flexible terms.

**The $3 Trillion Machine**

Major private credit firms — Blackstone, Apollo, Blue Owl, and others — pool capital from high-net-worth individuals, insurance companies, pension funds, and retail investors. They then lend that money to other companies. Sarin notes that over 50% of loans that were traditionally originated by banks are now being done by private credit firms. The investor base has grown far beyond billionaires and hedge funds, drawing in insurance policyholders and retirees. Richard Cox, a retiree, invested $30,000 of his retirement savings into Blue Owl on the advice of his broker. A second broker, upon hearing this, went silent before letting out "an audible gasp."

**The Exit Queue**

Private credit funds are structurally illiquid. Funds typically allow investors to redeem only around 5% of the total fund per quarter, and investors agree to this constraint upfront as the tradeoff for higher returns. Blue Owl sent Richard a routine share redemption mailing last summer — investment-speak for an invitation to ask for money back. He submitted his paperwork. While waiting, he started hearing news about the sector becoming "more shaky." He eventually got his money back, though he lost a small amount compared to his original investment.

He wasn't alone. Redemption requests surged across the industry. BlackRock received requests for 9% of one of its private credit funds; it paid back only the contractual 5%. Investors at Aries and Apollo collectively asked for over a billion dollars more this quarter than the firms are obligated to return.

**Why Investors Are Spooked**

Sarin identifies four interconnected drivers of the current unease. First, classic bank-run dynamics: when people see others racing for the exit, they join the line. Second, heavy AI-sector exposure — firms like Blue Owl have made large loans to AI-related companies, including financing a Meta data center in Louisiana. With some big tech companies scaling back plans, investors are nervous about how much of the AI boom is underwritten by private credit. Third, systemic interconnections: major investment banks and insurance companies are deeply embedded in private credit. If loans go bad, insurance policyholders may find their life insurance or home insurance payouts at risk. Fourth, and perhaps most consequential, opacity. Private credit funds have far lower disclosure requirements than banks. What little is disclosed often leads through "a labyrinth of LLCs and holding companies." Richard Cox said he didn't know how much risk Blue Owl was taking on, which meant he didn't know how much risk he was taking on himself.

**Is This 2008?**

Sarin is watching for blowups but is measured. She notes that redemptions are a built-in correction mechanism and that what's happening could simply be a normal downswing in a credit cycle — "after a period of loose credit and low underwriting standards, there is a correction in the market." She hasn't yet seen evidence of the systemic fraud or cascading bad loans that preceded 2008. But she emphasizes the uncertainty is structural: "there isn't much transparency about what types of loans are being made." Top banking executives including Lloyd Blankfein of Goldman Sachs and Jamie Dimon of JPMorgan have been raising alarms. Meanwhile, the Trump administration moved to make private credit available inside 401(k) plans — a proposal that arrived just as investors were scrambling to get out.

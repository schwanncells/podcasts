# Code Switch — "Pete Hegseth's American Crusade"

**Host:** B.A. Parker. **Guests:** Matthew Taylor (religious scholar; analyst of Christian nationalist theology and its policy implications).

## TL;DR

- **Pete Hegseth holds monthly Christian worship services at the Pentagon** as Secretary of Defense, participating in Rededicate 250 (a White House-sponsored all-day prayer gathering on the National Mall) alongside Christian nationalist leaders including Robert Jeffress and Franklin Graham.

- **Hegseth has three Crusade-related tattoos: a Jerusalem cross (symbol of the medieval Crusader kingdom), "deus volt" (Latin for "God wills it," the rallying cry of the Crusades), and "kafir" (Arabic for non-believer/infidel, obtained in March 2025 after his nomination).** Matthew Taylor interprets the "kafir" tattoo as a signal that Hegseth sees himself as an enemy of Muslims and is deliberately framing conflict in religious terms.

- **In his 2020 book *American Crusade*, Hegseth frames U.S. policy as a "360 degree holy war for the righteous cause of human freedom"** against globalism, multiculturalism, Islamism, and elitism, using crusader rhetoric to argue that Western civilization depends on Christian dominance and that American nationalism is inseparable from Christian nationalism.

- **Hegseth told 60 Minutes that Iran can be defeated partly because "the providence of our almighty God is there protecting those troops,"** explicitly linking military strategy to religious doctrine and framing the ongoing Iran war as a conflict between Christians and Muslims.

- **Taylor warns the war with Iran represents a dangerous collision between two theocracies: Iran's Shiite Islamic theocracy and Hegseth's vision of the U.S. as a Christian theocracy** that must stand against Muslims as an existential civilizational threat, with no clear path to ceasefire or resolution.

- **Hegseth's crusader vision implies endless global conflict between the world's third of Christians and third of Muslims,** as both religions are present in virtually every nation; Taylor notes this creates "a formula for endless conflict around the world" that Hegseth seems to embrace as necessary militancy.

- **The religious scholar Robert Jeffress, featured at Pentagon events, has stated that religions like Islam, Judaism, Mormonism, and Catholicism lead people to "an eternity of separation from God in hell,"** while Franklin Graham preached that "God also is a God of war," framing violence as theologically sanctioned.

- **Matthew Taylor emphasizes that introducing religion into modern war constitutes "ideological jet fuel"** and finds it contradictory and alarming that Hegseth, leading a nation with constitutionally mandated separation of church and state, is deliberately escalating religious framing in his prosecution of the Iran war.

**Hegseth's Tattoos and Crusader Identity**

Pete Hegseth displays three tattoos directly connected to the Crusades. The most prominent is a Jerusalem cross on his chest—a symbol from the medieval Crusader kingdom of Jerusalem. Matthew Taylor notes that Hegseth often emphasizes this one because it is the most ambiguous, sometimes used as a liturgical symbol in Anglican churches; Hegseth is Baptist (now Calvinist), so he cannot claim ecclesiastical legitimacy for the symbol.

The second tattoo, "deus volt"—Latin for "God wills it"—carries far less ambiguity. According to legend, clerics at the Council of Clermont in 1095 shouted this phrase in response to Pope Urban II's sermon calling for holy war and the slaughter of Muslims. The phrase became the rallying cry of the Crusades, sanctifying violence as God's will. Taylor describes it as encoding the theology that violence is divinely ordained, not perpetrated on human behalf but commanded by God.

The third tattoo, "kafir" (Arabic for non-believer or infidel), first appeared publicly in March 2025 after Hegseth's nomination as Secretary of Defense. In conventional Islamic theology, the term does not apply to Christians and Jews, who are considered "people of the book" sharing belief in the one God. Taylor interprets Hegseth's use of the term as deliberate: "I am your enemy. I'm owning that. I want to be your enemy," a declaration that he rejects any possibility of Muslim-Christian commonality and welcomes religious conflict.

**American Crusade and the Fusion of Nationalism with Christian Theology**

In his 2020 book *American Crusade*, Hegseth argues that the nation faces an existential threat and must wage "a 360 degree holy war for the righteous cause of human freedom" using "American nationalism" as its weapon. Taylor explains that for Hegseth, there is no meaningful distinction between American nationalism, Christian nationalism, and Western civilization—these are layers describing the same entity. Hegseth identifies leftists, globalists, multiculturalists, and Islamists as enemies of this civilization and calls for a militantly Christian U.S. posture to defend it.

The book is driven by a sense of desperation: Western civilization has been encroached upon, the hour is late, and only by adopting crusader rhetoric and mentality can the West survive. Hegseth casts Donald Trump as the "lead American crusader" and frames victory not as a limited military objective but as civilizational defense through "militancy and violence" against forces he views as synchronized ideological threats.

**Religion as Military Policy and the Iran War**

Hegseth's invocation of religion in military strategy is explicit and troubling. In a 60 Minutes interview about the Iran war, he stated that American victory is assured because of superior capabilities and will—and "the providence of our almighty God is there protecting those troops." When pressed on whether he views the conflict in religious terms, he acknowledged that while he does not claim religious justification for U.S. actions, he encourages troops to "lean into their faith" and rely on God, asserting there are "no atheists in foxholes."

Taylor identifies a critical problem: Hegseth's worldview posits an inevitable, endless conflict between Christians (roughly a third of the global population) and Muslims (roughly a quarter and growing). Within decades, the Muslim population may exceed the Christian population. Hegseth offers no clear end state—only perpetual warfare framed as civilizational necessity. Because Christians and Muslims exist in virtually every nation, this vision implies global religious conflict everywhere simultaneously.

**The Theocracy Collision**

Taylor emphasizes that the Iran war now involves two competing theocracies: Iran's Shiite Islamic theocracy (itself a radical vision of Islam centered on a specific clerical class) and Hegseth's vision of a Christian American theocracy standing as "the bulwark of Western civilization" against Muslims. The fundamental problem is that neither side can envision coexistence or negotiated settlement. Without a path to ceasefire or dialogue, the conflict becomes perpetual.

Hegseth's framing contradicts the U.S. constitutional separation of church and state. Taylor observes that Hegseth is deliberately reintroducing religious language into warfare precisely to amplify the conflict, when the only productive path would be to minimize religious framing and emphasize secular policy disagreements. By making the war explicitly about Christian civilization versus Islamic civilization, Hegseth is, in Taylor's assessment, "introducing ideological jet fuel" into a conflict that may otherwise be negotiable. The paradox is acute: a nation founded on church-state separation is being led by a Secretary of Defense who is weaponizing religious identity in service of military strategy.

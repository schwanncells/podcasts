# Planet Money — "Why is there a supplement craze if they don't even work?"

**Host:** Sarah Gonzalez. **Guests:** Jane Black (food politics reporter, Washington D.C.; covers wellness industry expansion).

## TL;DR

- **$70 billion U.S. supplement industry, growing fast** — 75% of Americans take supplements despite no evidence that supplements make healthy people healthier; sales expected to double over the next seven years.
- **Making a supplement is laughably easy** — Planet Money priced a custom branded supplement at $4.50–$7 per unit (minimum order 8,333 bottles for ~$33,000), or $5,500 for a pre-made stock formula with white-label branding.
- **FDA has almost zero authority over supplements** — The 1994 Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) actually gave makers *more* freedom, including legal rights to make health claims as long as they avoid specific keywords ("diagnose," "cure," "treat").
- **The "supports metabolism" loophole** — Supplement makers cannot claim a pill "burns fat" but can claim it "supports metabolism" despite both meaning the same thing; companies routinely use vague language that avoids FDA-regulated terms without triggering scrutiny.
- **Prevagen (synthetic jellyfish protein) $165M sales with zero proof** — The supplement was rejected by FDA twice as unsafe, but the company bypassed review by first adding it to a food product (NeuroShake), self-certifying it as safe, then using that food status to sell the supplement; the FTC won a false-advertising case in 2025, eight years after filing.
- **What you read on the label may not be what's in the bottle** — Consumer Labs testing found some turmeric supplements contained zero turmeric, others contained far more than advertised; two-thirds of elderberry supplements on Amazon were not authentic elderberry; supplement makers are not required to prove ingredient accuracy unless sued.
- **Public backlash killed every serious regulation attempt** — In 1966, Congress received 2 million letters (more than during Watergate) opposing supplement disclaimers; in the 1990s, the supplement industry staged a media campaign with Mel Gibson ads depicting SWAT raids if Congress regulated vitamins, and health food stores put black curtains over the supplement aisle.
- **Placebo effect is real, and maybe that's okay** — Supplements likely don't work due to their active ingredients, but they reliably produce placebo effects that make people feel better; experts who advocate for regulation still take some supplements themselves.

**The Supplement Industry Boom and Lax Regulations**

Sarah Gonzalez and Jane Black open the episode by noting that Americans are in the midst of a supplement craze. The U.S. supplement industry is worth $70 billion and growing. Seventy-five percent of Americans take supplements, choosing from roughly 100,000 different products—protein powders, pre-workouts, probiotics, fat burners, joint health, gut health, and skin products. Supplement sales have increased around 50 percent since before the COVID-19 pandemic, driven partly by wellness influencers on TikTok and Instagram and partly by broader distrust in institutions and pharmaceutical companies.

The hosts demonstrate just how easy it is to create a supplement by interviewing Frank Cantone, the CEO of SMP Nutri, a supplement manufacturer. Frank explains that he and his company can create custom supplements from scratch or use one of more than 800 pre-made stock formulas ready to be white-labeled. For a custom Planet Money branded supplement targeting focus and hair health—a gummy combining creatine, lion's mane, collagen, and green tea—the smallest possible order would be 8,333 bottles at a cost between $4.50 and $7 per unit. Half the cost ($33,000) is due up front. Alternatively, they could order a pre-made stock formula for just $5,500 and slap on their own label, even if that exact same gummy is being sold under different brands simultaneously.

**The Historical Roots of Supplement Regulation Failure**

Black walks through the history of supplement regulation in America, revealing a pattern of lax oversight dating back over a century. In the 1910s, supplements claimed to cure malnutrition using strychnine (rat poison). In the 1920s and 30s, companies tried to sell yeast claiming it would solve issues including "furry tongue." Around the same time, radioactive water was marketed as a cure for fatigue until a New York tycoon's jaw disintegrated.

By the 1950s, doctors were calling all of this "medical quackery," but the FDA struggled to regulate it. When the FDA was created in 1906, supplements were not mentioned. The agency attempted regulation multiple times. In 1966, the FDA proposed a disclaimer on supplement bottles stating that vitamins and minerals can be obtained from food and that there is no scientific basis for routine supplement use. Congress received more than 2 million letters opposing this—more letters than Congress received during Watergate—and the proposal died.

**DSHEA: A Regulatory Breakthrough for the Supplement Industry**

In 1994, Congress passed the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA), which Melanie Benish, a lawyer at the Environmental Working Group, describes as so lax that experts don't consider supplements to be regulated at all. Rather than restricting the industry, DSHEA actually gave supplement makers *more* freedom, including the legal right to make health claims on labels. The only constraint: they must avoid specific keywords. A supplement cannot claim to diagnose, prevent, cure, treat, or mitigate a disease like Alzheimer's. But it can claim to "improve memory"—a distinction that, on closer inspection, is nearly invisible. A claim to "support metabolism" is functionally identical to "burn fat" but skirts FDA oversight.

Melanie points out that if a supplement bottle says "supports X" or "promotes Y," that is a signal that the claim has not been proven. The fine print on bottles reads: "These statements have not been evaluated by the FDA." While supplement makers are theoretically supposed to back up their claims with studies, many of those studies are funded by the supplement industry itself, creating conflicts of interest. Critically, there is no regulation requiring supplement makers to *prove* their product does what it claims.

**The Prevagen Case: How the GRAS Loophole Works**

The most egregious example Melanie cites involves Prevagen, a supplement made from a synthetic version of green fluorescent protein found in jellyfish. The story began in the 1990s when a Wisconsin man named Mark Underwood, inspired by his mother's interest in jellyfish's ease of movement, decided to create a memory-boosting supplement using synthetic jellyfish protein. The company first went to the FDA, which rejected the ingredient twice as insufficiently safe—a low bar that the company still failed to clear.

But there is a legal workaround. If a supplement ingredient has never been used in food before, companies can declare it "generally recognized as safe" (GRAS) and put it in a food product first. Once it is part of the food supply, it can then be added to supplements. Supplement trade lawyers reportedly advise clients to exploit this loophole. That is exactly what the jellyfish company did. They created NeuroShake, a food product, and formally notified the FDA they were introducing the jellyfish protein as a food ingredient. The FDA questioned the safety again, but once it was a food ingredient, the company could self-certify safety and bypass FDA review. The supplement pill was sold the entire time, even before the food product was created. Between 2007 and 2015, Prevagen racked up more than $165 million in sales.

People reported chest pain, seizures, and strokes while taking Prevagen, but the FDA lacks the authority to pull a supplement off the market unless it proves an imminent hazard—a process that takes years and millions of dollars. The Federal Trade Commission brought a false-advertising case in 2017. After nearly eight years, the FTC won in 2025, proving there was only one clinical trial and it did not show Prevagen improved memory. Today the label simply says "Prevagen for your brain"—still vague. The product remains in the top five of Amazon's vitamin and mineral supplements.

**What Is Actually in Your Supplement?**

Marion Nestle, a legendary food and nutrition scientist with 17 books on food politics and a background in molecular biology, raises a fundamental problem beyond unproven claims: the lack of oversight over what actually goes into supplement bottles. She notes she does not take vitamin supplements because she does not know what is in those packages. What the label says is not necessarily what is in the bottle.

Technically supplements are supposed to contain what they claim, but nobody has resources to verify this except through lawsuits. The FDA sometimes spot-checks food ingredients to ensure accuracy, but supplements have no such oversight. Consumer Labs, a private supplement testing company, has tested supplements and found shocking variations. A batch of turmeric pills contained one with essentially no turmeric and others with far more than advertised. The same lab has found wide variations in echinacea and elderberry supplements. More than two-thirds of elderberry supplements sold on Amazon did not contain authentic elderberry. According to a 2017 study, 20 percent of liver toxicity cases were tied to herbal and dietary supplements. Over the last 30 years, supplement-related liver failure increased eightfold, so much so that people began being put on wait lists for liver transplants.

**The Value of Third-Party Testing and the Placebo Effect**

Marion notes that some supplements on shelves are more trustworthy than others—specifically, those marked with NSF or USP certification, which indicates third-party testing confirming the ingredients listed are accurate and at non-harmful levels. Frank's supplements carry NSF certification. She emphasizes that most supplements, whether certified or not, likely are not causing real harm, even if they likely are not providing benefit. Some are harmful, which is a problem, but most are not particularly harmful.

Remarkably, despite advocating for regulation, Nestle herself does not take supplements and expresses a relaxed attitude about the whole affair. She says there is no evidence that supplements make healthy people healthier, but if people believe they work and feel better as a result, she is not going to argue with them. It is like religion—you do not argue with people about religion.

This gets at the genuine value supplements do provide: the placebo effect. Nestle explains that there is plenty of evidence that supplements are "fabulous placebos." If a person takes a vitamin C gummy believing it prevents colds and then does not catch a cold, the placebo effect is real. Life is hard these days, and if a supplement pill makes people feel better, it may not be worth arguing the point. Both hosts acknowledge they still take some supplements despite the lack of evidence, driven partly by the psychological benefit and partly by habit.

**The Century-Long Pattern of Consumer Resistance**

The episode closes with a reflection on American history. Nothing has stopped Americans from wanting supplements for the past century. Even if supplement makers were fully transparent about the uncertainties and risks, it likely would not change consumer behavior. Congress, regulatory agencies, and the supplement industry have battled for decades, but consumer demand and consumer backlash have consistently won out. The supplement craze is here to stay.

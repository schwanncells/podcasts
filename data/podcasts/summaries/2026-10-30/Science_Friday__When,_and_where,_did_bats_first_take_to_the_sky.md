# Science Friday — "When, and where, did bats first take to the sky?"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guest:** Dr. Emma Teeling (professor of zoology, University College Dublin; co-founder and lead researcher, BAT1K Global Bat Genome Sequencing Consortium; studying bat evolution, aging, and immunity for over 25 years).

## TL;DR

- **Bats originated in Europe 60–65 million years ago**, coinciding with the dinosaur extinction event when a meteorite impact potentially caused darkness for over two years.
- **BAT1K sequenced 103 bat genomes to reference quality and integrated 65 fossil bat specimens**, combining molecular and anatomical data to reconstruct the first complete evolutionary tree showing how bats descended from insectivores within the Arasatheria mammal group.
- **Fossil bats from 50–55 million years ago already possessed echolocation**, as evidenced by highly coiled cochleae in their inner ears and specialized jaw anatomy—indicating echolocation evolved very early in bat history, not gradually.
- **Bat genomes are highly mosaic due to ancient hybridization**, meaning different regions of the genome tell conflicting evolutionary stories; this pattern had made it extremely difficult to reconstruct bat evolutionary history before recent advances in computational tools.
- **~1,500 bat species exist today, representing one in five of all living mammals**, with 21 different family groups found globally, from blood-feeding vampires in South America to nectar-feeding and insect-eating specialists.
- **Bats show remarkable adaptations in immunity, tumor suppression, and vision-olfaction tradeoffs** that researchers are now decoding through comparative genomics—discoveries that required overturning some of Teeling's own earlier published work when new data contradicted prior conclusions.

**The Timing and Context of Bat Origins**

Dr. Emma Teeling and the BAT1K Consortium found that the ancestral bat—the modern form of bat we recognize today—originated approximately 65 million years ago. This timing coincides precisely with the extinction of dinosaurs when a meteorite impact struck Earth. Teeling notes that the impact potentially caused darkness for over two years, and bats appear to have responded to this ecological upheaval. The ancestral bat did not emerge fully formed; instead, bats were evolving for about 20 million years before this point from other mammalian ancestors, making the full evolutionary transition roughly 80 million years long.

The discovery of bats' closest living relatives overturned earlier assumptions. Rather than being closely related to mice, bats belong to a group called Arasatheria that includes shrews, carnivores (lions, tigers, bears), horses, whales, and deer. The earliest branch within this group, originating roughly 80 million years ago, consisted of true shrews and insectivores—the closest living relatives of bats.

**Integrating Genomic and Fossil Data**

Teeling's team took an innovative approach by combining 103 bat genomes sequenced to reference quality with anatomical data from 65 fossil bat specimens. Previously, evolutionary trees built from DNA alone could not meaningfully incorporate fossil data because ancient fossils yield no extractable DNA. The team, led by Liliana Davalos, developed new computational tools to integrate genetic information with anatomical characteristics—bone shapes, tooth sizes, and diagnostic morphological features preserved in fossils—into a single evolutionary framework.

The fossil record of bats is unusual in its richness. Specimens from 50 to 55 million years ago are so well-preserved that researchers can observe not only wing structure (indicated by very long fingers) but also the fine details of inner ear anatomy. These Lagerstätten fossils reveal that early fossil bats possessed highly coiled cochleae and specialized jaw characteristics indicating they possessed both flight and echolocation—meaning echolocation is an ancient trait, not a gradual later acquisition.

**Genome Mosaicism and Evolutionary Surprises**

A major finding was the discovery that bat genomes are highly mosaic in structure. Different regions of the genome appeared to tell different evolutionary stories because bats have a remarkable capacity for hybridization across species boundaries. This mosaicism had previously made it extremely difficult to reconstruct a clear evolutionary tree. Teeling notes that the research forced her to revise and even contradict conclusions she had published since 1997, including findings about which echolocating and non-echolocating bats grouped together evolutionarily. She states that the new genomic data "rewrote the evolutionary history" and that it was fortunate the corrections came from her own team rather than from outside critics.

**Unresolved Questions and Research Directions**

The BAT1K study opened far more questions than it answered. The location of proto-bats—the transitional forms before true bats—remains unclear, and paleontologists continue searching for potential proto-bat candidates to refine understanding of the transition. Teeling's team is pursuing multiple research directions: studying aging in long-lived bat populations over 15–20 years; investigating whether some bat species show evidence of genomic decline; exploring whether the evolution of echolocation involved a loss of vision or olfaction; and comparing genomes of nectar-feeding bats against insect-feeding bats to understand how diet-driven metabolic shifts altered the genome.

A particularly important area is understanding bat immunity. Bats can live with viruses that would sicken other mammals and can suppress tumor formation with remarkable efficiency—abilities Teeling's team is decoding at the genomic level.

**The Global Nature of Bat Science**

Teeling emphasizes that the diversity of bats—with species found on every continent and feeding on everything from blood to nectar to insects—requires truly global research collaboration. Vampire bats are found only in South Americas, and understanding their genomic adaptations requires partnerships with researchers in those regions. She notes that the constellation of natural experiments already conducted by evolution—comparing long-lived versus short-lived species, different feeding strategies within single families, and global dispersal patterns—means that sequencing bat genomes is equivalent to having "evolution's notebook" of 15 to 55 million years of adaptive change directly available for study. However, continued progress will require more researchers, more funding, and continued international collaboration.

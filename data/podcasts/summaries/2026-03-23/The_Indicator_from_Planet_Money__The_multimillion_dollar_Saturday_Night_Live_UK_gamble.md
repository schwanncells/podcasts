# The Indicator from Planet Money — "The multimillion dollar Saturday Night Live UK gamble"

**Host:** Waylon Wong. **Guests:** Tala Vistrom (British journalist covering media and culture); Erica Horton (British television and comedy scholar, PhD); Evan Shapiro (media analyst and former executive vice president at NBCUniversal).

## TL;DR

- **SNL made its British debut on Sky One** — the first episode was hosted by Tina Fey, with SNL creator Lorne Michaels and Seth Meyers attending table reads in London to oversee the adaptation.
- **Corporate synergy drives the project** — Comcast owns NBC (which airs the US SNL), Universal (producing the UK version), and Sky (airing it), making the expansion a natural extension of a 2018 acquisition.
- **US SNL viewership has dropped from 13 million in the late 1970s to 4–5 million today** — but it is the biggest show on YouTube in the US by unique views, signaling where the real audience lives.
- **Each episode costs up to $4 million** — driven by elaborate live sets built for 5-minute sketches then dismantled, large writing staffs, and multiple camera crews. A typical British panel show like *Have I Got News for You* costs well under $500,000 per episode.
- **Sky has only ordered six episodes** — media analyst Evan Shapiro warns that the short run creates a "ticking time bomb" that makes it hard to build a show's identity; Seinfeld and Family Guy both took time to find their footing.
- **The British–American comedy gap is real** — Horton describes American comedy as "glamorous and sexy, saying 'look at me,'" while British comedy is "much more like, oh God, are you looking at me? I'm so sorry." Past transatlantic adaptations have mostly failed.
- **Digital distribution may be the lifeline** — Sky is airing the show on both Sky One and its streaming service Now; the dual broadcast/streaming model reduces dependence on advertising and gives sketches a second life via YouTube and TikTok clips.

**Why SNL UK, Why Now**

Wong explains that Saturday Night Live's UK launch is the direct result of Comcast's 2018 acquisition of UK satellite network Sky. Because Comcast also owns NBC and Universal, the company could greenlight a British version with full vertical integration — one parent company producing, broadcasting, and streaming the show. The US SNL's declining linear viewership (down from 13 million in the late 1970s to 4–5 million today) gives the franchise a financial incentive to expand its brand into new markets rather than rely on its domestic television numbers.

**The Transatlantic Comedy Problem**

Horton argues that American and British comedy operate from fundamentally different cultural assumptions. American comedy emphasizes glamour and performance — "look at me" — while British comedy defaults to self-deprecation and discomfort. That gap has sunk most cross-Atlantic adaptations: she cites a failed British remake of Fawlty Towers called *Amanda's* as an example. The more optimistic counterpoint is *Have I Got News for You*, a British quiz-panel format that launched a US version on CNN in 2024 hosted by Daily Show alumnus Roy Wood Jr., now in its fourth season. The show's lesson, Horton suggests, is to treat each version as its own entity rather than a carbon copy — the same approach that made both the UK and US versions of *The Office* massive but distinctly different hits.

**The Cost Problem**

Shapiro describes the economic reality of live sketch television: multiple stages, elaborate sets built only to be torn down after a five-minute sketch, large writing teams generating what amounts to twelve short plays per week, and extensive camera crews. Vulture reported a single US SNL episode costs $4 million. A typical British panel show runs well under half a million dollars per episode. Sky declined to share its per-episode budget, but the show is being co-financed by Lorne Michaels' production company Broadway Video. Shapiro notes Sky has a comparatively healthy budget for a UK broadcaster, but the numbers are still a significant leap from what British television typically spends.

**Six Episodes and the Patience Problem**

Shapiro's sharpest criticism is the six-episode order. He frames it as telling everyone involved — writers, cast, showrunners — that they have a month and a half to prove it works before the plug gets pulled. Pressure at that scale, he argues, is actively hostile to comedy. He points to Seinfeld as a show that was not initially successful and Family Guy as one that was canceled before it became a hit. The problem isn't just creative pressure: six episodes gives the audience almost no time to discover and develop a relationship with a new cast. Horton counters that social media and clipping culture can shorten that discovery timeline — a sketch that resonates can circulate on TikTok for weeks after airing.

**Streaming and the Digital Fallback**

Wong and Shapiro note that Sky is threading a dual distribution strategy: linear broadcast on Sky One alongside streaming on Now, the platform that also carries prestige American imports like *The White Lotus* and *Succession*. The subscription model provides baseline revenue regardless of episode-by-episode ratings. Shapiro observes that the goal for streaming services has shifted from chasing new signups to retaining existing subscribers, which makes unscripted and sketch content more attractive. He also challenges the assumption that digital-first content skews young: one of YouTube's fastest-growing audience segments is users over 55. Horton adds that for younger viewers, TikTok clips are the primary discovery mechanism — not broadcast schedules.

# Planet Money — "BOOKstore Economics"

**Host:** Alexi Horowitz-Ghazi. **Guests:** Fischer Nash (book buyer, Carmichael's independent bookstore, Louisville Kentucky); Steven Pace (director of trade sales, W.W. Norton & Company; former bookstore owner and publisher sales representative).

## TL;DR

- **Book buyers evaluate 12,000-15,000 titles per season and stock ~3,000** — Fischer Nash, the "final gatekeeper," spends ~30 seconds on each title using Adial Vice, a platform that displays publisher catalogs with metadata including author following, print run size, and comparable sales data.
- **95% of books are returnable, shifting risk from bookstores to publishers** — this business model originated during the Great Depression as a way to keep bookstores solvent and has persisted because no major publisher has dared eliminate it since.
- **Comparable titles and author track records are the biggest predictors of success** — Fischer ordered one copy of Eve McDonald's *Carthage* after seeing that her previous book *Hannibal* sold three copies, a "pretty decent amount" for history in a small store.
- **Display space is the difference between visibility and obscurity** — ordering four copies qualifies a book for the center display table; ordering one means shelf placement in a back corner; Category (BISAC code) determines initial shelf location.
- **Publishers destroy unsold inventory via industrial pulping rather than remaindering** — Steven Pace witnessed a giant conveyor belt shredding machine running 24/7 in the 1990s, capable of reducing a $29.99 book to 10 cents of pulp in 15 seconds, a practice publishers use to protect brand value during format transitions.
- **Print run determines a book's financial ceiling** — Steven, as head of trade sales, must estimate demand through orders from book buyers nationwide and set the first print run high enough to never stock out but low enough to avoid warehouse overflow; too few books mean losing months of sales momentum if a reprint is needed.
- **Planet Money book distributed to airports, cruise ships, libraries, military bases, Europe, and Asia** — Steven mobilized the entire Norton sales force to place copies in "every retail outlet you can imagine," including foreign translations in Korean and Chinese scheduled for June and September 2026.

**The Book Buyer's 30-Second Decision Tree**

Fischer Nash, the book buyer at Carmichael's independent bookstore in Louisville, Kentucky, is tasked with choosing which books to stock from a seasonal avalanche of 12,000 to 15,000 titles. Each season, major publishers' catalogs collectively contain up to 2,000 titles per publisher, and Fischer aims to order about 20-25% of available titles (roughly 3,000 books) within the bookstore's limited 1,800 square feet of space. Fischer works through the Adial Vice platform, which consolidates all major publishers' catalogs and metadata in one place. The evaluation process, which sounds complex when explained, actually unfolds in roughly 30 seconds per title.

Fischer considers several data points during this brief window. First are the basics: hardcover or paperback, list price, cover image, and summary. Then comes the author assessment—is the author a household name like a celebrity or former White House insider? Does the author have significant social media following (millions of followers on Twitter matter; anything under a million is less influential)? Is the author local? A University of Louisville professor would trigger a larger order from Fischer. Publicity and media appearances factor heavily: will there be a book tour, television appearances, or podcast placements? Physical attributes matter too—books over 500 pages signal risk (fewer people commit to long reads), while the "sweet spot" is 250-400 pages.

**Comparable Titles and Track Records as Decision Signals**

The most important section of each catalog entry is the comparable titles and sales tracking data. This shows Fischer how many copies the bookstore has previously sold of similar titles or previous works by the same author. For historian Eve McDonald's new book *Carthage*, Fischer could see that the store had sold three copies of her prior book *Hannibal*—not a large number in absolute terms, but respectable for a history book in a small bookstore. That track record convinced Fischer to order the book. By contrast, if an author had sold zero copies previously, the risk calculus would shift significantly. This track record system gives Fischer a concrete prediction: if a reader base existed before, it likely exists now.

**The Display Table Hierarchy and BISAC Codes**

Ordering decisions cascade into placement decisions. A bookstore operates on a strict inventory hierarchy. At the lowest level sits shelf placement in the back of the store. Moving up, ordering two copies makes the book slightly more visible through the visual psychology of multiple spine-out copies. The major jump occurs at four copies—the minimum needed to qualify for the display table at the center of the store, the "holy grail" of bookstore real estate. Publishers assign each book a BISAC (Book Industry Standards and Communications) code that suggests a category; the *Carthage* book received a business/economics code. Fischer initially categorized the Planet Money book the same way but then reconsidered: the business section at Carmichael's is tucked in a back corner, hidden from entering customers. After previously making this mistake with Andrew Ross Sorkin's *1929* (which sold out because of high demand Fischer hadn't anticipated), Fischer reclassified the Planet Money book as general new nonfiction, placing it on a prominent shelf near the entrance and on the center display table.

**Returnability as Risk Protection**

Ninety-five percent of books sold to bookstores are returnable, meaning Fischer can send unsold inventory back to the publisher and receive a refund minus shipping costs. This unusual arrangement originated during the Great Depression as publishers' way of preventing bookstore failures; once established, no major publisher has dared eliminate the return privilege since. The model is unique to publishing—supermarkets cannot return unsold orange juice to Tropicana, bearing the risk themselves. Bookstores negotiate around 50-60% of list price (so roughly $20 per $40 hardcover), and returnability acts as an insurance policy. However, the insurance comes with a penalty: bookstores must absorb freight costs to return books and often wait months to process returns, during which unsold inventory consumes valuable shelf space and capital.

**The Publisher's Print Run Calculation**

Steven Pace, W.W. Norton's director of trade sales, faces a larger-scale version of Fischer's problem: determining the first print run. Steven must estimate nationwide demand by aggregating orders from book buyers like Fischer across thousands of independent and chain bookstores. His sales force continuously feeds order estimates back to central command as they pitch the Planet Money book around the country. Steven must set the first print run high enough that no customer walks into any bookstore asking for the book and leaves empty-handed—a stock-out means losing momentum that's nearly impossible to recover when reprinting takes months. Simultaneously, he must avoid printing so many books that the warehouse becomes clogged with slow-moving inventory consuming capital and warehouse space.

**Pulping as Industrial Waste Management**

If a book severely underperforms, publishers have two options: remaindering (selling bulk inventory to discount retailers who resell at 40% off or deeper) or pulping. Steven describes witnessing pulping in the 1990s at a major publisher warehouse—a giant conveyor belt fed books into an industrial shredder operating 24/7, reducing a $29.99 hardcover to 10 cents of recyclable pulp in 15 seconds. Publishers sometimes choose pulping over remaindering because finding a book in a discount bin or seeing the hardcover discounted right before a paperback release damages brand perception. Steven used Walter Isaacson's Elon Musk biography as an example—it sold well initially, then stopped; hundreds of thousands of leftover copies likely faced pulping or remaindering. The image of literary destruction stayed with Steven throughout his career, informing his drive for precision in print run calculations. W.W. Norton avoids pulping when possible, but the option exists as a last resort for managing unsold inventory.

**Distribution Strategy and Multi-Channel Placement**

Steven Pace mobilized Norton's entire sales force to place the Planet Money book in "everything, every chain bookstore, every independent bookstore." The distribution extends far beyond traditional retail. Planet Money books appear in airport bookstores (with 10+ copy bays), online retailers like Amazon and Bookshop.org, libraries, gift shops, military bases, and cruise ships. Cruise line bookstores exist onboard, and at port calls, carts with 20-40 books appear when ships dock. The book has been ordered by retailers in Europe, the Middle East, Thailand, Singapore, and Malaysia. A Korean translation is scheduled for June 2026 and a Chinese translation for September 2026. Steven's approach mirrors Michael Lewis's distribution strategy—maximum visibility across all possible retail channels.

**The Planet Money Book's Specific Journey**

Alexi Horowitz-Ghazi visited Carmichael's bookstore to follow the Planet Money book through this system. Fischer encountered the book in Norton's winter 2026 catalog in November, evaluated it in ~30 seconds, and initially ordered four copies per store (supporting display table placement). Norton's sales representative, Meg Sherman, nudged Fischer during their seasonal meeting, suggesting Fischer consider ordering more. Combined with the unusual factor that Alexi's visit to Carmichael's for this episode suggested local demand would rise, Fischer ultimately ordered 20 copies per store for a total of 40+ copies across both locations. The book's comparables (99% Invisible and Atlas Obscura books, both strong sellers at Carmichael's) suggested brand recognition would translate to demand. On the official launch day, Alexi purchased a copy from McNally Jackson bookstore in lower Manhattan, completing the journey from publisher decision to shelf to customer hand.

# The Ezra Klein Show — "Fareed Zakaria on the Moral Cost of Trump's War"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Fareed Zakaria (host of Fareed Zakaria GPS on CNN; columnist for the Washington Post; author of *The Age of Revolutions*; foreign policy scholar specializing in geopolitics and American global leadership).

## TL;DR

- **Trump threatened to annihilate Iranian civilization** — posting "A whole civilization will die tonight. Never to be brought back again" on social media over Easter weekend, articulating what would constitute genocide if executed, before backing down to a ceasefire two days later.
- **The war strengthened Iran's position, not America's** — Iran began with a destroyed nuclear program and US military superiority; it ended with demonstrated control over the Strait of Hormuz (worth ~$90B annually in future revenue), rights to uranium enrichment, lifted sanctions, and demand for reparations—all previously unthinkable concessions.
- **Trump's rhetoric abandons 80 years of American moral leadership** — Unlike Bush in Iraq (who invoked ideals of nation-building), Trump frames geopolitics as pure extraction: "How can I squeeze this situation for money?" rather than creating an open global economy and public goods.
- **A "predatory hegemon" hollows out US soft power** — If America now acts rapaciously like other empires rather than differently, allies (Canada, India, Australia) will stop betting their futures on US integration and instead "buy insurance" through diversified relationships with China and others—a structural shift hard to reverse.
- **Trump mused about charging tolls on the Strait of Hormuz** — a radical departure from America's 200-year commitment to freedom of navigation; this extractive mentality contrasts starkly with the post-WWII order Roosevelt designed to help defeated nations rebuild.
- **The war destroyed more than Iranian targets** — It weakened the Western alliance, elevated China's role in Middle East settlements, shifted trade payments toward crypto and yuan (undermining the dollar), and lifted Russian oil revenues by $4–5B monthly, while America gained near-zero strategic benefit.
- **Netanyahu's legacy may be splitting US-Israel solidarity** — Israel won militarily but faces eroding American support (majorities now unfavorable), Dutch/German alienation, and a 10% occupation of Lebanon with 600,000 displaced residents, raising questions about long-term costs of short-term military victory.
- **Liberalism's institutional exhaustion leaves it vulnerable** — The left institutionalized its moral ideas (democracy, human rights, welfare states) into rules and bureaucracies that now feel hollow; Trump's appeal exploits this by offering naked self-interest and passion, while liberals defend procedures rather than values.

---

**Trump's Threat and the Geopolitical Oscillation**

Zakaria opens by discussing Trump's Easter Sunday post threatening to "wipe out" Iranian civilization, followed days later by a ceasefire and talk of partnering with Iran. This whiplash—from genocide threat to negotiation—reveals something Zakaria calls behavior warranting a "wellness check." He notes that even traditionally conservative figures like Tucker Carlson, Megan Kelly, Marjorie Taylor Greene, and Alex Jones called the threat "vile," "evil," "madness," and "genocidal."

The threat itself matters because it abandons what Zakaria considers fundamental to American identity: a post-WWII commitment that the US would not be "another imperial hegemon." From the founding, America had theoretically positioned itself differently from European powers—not extracting tribute from defeated nations, but rebuilding them, inventing foreign aid, and creating an open global system. Trump's casual threat to annihilate 93 million people signals a departure to an older model of power: 18th-century rapacious imperialism where individual human dignity doesn't factor into calculations of dominance.

**The Actual Outcome: Iran's Unexpected Gains**

Klein and Zakaria examine the claim that Trump's threats were a successful negotiating bluff—that frightening Iran forced concessions. The evidence suggests the opposite. At the war's start, Iran's nuclear program was "completely and totally obliterated" (Trump's own phrase, echoed by Israel's atomic agency). Iran had no meaningful military capacity to project power beyond its borders.

By the ceasefire, Iran had lost some military hardware but gained something far more consequential: demonstrated control of the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly 30% of global seaborne oil passes. Iran's new 10-point peace plan—which Trump described as a "workable basis for negotiation"—includes Iran controlling the strait, uranium enrichment rights, lifting all sanctions, and reparations. Zakaria's key insight: why, if these were Iran's maximum demands, didn't we negotiate on this basis three months ago? The answer is we couldn't have—Iran would never have asked for strait control before proving it could enforce it militarily. Trump's war handed Iran leverage it lacked, then let Iran keep it.

The financial implications are staggering. Iran currently makes ~$45B annually from oil exports. Control of strait tolls could generate $90B yearly—doubling its revenue. Zakaria emphasizes this is not a negotiating win for America; it's America backing down from a position of strength.

**The Extractive Vision of Trump's Geopolitics**

Zakaria identifies Trump's worldview as fundamentally transactional: "How can I squeeze this situation for money?" When Trump mused about the US and Iran jointly controlling the Strait of Hormuz and the US "taking a cut of those tolls," Klein and Zakaria treat this as historically radical. For the entire post-WWII era, America's foreign policy doctrine held that the US would ensure freedom of navigation globally—not charge rent. This traces back to 1798 (the Quasi-War with France was over navigation rights) and the wars against Barbary pirates.

In contrast, Trump's instinct is pure extraction: tariffs where he spots divergence, squeezing allies dependent on US military aid, taking a cut of critical chokepoints. Zakaria argues this is disastrous not just morally but strategically. The post-WWII system succeeded because it was enlightened self-interest: America built an open global economy, which enriched America enormously. Short-term extraction erodes the system America benefits from most.

**Geopolitical Consequences: Reordering Around America**

The war and Trump's behavior are triggering structural shifts in how other powers relate to America. Canada, for 30–40 years, bet its entire future on tight integration with the US—politically, economically, every way. Watching Trump use that dependence to extract concessions, Canada is now diversifying relationships with China and India, "buying insurance." India is doing the same. Zakaria warns this is a cascading process: once countries believe America might defect and pursue self-interest above alliance commitments, they have incentive to defect first. The result is a downward spiral toward a less open, less rule-based world.

China is notably free-riding: it benefits from the US-maintained global order while criticizing it and having no alternative vision. If America abandons leadership, the world doesn't reorganize around China; it fragments. European powers are now desperate for American leadership precisely because they've imagined life without it and found it bleak. But that desire for the old US role is being tested by Trump's consistent undermining.

The war also reordered the Gulf. China had to get involved to broker settlements; payments are now made in crypto or Chinese yuan instead of dollars, weakening dollar hegemony. Russia gained ~$4–5B monthly in extra oil revenue. The Western Alliance fractured as Trump blamed allies despite their irrelevance to the outcome.

**Netanyahu's Pyrrhic Victory**

Zakaria acknowledges that from Netanyahu's narrow view (Iran as existential threat), the war achieved something: Iran's military is crippled and its nuclear program destroyed. But he questions the cost. Israel already had military superiority; containment was working. The Obama-era nuclear deal had zero enrichment, 98% of Iran's uranium removed from the country, and the most intrusive inspections ever.

More troubling: Netanyahu's decades as prime minister may be remembered for splitting the US-Israel alliance. A majority of Americans now view Israel unfavorably; young people overwhelmingly so. In Germany (historically pro-Israel for Holocaust reasons), youth are increasingly alienated. The Dutch joined South Africa's case at the International Court of Justice. Israel occupies 10% of Lebanon, has displaced 1 million Lebanese, with 600,000 possibly never allowed home—a scale equal to a second Nakba.

Zakaria notes Israel was founded with the aspiration to be "a light unto nations." For most of the world, it no longer appears that way. That is "a huge moral loss" because Israel's legitimacy rested partly on a moral claim.

**The Broader Question: What Civilization Died?**

Klein reframes the threat: perhaps the civilization that died isn't Iranian but American—American civilization understood as commitment to rules, decency, and restraint. Trump routinely violates what we might call "civilized behavior": the tweets, the rhetoric against enemies, the contempt for norms. Klein argues Trump has shown these values to be hollow and unenforceable.

Zakaria doesn't entirely agree. He insists on comparing America to other great powers, not to an ideal. Compared to the Soviet Union, Nazi Germany, Imperial Britain, Imperial France, and Imperial Japan—all rapacious colonial empires—America has been qualitatively different. It rebuilt Europe and Asia, created foreign aid, and structured power differently. Yes, America made mistakes (Vietnam, hypocrisy on democracy). But the broad historical arc shows a distinctive attempt to use hegemonic power differently.

This leads to a broader exchange about liberalism's exhaustion. Zakaria notes that liberalism has essentially won: every social democratic demand from the 19th century (emancipation, women's equality, racial equality, child labor laws, welfare states) has been institutionalized across the West. But those victories are now routinized into bureaucracies that feel uninspiring. Liberalism lacks a grand narrative or mission; it offers procedural defense of institutions rather than a moral vision.

**Liberalism's Institutional Problem**

Klein pushes back: liberalism began with profound moral ideas about individual dignity and freedom. Over time, it encoded these into rules and institutions (treaties, laws, organizations). But institutions fail, bureaucratize, and become unresponsive. The problem is that when people lose faith in institutions, liberalism has no answer other than "defend them anyway." It can't appeal to the underlying values with passion and inspiration the way Trump can appeal to naked self-interest and power.

Zakaria concedes the point has merit. He worries that liberalism is too suspicious of passion, too invested in moderation, precisely because it was born from distrust of religious fervor and hierarchical domination. But he's uncertain whether "finding meaning" in politics is safe; the post-liberal right might channel that passion toward dangerous ends.

He does offer one area where liberalism lost its moorings: affirmative action. Lyndon Johnson's original case—remedying 100 years of enslavement and Jim Crow—made sense. But it expanded to all people of color, quotas, DEI mandates, until core liberal principles (judging people by character, not color, as MLK said) were abandoned. Liberalism failed to course-correct institutionally.

**The Hauntings of Imperial Overstretch**

Zakaria raises a historical parallel: the British Empire. After World War I, it reached its maximum territorial size—then collapsed within 20–30 years. The reason: British elites became obsessed with controlling Iraq, Afghanistan, and strategic nodes (the Suez Canal, Upper Sudan) to secure supply lines, only to find themselves thinly stretched. Meanwhile, Germany and America were becoming more productive; Britain was neglecting its own competitive position.

America faces a similar dynamic. In 25 years, the US has fought three Middle Eastern wars, expending enormous military, political, and moral capital in regions where strategic gains are marginal. Meanwhile, China is rising (though it's a "free rider" on the US-maintained global order, offering no alternative vision). The temptation to maintain imperial control of distant regions while one's actual competitive position erodes is a haunting parallel.

Zakaria notes that when world opinion polling shows 36% approval of Chinese leadership vs. 31% for American, it's less an endorsement of China than a rejection of America. But it signals decline in relative standing.

**Books and Closing Thoughts**

Zakaria recommends three books:

1. *A World Safe for Democracy* by John Ikenberry—explains what the "rules-based international order" actually is and why America built it.
2. *The Irony of American History* by Reinhold Niebuhr—warns of the danger of powerful nations believing they are virtuous; calls for "Christian realism" (meaning the humility at Christianity's core).
3. *The Quiet American* by Graham Greene—a novel set in Vietnam showing an idealistic American abroad who believes America will bring peace and justice, contrasted with a world-weary British journalist. "Sometimes novels do it better than anything else," Zakaria notes.

He closes by emphasizing that though Trump represents something dangerous, it's not yet clear if it's temporary or a deep, lasting shift in American identity. Much depends on whether 2028 brings a clear repudiation of Trumpism. If not, the world's structural reordering may be irreversible. America still has moral capital built over 80 years, but "reputations take a lifetime to build and are very easy to destroy."

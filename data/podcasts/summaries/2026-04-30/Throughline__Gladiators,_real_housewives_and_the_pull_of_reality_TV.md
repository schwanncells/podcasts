# Throughline — "Gladiators, Real Housewives, and the Pull of Reality TV"

**Host:** Runda Abdel-Fattah. **Guests:** Galoka Bolte (reality TV casting director, 20 years; cast *Joe Millionaire*, *MasterChef*, *Let's Make a Deal*, *RuPaul's Drag Race*, *Million Dollar Listing*, *The Real Housewives of New Jersey*, *F-Boy Island*); Dr. Janice Gravani (licensed clinical psychologist); Raquel Gates (associate professor of film and media studies, Columbia University); Dr. Jatia Hart (nuclear engineer, *Survivor* Season 28 contestant); and Jeff Jenkins (founder/executive producer, *Keeping Up with the Kardashians* spinoffs).

## TL;DR

- **Gladiators appeased Roman masses by channeling their rage downward** — enslaved people watched combat to feel momentarily superior to the people they were watching, keeping anger directed at spectacle rather than system. Reality TV uses the same formula today.
- **Milgram experiments (1962) showed 2/3 of Americans would administer potentially fatal electric shocks** when a man in a white coat told them to. The findings horrified the country and seeded decades of psychology research into what makes ordinary people do cruel things — research that directly informed reality TV's emotional manipulation playbook.
- **Survivor (2000) launched the pseudo-bloodsport era** — framed as a Lord of the Flies social experiment during post-Y2K technophobia. Editing and dramatic music create narrative tension from unedited footage that would be "quite boring." Casting director Bolte: editing "boil[s] down" and "make[s] a character" and "make[s] a story."
- **Trump revived his career through The Apprentice as 21st-century kayfabe** — after bankruptcy and failed casinos in the 1990s, his larger-than-life persona faded. The show (a Survivor spinoff) let him rebuild his brand, and supporters assumed his wealth meant he was good at everything else. From *Apprentice* host to president, he used the reality TV formula: stoke anxiety/fear, then promise only he can fix it.
- **Kardashians built a multi-billion-dollar brand by sharing their dysfunction** — started from a high-profile murder case (OJ trial) and a sex tape, transformed into "America's royal family." Producer Jenkins: "If you grow up with that, and it's seeping into your pores, it just becomes part of who you are." They showed that ordinary people with a camera can become influential, embodying a new American dream: celebrity and unbridled wealth.
- **Reality TV's love shows have evolved from fantasy (The Bachelor) to cynicism** — *The Bachelor* (20+ years) fused romance with fairy-tale narrative and proposal endings. *F-Boy Island* mirrors dating apps as competitive swipe-and-discard. Newer shows like *Married at First Sight* and *90 Day Fiancé* focus on post-marriage realities: "the reel slips out" in quiet moments of conflict, acknowledging that love is hard to sustain.
- **Loneliness drives the appeal** — as technology substitutes online for in-person connection, people form parasocial relationships with reality TV characters. We're all curating images online, expecting perfection, sharing everything. Reality TV provides what life doesn't: neat narrative conclusions and the fantasy of human connection.

**The Rage Machine: Gladiators Then, Reality TV Now**

The episode opens with a comparison: Roman emperors used gladiatorial combat to manage the rage of enslaved and impoverished masses. By watching one person defeat another, people could channel their anger into spectacle rather than rebellion. Reality TV uses an identical formula. Gravani notes that *Survivor* and *Real Housewives* tap "similar emotions" — the schadenfreude of watching someone lose, the momentary sense of superiority, the cathartic expression of anger. The parallel is deliberate: both systems transform systemic powerlessness into entertainment.

**Survivor: The Birth of Pseudo-Bloodsport**

In 2000, at the height of post-Y2K technophobia, two cultural events collided: the film *Gladiator* (celebrating real-life bloodsports) and the debut of *Survivor*, a new reality show framed as a Lord of the Flies social experiment. Survivor producer Mark Burnett explicitly drew on *Swiss Family Robinson*, *Robinson Crusoe*, and *Lord of the Flies* as inspiration — the fantasy of a pre-civilization society magically conforming to what viewers already believed about human nature. Gates observed: "It feels like the fantasy of Survivor is that you have this, like, pre-civilization society that magically conforms to everything you already sort of believe about society, but it naturalizes it. So it's not, like, producer interference. It's not sexism. It just so happens to be that, you know, young dudes dominate the game over and over and over."

The production mechanics are transparent but effective. Bolte explained that contestants sign releases understanding they're being filmed, but what viewers see is edited reality: "If you watched the unedited footage all the way through, it would be quite boring." Editing adds "suspenseful music to kind of create the mood and anticipation" and reorders events "for context to make it feel more exciting and more dramatic." Hart, a nuclear engineer who competed in Season 28, describes feeling like "a cog in the machine" — the show's editing reduced her to a character trait (bossy, not intellectual) rather than a full person.

**Psychology, Power, and the Rage Machine Formula**

Gates traced the psychological infrastructure underlying reality TV back to post-World War II. After witnessing Nazi atrocities, Americans grappled with the question: could ordinary people do evil? This led to the Milgram experiments (1962), where researchers told volunteers to administer electric shocks to another person. Two-thirds obeyed, even when warned the shocks were potentially fatal, because a man in a white coat told them to. The Stanford Prison Experiment followed, using similar methods to probe human cruelty. These experiments horrified America but also became a blueprint: reality TV discovered it could place ordinary people in controlled settings and watch their behavior deteriorate.

Gates connected this directly to the late-1990s cable news boom. With proliferating networks competing for viewers, news became sensational entertainment. The OJ Simpson trial, the Oklahoma City bombing, 9/11, and subsequent wars were packaged as ongoing dramas with dramatic music and emotional manipulation. The formula crystallized: create fear and rage, then promise that only by staying tuned can viewers stay safe. This "rage machine," as Gates called it, ran on advertiser dollars — networks made money on fear because fear kept people watching.

**The American Dream, Kayfabe, and Trump**

The episode's second major section traces how reality TV collapsed the line between performance and reality, using Trump as the central case study. Gates explained that pro-wrestling had long used the term "kayfabe" — maintaining your character outside the ring. Trump, a WWE regular, applied kayfabe to himself. In the 1970s–90s, his name became synonymous with American wealth and the American dream. But by 2000, he was a "washed-up businessman" with bankruptcies and failed casinos. When *The Apprentice* (a Survivor-ish business competition) came calling, he seized the chance to revive his persona. On the show, he claimed to be "the largest real estate developer in New York," owner of "model agencies, the Miss Universe pageant, casinos." None of that was literally true — he had to rebuild the illusion.

The kayfabe worked. Supporters assumed: if he's wealthy and successful in business, he must be good at everything else, including running the country. Gates: "People assumed, or Trump supporters at least, that if he's a wealthy, successful, powerful businessman... I think he's smart enough to run the country." From 2016 onward, Trump applied the reality TV formula to politics itself — stoke outrage and fear ("Get her out of here"), promise only he could fix it ("You're fired"), and repeat until the formula took him to the presidency.

**Kardashians and the New American Dream**

While Trump was reviving his brand, the Kardashians were building theirs from scratch. They entered public consciousness via the OJ Simpson trial (their father was OJ's attorney). Then Kim released a sex tape. Rather than disappearing in shame, the family leaned into the camera. Producer Jenkins was struck by their comfort: "When I first saw video of the entire family... that bell goes off." Unlike Kennedy or other political families who had parasocial relationships thrust upon them, the Kardashians *chose* constant visibility. Jenkins notes: "Kim, whose destiny is this experience of being on television and sharing, had very few boundaries. She will reassure me, no, keep rolling."

The show ran for 20+ seasons and spawned a multi-billion-dollar brand empire. Gates emphasized the timing: "Kim came into her prime exactly as social media was becoming the way of the world. That's lightning in a bottle, timing." They embodied a new American dream — not the post-war home-and-family fantasy, but unbridled wealth, celebrity, and the idea that anyone with a camera and shamelessness could become influential. The trade-off: constant surveillance, vulnerability to judgment, and no distinction between public and private life.

**Reality TV's Evolution: From Fantasy Love to Cynical Relationships**

The final section tracks how dating shows have shifted from escapist fantasy to cynical realism. *The Bachelor* (20+ years on air) married romance to fairy-tale narrative — the ending is a proposal with a diamond ring, fulfilling the fantasy of "the one." To maintain the illusion, producers must believe in the fantasy themselves; the casting process seeks people who could plausibly be great matches, not "the craziest person" for ratings.

But as real life became lonelier, reality TV evolved. *F-Boy Island* treats dating as a competitive game mimicking Tinder — "swipe" through partners, curate your image, or you get no matches. Newer shows like *Married at First Sight* and *90 Day Fiancé* skip the proposal ending and focus on post-marriage realities: the moment when the "reel slips out" and conflict emerges. These shows acknowledge, as Gates put it, "a growing cynicism, quite frankly, around, like, traditional models and narratives around love and around relationships."

**Loneliness and Parasocial Connection**

The throughline connecting all three sections is loneliness. Technology has substituted online connections for offline ones. People now spend hours scrolling social media, curating their own images, expecting perfection, forming parasocial relationships with strangers. As Gates noted: "The lonelier we get, the more seductive the fantasy that we'll find real human connection becomes." Reality TV provides what real life doesn't: neat narrative conclusions, clear winners, and the feeling of knowing someone intimately (even though it's one-directional). We watch characters struggle with the same things we do — love, money, status — but edited into coherent stories with resolution. It's a refraction, not a reflection of reality, but it lets us work out our hopes and anxieties through other people's lives.

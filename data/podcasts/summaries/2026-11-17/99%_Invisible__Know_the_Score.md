# 99% Invisible — "Know the Score"

**Host:** Roman Mars. **Guest:** Vivian Leigh (producer; also the subject of the main narrative about identity theft and credit score recovery).

## TL;DR

- **Identity theft via data breach led to fraudulent car loans and utilities in Vivian's name** — someone used her leaked personal information to attempt a zero-down Mercedes-Benz purchase and register for multiple apartments, utilities, and car insurance, forcing her into months of police reports, FTC affidavits, and credit recovery.
- **Credit scores range 300–850 and determine housing, car loans, and credit access in the US** — the three-digit number is now a universal gatekeeper despite originating as a narrowly targeted tool for businessmen assessing other businessmen.
- **Louis Tappan founded the Mercantile Agency in 1841 as the first large-scale credit reporting service** — it collected reputational and financial data on merchants using a network of local attorneys, compiling information into ledger books read aloud behind screens to protect subscriber privacy.
- **Early credit reports relied on subjective character judgments** — women required male account holders, non-white applicants faced discrimination, and gossip (like rumors about a merchant's wife leaving him) could destroy creditworthiness; one merchant sued Tappan for libel and reached the Supreme Court.
- **FICO scores launched in 1956 by William Fair and Earl Isaac to remove human bias** — the algorithm evaluated payment history, debt amount, credit history length, recent applications, and account variety rather than drinking habits or marital status.
- **Equal Credit Opportunity Act (1974) made credit scores the legal shield against discrimination claims** — lenders realized algorithmic screening eliminated gender, race, and religion as explicit factors, though historical inequities remained embedded in the data itself.
- **One in five consumers has verified credit report errors; one in 20 has errors serious enough to deny credit** — regional credit bureaus consolidated into three monopolies (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) that profit from selling consumer data despite poor security (Equifax 2017 breach compromised ~147 million people).
- **Credit freeze is free, available since 2018, and blocks new lenders from accessing your report** — freezing does not affect existing credit cards or scores and can be toggled instantly online or by phone to allow specific credit checks.

**The Origins of Credit Reporting and the Mercantile Agency**

For most of American history before the 1830s, credit operated within tight-knit communities where lenders and borrowers knew each other personally or through secondhand reputation. As the country industrialized and cities grew, strangers began conducting large credit transactions across vast distances with no way to verify trustworthiness. Josh Lauer, a historian of consumer surveillance and financial identity, explains that the Panic of 1837—a major financial crisis that collapsed hundreds of banks and left thousands of borrowers unable to repay—forced the issue. Merchants realized the economy depended on credit but lacked reliable information.

Louis Tappan, a New York merchant and abolitionist whose silk business was nearly bankrupted in the panic, saw an opportunity. In 1841, he founded the Mercantile Agency, the world's first large-scale credit reporting service. It collected financial and reputational data on credit seekers and sold it to lenders. Tappan built a network of confidential correspondents—mainly attorneys scattered across the country—who gathered information on merchants in their local communities. In exchange for free work, the correspondents received debt collection referrals if a merchant they reported on defaulted. The data was housed in intimidating ledger books, and to protect subscriber privacy, clerks read information aloud from behind screens so subscribers could not see other entries.

**Character, Capacity, and Capital: The Subjective Foundations of Creditworthiness**

Early creditworthiness determinations rested on the "three C's": character, capacity, and capital. Capital meant property and assets; capacity meant work experience and business success. Character—the dominant factor—was deeply subjective: lenders judged whether a person was well-liked, honest, lazy, a drunkard, or a gambler. These judgments often relied on gossip, hearsay, and personal opinion. One merchant discovered his report included a rumor that his wife was about to leave him, and he sued Tappan for libel in a case that reached the Supreme Court.

Merchants hated the system, calling it espionage and gossip-peddling that could destroy them whether the information was true or relevant. The Mercantile Agency had no obligation to disclose reports to the people being reported on. Despite the outcry, satisfied lenders found the service invaluable—one customer reported saving "$100 for every dollar" spent on information. Within decades, the credit reporting industry expanded from New York to Boston, Baltimore, and Philadelphia, eventually spreading nationwide.

**The Rise of Consumer Credit and the Credit Man**

After the Civil War, the credit reporting industry evolved from serving businesses to serving grocers and department stores. Grocers needed to screen everyday customers paying by tab; they no longer knew everyone in their neighborhood. Department stores supercharged the industry by offering credit accounts and charge accounts to boost sales through volume. This necessitated dedicated credit departments and a new profession: the credit man.

The credit man—typically a white man in a suit—sat across from customers and determined creditworthiness through interrogation of personal character. This system put the subjectivity of credit assessment on full display. Women had to open accounts in a man's name and could not hold their own credit. Non-white applicants faced discrimination and were unlikely to even attempt opening accounts at major department stores. The credit man's subjective judgment remained the foundation of creditworthiness well into the 20th century, embedding racial and gender bias into credit access.

**FICO Scores and the Promise of Objectivity**

In 1956, engineer William Fair and mathematician Earl Isaac founded Fair Isaac and Company (FICO) to develop a more efficient, computer-based way of screening creditworthiness. Rather than evaluating character through personal judgment, they created a statistical model that scored five measurable factors: payment history, amount owed, length of credit history, recent credit applications, and variety of credit accounts. The algorithm output a single, clean three-digit number—the credit score—that promised objectivity and efficiency.

Credit scoring gradually caught on, but lenders became fully motivated to embrace it in 1974 with the passage of the Equal Credit Opportunity Act, which made it illegal to deny credit based on gender, marital status, race, color, religion, or age. Lenders realized that algorithmic scoring provided a legal defense against discrimination claims. By 1989, all major consumer credit bureaus adopted FICO's standardized model. By 1995, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac began requiring credit scores for mortgage screening, cementing the score's weight in American financial life.

**The Hidden Inequities in Algorithmic Scoring**

Credit scores appeared to solve the problem of bias by removing human judgment, but they did not eliminate the underlying unfairness. Chi-Chi Wu, director of consumer reporting advocacy at the National Consumer Law Center, explains that while scores removed the subjectivity of "the credit man doesn't like the way you look," they embedded a different kind of systemic problem: they took historical inequality—centuries of slavery, Jim Crow, and discrimination in lending—and encoded it into an algorithm that then perpetuated and reinforced it.

A credit score cannot explain why a person missed payments; it only records that they did. Medical emergencies, job loss, illness, and accidents can impair credit, but the score treats all delinquency equally, conflating financial irresponsibility with circumstance. More fundamentally, the US has a long history of denying credit to Black and Latino consumers, creating generational wealth gaps. When credit scores treat everyone as if they started on a level playing field, they embed that historical disadvantage deeper and use it as a gatekeeper to housing, insurance, employment, and further credit.

**The Big Three and Data Security**

Over 100 years, thousands of regional credit bureaus consolidated into three: Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion. Wu describes them as a "functional monopoly" because consumers have no choice—you must have your data in their system if you want credit. This lack of competition means the big three have little incentive to fix errors or improve security. One in five consumers has a verified error on their credit report; one in 20 has an error serious enough to deny credit. In 2017, Equifax experienced one of the largest data breaches in US history, compromising the personal information of approximately 147 million Americans. Despite handling consumers' most sensitive financial data, the big three profit from selling that data while offering identity theft protection services against the breaches they themselves caused—a perverse arrangement Josh Lauer calls "fairly twisted."

**Credit Freezes: A Consumer Tool and Public Service Announcement**

In the second half of the episode, Vivian walks listeners through the practical mechanics of credit freezes. A credit freeze blocks new lenders from accessing your credit report without your consent, preventing unauthorized credit applications in your name. The tool became free and legally required in 2018, partly in response to the Equifax breach. Freezing your credit has no impact on existing credit cards, credit score, or existing loans; it only blocks new credit inquiries. Identity theft occurs every 4.9 seconds in the US, making credit freezes an essential protective measure. The process is simple: sign up for a free account with each of the three bureaus, verify your identity, and toggle a freeze on or off instantly through their websites, apps, or by phone. If you need to open new credit, you can unfreeze with a specific bureau, allow the lender to pull your report, and refreeze immediately—all at no cost and with unlimited toggles.

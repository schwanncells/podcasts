# Bits + Bips — "What Nvidia's CFO Pick Says About the Jensen Huang Probe"

**Host:** Austin Campbell (host, Zero Knowledge Group). **Guests:** Ram Alawalia (leader, Lumina); Seth (Franklin Templeton).

**Jensen Huang's Resignation and the National Security Dimension**

Jensen Huang resigned as NVIDIA's CEO and board member on November 12, following admissions of infidelity. The core allegation centers on a relationship with Alice Wong, an executive at Megaspeed, a Singapore firm that reportedly purchased billions of dollars' worth of NVIDIA chips over two years. U.S. officials suspect Wong may be a Chinese intelligence recruit. The board opened an independent national security probe, and Reuters has also reported Huang had an affair with Kimberly Powell, VP of healthcare, as well as a second unnamed employee.

The timing and severity of the resignation remain contested. Ram Alawalia argues that infidelity is not uncommon among founders (citing Elon Musk and Bill Gates) and shouldn't be the decisive factor. Instead, the question is whether Huang knowingly violated export control laws by allowing chips to reach China through Megaspeed—a determination only the board's investigation can answer. Scaramucci's position, which speakers largely agree with, is that Huang cannot be reinstated until the investigation concludes. If no violations are found, speakers argue the board has compounded its error by removing him.

**Why Colette Kress as Interim CEO Raises Questions**

The selection of CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO is controversial and may signal deeper internal complications. Ram argues this was a "terrible decision" because CFOs are not general managers—they don't understand the business, can't negotiate supply chains, and lack customer relationships. The natural internal successor would have been Ian, head of the data center business, who built NVIDIA's core revenue driver. Instead, the board chose Kress, who has historically been skeptical of Huang's financing deals for open-source AI development.

One theory is that the board wanted to signal an external search while avoiding promotion of a potentially compromised internal candidate—suggesting Ian may have knowledge of or involvement in the chip-diversion scheme. Alternatively, speakers suggest the board is running a pure holding pattern and plans to recruit an external CEO (ARM's CEO or Broadcom's COO mentioned as possibilities) while Kress stabilizes investor relations. Seth argues Kress serves as a credible interface with Wall Street and the buy-side during the crisis, while also noting her appointment wouldn't reassure customers the way a business operator would.

**NVIDIA's Role in Chip Proliferation to China**

Chinese frontier labs—DeepSeek, Kimmy, and others—have reportedly incorporated NVIDIA's Blackwell chips, the company's flagship product. This suggests either intentional export control violations or a failure of NVIDIA's own compliance regime. Ram frames this as NVIDIA's accountability issue: the company has a finite customer base and plenty of tools to ensure compliance (server inspection, tracking systems), yet billions of dollars' worth of chips reached China anyway.

The question of *how* remains contested. Some sources allege China leaked information about Huang's affair in retaliation after he attempted to restrict Megaspeed shipments. Others, particularly Rubio allies, argue the affair was incidental and that geopolitical pressure is the real driver of Huang's removal.

**The Rubio Factor and Open-Source AI Policy**

Marco Rubio, a known China hawk, reportedly reviewed a CIA report alleging Wong was an MSS recruit and rewrote it in a more aggressive fashion, circulating it through the White House and to Trump. Rubio's broader strategic goal may be to eliminate NVIDIA's support for open-weight models and low-cost, accessible AI—philosophies Huang has championed through investments in Hugging Face, Poolside, and other open-source projects.

Ram argues that Huang's view—letting China build on the American tech stack—is actually sound policy. It generates export revenue, enables competition, and prevents monopolies. Rubio's view is incompatible with this. Without Huang's backing, speakers predict a sharp decline in financing for open-source alternatives and a narrowing of the AI ecosystem toward proprietary frontier models.

**Huang's Ecosystem Impact and GPU Financing**

Huang did more than sell chips; he engineered credit agreements that allowed CoreWeave, Nebius, and other infrastructure startups (NeoClouds) to exist. Ram describes NVIDIA as "the central banker for AI," underwriting growth that would otherwise be impossible. With Huang sidelined and Colette Kress—historically skeptical of these deals—in an interim role, sources expect GPU financing to tighten significantly. This would slow capex investment in the AI infrastructure layer and potentially ripple into broader macro effects, especially if the Fed is simultaneously raising rates.

Austin notes this creates a timing problem: if the Fed raises rates three times into mid-2027 while also cutting GPU financing, the U.S. risks a policy mistake that depresses AI-driven GDP growth (which has contributed one to two percentage points recently).

**Huang's Potential Return and Political Dynamics**

Altimeter Capital's Brad Gerstner and Gavin Baker co-signed a letter asking the board to consider bringing Huang back, suggesting he gave his blessing or at least did not object. However, Scaramucci's warning—that reinstating a CEO while a sanctions investigation is active creates reputational and legal risk—carries weight. If the DOJ issues a criminal charge after Huang's reinstatement, it would be catastrophic for the company.

Seth notes that Huang's $25 million donation to the White House in October may have been strategic. Trump's cryptic 60 Minutes comment about "the color of jumpsuits" (referring to criminal defendants) adds urgency to the investigation. Speakers largely expect Huang to return within three months, contingent on the investigation's findings and political dynamics under the Trump administration. Ram and Seth both believe Trump's incoming administration will be more lenient on enforcement, making Huang's return more likely, though this remains speculative.

**Anthropic's IPO: Valuation and Business Model Uncertainty**

Anthropic priced its IPO at $1.4 trillion, raising $64 billion with up to 20% set aside for retail investors. The Class A structure grants economic rights but no voting power; seven co-founders retain 50.1% voting control through a founder LLC. The core metrics: Q3 revenue of $17 billion (up 47% quarter-over-quarter, but down from 144% growth in Q2), adjusted operating margin of 15%, and a net loss of $23.3 billion (including a $21.9 billion non-cash charge on SAFEs).

Seth views the IPO favorably, calling the S-1 "solid to strong" with no obvious red flags. He highlights Anthropic's 900% revenue retention, recent product announcements (a note-taking agent comparable to Granola), and structural advantage over OpenAI due to Microsoft's revenue-sharing arrangement with OpenAI (which limits OpenAI's ability to reinvest in R&D). Seth's valuation range: fair at $1.4 trillion, richly priced above $2 trillion.

Ram raises a capital-intensity concern unique to frontier labs: unlike Meta or TikTok, Anthropic has committed to roughly $900 billion in capex. This creates a timing problem. Anthropic doesn't just have to be right on model capability; it has to monetize quickly enough to service its hardware commitments. If capex bills come due and revenue targets slip, the company faces existential pressure.

**The Business Model Transition: Tokens to Outcomes**

Seth argues the frontier lab business model is in transition. Currently, revenue comes from token pricing ($50 per million tokens). In the future, he expects direct monetization of outcomes—revenue-sharing on therapeutics, rare disease treatments, or other high-value applications. Anthropic's recent investment in wet lab space in San Francisco signals this shift.

Austin pushes back: if Anthropic shifts to outcome-based pricing (e.g., 20% of therapeutic revenue), it only works if Anthropic's marginal advantage is so large that companies willingly share equity-like economics rather than train on open-source alternatives. Chinese frontier models like Kimi K3.1 and Qwen 4 Max are "pretty fucking good," and if the price differential is hundreds of dollars versus percentage ownership of a moonshot, the choice becomes harder for customers.

Seth concedes the Chinese models create a one-cart-point competitive shadow—they're always about nine months behind frontier models due to distillation and training on frontier output. However, the U.S. could prohibit access to these models. Without Huang supporting open-source alternatives, customers may default to Anthropic simply because switching costs (data portability, context loss) are high.

**Adoption Trends and Open-Source Competitive Dynamics**

Austin highlights that some enterprises—particularly in financial crime and anti-fraud detection—prefer open-weight models for cases where proprietary training data cannot be shared with a third party. These use cases are hard to ban; MIT-licensed open-source software is protected in the U.S. by First Amendment precedent (encryption battles of the 1990s). Ram agrees that open-source models are real, permanent, and not subject to government prohibition.

Seth and Ram both acknowledge that Anthropic's non-coding market (MS Office competitors, creative tools, life sciences) has significant upside that coding and software development alone won't capture. The lock-in effect of multi-modal products (Anthropic's integrated note-taking, context retention across sessions) favors Anthropic over point solutions like Granola.

**Final Investment Verdict**

Seth and Ram are both buyers at $1.4 trillion for different reasons. Seth views it as a fair price that benefits crypto (a pop could send capital back into digital assets). Ram emphasizes Anthropic's $100+ billion in cash and conservative balance sheet, with only $350 billion in committed obligations—significantly lower than OpenAI's liabilities. Both expect Anthropic to navigate the business model transition, though the outcome remains uncertain and dependent on factors outside the company's direct control (open-source competitive pressure, regulatory environment, customer willingness to adopt outcome-based pricing).

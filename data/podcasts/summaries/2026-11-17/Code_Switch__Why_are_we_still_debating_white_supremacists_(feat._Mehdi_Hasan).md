# Code Switch — "Why are we still debating white supremacists? (feat. Mehdi Hasan)"

**Host:** B.A. Parker and Gene Demby. **Guests:** Mehdi Hasan (journalist, broadcaster, founder and editor-in-chief of Zeteo; professional debater and author of a book on argument strategy); Nicholas Bucola (professor of government at Claremont McKenna College; author of *The Fire is Upon Us*, a study of the 1965 Baldwin-Buckley debate).

## TL;DR

- **James Baldwin defeated William F. Buckley Jr. 544 to 100 in a 1965 Cambridge Union debate** on whether the American dream is built at the expense of Black Americans. Baldwin won by switching to first person ("I picked the cotton") to make the abstract personal, while Buckley attempted to discount Baldwin's standing entirely.
- **Mehdi Hasan participated in a Jubilee "Surrounded" episode with 20 far-right conservatives, including self-identified fascists**, deliberately to show YouTube's 14 million viewers that these figures should not be listened to or respected—despite the personal toll of being told by 20 people to leave the country.
- **Debate format determines outcomes—Baldwin lost a subsequent TV debate with Buckley** using a more aggressive format because Buckley's interruption and on-his-feet style gave him advantage. Format, audience, and rules of engagement are critical to debate effectiveness.
- **The core tension: platforming bigots legitimizes them, but staying silent concedes ground.** Hasan refuses to debate people debating in bad faith or questioning his existence, yet broke his own rule for Jubilee because the audience (not his opponent) matters most.
- **White supremacy as doctrine originates in Europe and harms both victims and perpetrators.** Baldwin argued that figures like Sheriff Jim Clark—using cattle prods against voting-rights activists—suffer a damaged soul; the system damages everyone it touches.
- **Social media clips distort debate outcomes.** A white supremacist from Jubilee clipped his own remarks narrowly to claim victory, contradicting what viewers see in the full episode, illustrating how algorithm-driven platforms enable bad-faith narrative control.
- **Marginalized people are increasingly asked to debate their own existence and worth.** Hasan argues that Muslims shouldn't have to disprove Islamophobia, Black people shouldn't have to fight anti-Black racism alone—the psychological cost of "debate me" culture falls unfairly on minorities.
- **Love of country, Baldwin-style, requires radical confrontation.** Baldwin modeled love rooted in consciousness-raising and demanding better, contrasting with Buckley's love-as-devotion. For immigrant critics, the demand to stay silent or "keep quiet" contradicts the American right to critique.

**Debate as a Tool for Truth-Seeking and Democracy**

Mehdi Hasan, a professional debater and journalist, frames debate as foundational to democracy. He believes good clash of opinions and ideologies is how democracies function and how people discover truth. However, Hasan is selective about whom he debates. He refuses to debate people in bad faith or those questioning his very existence—he declines Fox News invitations, for instance—but he recognizes debate's value when the audience is the real target, not the opponent. His book on argument strategy emphasizes that knowing your audience is the key to any debate.

**The Baldwin-Buckley Debate as a Historical Benchmark**

Nicholas Bucola, who authored a book-length study of the 1965 Cambridge Union debate, explains that Baldwin and Buckley embodied the "poets" of their respective revolutions. Baldwin was the leading writer of the civil rights movement; Buckley was a founder of American conservatism. Baldwin felt a responsibility to debate Buckley and others like segregationist James Jackson Kilpatrick, viewing sophisticated intellectuals who could eloquently defend segregation and white supremacy as more dangerous than street-level bigots—because they knew exactly what they were doing and could weave "webs of deception." Baldwin's strategy was to personalize the abstract: he shifted from discussing oppression of "Negroes" in general to saying "I picked the cotton. I took the cotton to market. I built the railroad under someone else's whip for nothing." This first-person turn made everyone in the room identify with Baldwin personally.

Buckley, trained in formal debate since childhood, countered by attempting to discount Baldwin's standing, insisting that skin color should be "utterly irrelevant" and implying Baldwin had been treated with unwarranted adoration due to white guilt. Buckley also accused Baldwin of using "British accents" in this debate—code-switching—that weren't his "real" language, a charge Bucola reads as Buckley trying to invalidate Baldwin's right to speak. Baldwin won the formal vote decisively. However, he lost a subsequent TV debate with Buckley a few months later, demonstrating that format fundamentally shapes outcomes: the TV format allowed Buckley to interrupt more aggressively, shifting advantage to him.

**The Dilemma of Platforming White Supremacists**

Hasan participated in Jubilee's "Surrounded" episode, sitting in the middle of a circle of 20 young far-right conservatives, including at least one self-identified fascist. He went in expecting Trump supporters but found people openly claiming fascism. When asked why he would do this, Hasan explained he was confident he could handle the number and saw an opportunity to perform for a third-party audience—the YouTube viewers. His goal was not to change the fascist's mind but to show 14 million viewers that these people should not be listened to or respected. Yet the experience took a profound personal toll. For two hours, he was told to leave the country, subjected to Islamophobic remarks, and pressured to defend his right to exist in America. Even as a thick-skinned professional with decades of experience, Hasan said it took a toll to sit as a brown Muslim immigrant and be told repeatedly that he shouldn't be there.

Hasan is torn about whether he would do it again. On one hand, he violated his own rule—he debates only those he considers worthy of debate and refuses to debate people who don't see him as an equal. On the other hand, if even 1 percent of the 14 million viewers walked away understanding that these were clearly Nazis and his points were good, he felt he achieved something. The dilemma persists: visibility and audience reach come with the cost of normalization and personal harm.

**The Role of Format, Audience, and Rules of Engagement**

Hasan emphasizes that debate outcomes depend heavily on rules of engagement. The Baldwin-Buckley Cambridge debate had formal parliamentary rules, points of information, and structured turns—conditions Baldwin had to have explained to him before the debate began, while Buckley trained in such formats his whole life. Under those rules, no one interrupted Baldwin, only Buckley. Had Baldwin not been protected by the format, the outcome might have differed. American high school debate has extensive technical rules that Hasan finds unhelpful for real-world argument—actual debates, he argues, have no rules; you must think on your feet in workplaces, on TV, in bars, and in politics.

**White Supremacy as a System That Harms Everyone**

Bucola explains Baldwin's framing of white supremacy as a doctrine that originated in Europe and was imported to America. Baldwin argued that everyone is harmed by it—not just those targeted but also those who perpetuate it. Baldwin pointed to Sheriff Jim Clark, seen on television using cattle prods against voting-rights activists, and said the ghastly thing happening to Clark's victims is matched by something "much, much worse" happening inside Clark's own soul. The system of white supremacy damages the humanity of both the subjugated and the subjugators by normalizing the false belief that social status is deserved.

**Social Media Distortion and Bad-Faith Narrative Control**

Hasan described a troubling dynamic after Jubilee: a white supremacist clipped his own remarks narrowly and posted them claiming he "destroyed" Mehdi Hasan, and his followers believed it. This is not what viewers see in the full episode, but social media's algorithmic echo chambers allow bad-faith actors to control narrative within their own circles. This is why Hasan worries about platforming right-wingers—not because a good-faith debate forum is bad, but because social media makes it trivial to distort outcomes and spread false claims to a captive audience.

**The Unfair Burden on Marginalized Communities**

Hasan points out that contemporary "debate me" culture has evolved to place an unfair burden on minorities. The debates that bubble up tend to be framed as: "Marginalized person, defend why you should exist." He argues that Muslims shouldn't have to disprove Islamophobia, Black people shouldn't have to fight anti-Black racism on their own, and the same applies to trans and gay people. The psychological and psychosocial toll of this expectation is enormous—it asks minority communities to do emotional labor to convince bigots of their own humanity.

**Baldwin's Model of Love as Confrontation**

Bucola contrasts Baldwin's and Buckley's views of patriotism. Baldwin modeled love of country as radical empathy combined with radical confrontation—making people conscious of what they cannot see and demanding they transform into something better. Buckley modeled love as devotion and protection of existing order. Baldwin imagined a version of love that doesn't rely on exclusion, that doesn't say "we're in, you're out." Hasan, as a naturalized immigrant, regularly faces social media attacks saying he has no right to critique America since he wasn't born there. He invokes Baldwin's famous line: "I love America more than any other country in the world, which is why I reserve the right to criticize her unconditionally." He argues that this is what America is—the right to critique and demand better is not a betrayal of love but its deepest expression.

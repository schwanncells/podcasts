# Gastropod — "A Dangerous Invention: The Secret History and Hidden Harms of Energy Drinks"

**Host:** Cynthia Graber, Nicola Twilley. **Guests:** Wolfgang Furweger (author, *The Red Bull Story*); Aaron Goldfarb (spirits and drinks journalist, author of *Dusty Booze in Search of Vintage Spirits*); Karen Pasch (health behavior researcher, University of Texas Austin); John Higgins (sports cardiologist, University of Texas Medical School).

## TL;DR

- **Red Bull was not invented in Austria but in Thailand** — Dietrich Mateschitz discovered the Thai drink Krateng Daing in the 1970s, acquired it from a father-son duo (taking 49% ownership), and relocated it to Austria where he reformulated it with fizz, new flavoring, and the iconic tall thin can to create the world's first successful energy drink brand.
- **Red Bull marketed itself through illegal bootlegging and club culture** — the drink was initially banned in Germany, but smugglers transported it to Munich clubs where its illegality made it appear cooler; once legalized in 1994, demand was so massive factories couldn't keep up with production.
- **Red Bull is fundamentally a marketing company, not a beverage manufacturer** — the company pays other firms to can, distribute, and manufacture the drink itself; Austria's business registry lists Red Bull's stated purpose as "making marketing for the brand of Red Bull" to earn money for founder Mateschitz.
- **Monster, Celsius, Prime, and others flooded the market after Red Bull's success** — the energy drink category exploded into an $85 billion U.S. market; the average American now consumes about 8 gallons per year, with brands using celebrity endorsements (Logan Paul, Kim Kardashian) and extreme sports sponsorships to target young consumers.
- **Four Loko (caffeinated alcohol) killed at least four teenagers and triggered an FDA ban on caffeine-alcohol blends** — in 2010, the FDA ordered reformulation after deaths linked to the product; mixing caffeine (a stimulant) with alcohol (a depressant) allows drinkers to stay awake and consume far more alcohol than they would otherwise, dramatically increasing risk of alcohol poisoning.
- **Teens consume twice the daily recommended caffeine in a single energy drink** — the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends zero caffeine for children under 12 and a maximum of 100 mg daily for teens; a single can of Monster (160 mg), Celsius, Alani Nu, or Prime (200 mg each) already exceeds safe limits, with hidden caffeine from guarana extract often not fully disclosed.
- **Energy drinks uniquely impair cardiovascular function in ways coffee does not** — studies show energy drinks reduce arterial blood flow, raise blood pressure for up to a week after consumption, make blood platelets stickier, and restrict oxygen delivery to the brain; the U.S. Navy prohibits pilots from consuming energy drinks 24 hours before flying due to reduced cerebral blood flow.
- **Multiple deaths have been linked to energy drinks in teens with underlying heart conditions or those taking stimulant medications** — Anise Fournier, 14, died of cardiac arrhythmia after two 24-ounce cans of Monster; a 15-year-old on ADHD medication (Ritalin or Adderall) died during a school ski trip after consuming Red Bull; thousands of U.S. teens visit emergency rooms annually with energy drink–related cardiac incidents.

**How Dietrich Mateschitz Invented the Energy Drink Category**

In the 1980s, toothpaste salesman Dietrich Mateschitz traveled to Hong Kong on business and read a *Newsweek* ranking of Japan's highest taxpayers. The top earner wasn't Toyota or Kawasaki, but Taisho Pharmaceuticals—a company made wealthy by an energy drink called Lipovitan. Intrigued by the profit margins, Mateschitz spent years tasting every energy drink he could find. He discovered a Thai product called Krateng Daing (Red Bull in English), created in the 1970s as a cheap, blue-collar drink for truck drivers and construction workers. The original was unappetizing—no carbonation, dull taste, unappealing bottle—but Mateschitz saw potential.

He approached the owner, a father-son duo, with a deal: "Let me take your product to Europe and I will sell it there. You will own 51 percent. I will own 49 percent." Mateschitz knew the European market would demand a glow-up. He added fizz, tweaked the flavor to match Austrian gummy bears, and—most critically—moved the drink into a tall, thin aluminum can. The design looked high quality and exclusive, unlike any drink on European shelves. When German officials demanded proof that Red Bull wasn't poison, Mateschitz abandoned the approval process and relocated to Austria, where the smaller nation proved more permissive.

**Building Cool Through Illegality and Club Culture**

Red Bull initially flopped in Austria. Almost nobody wanted it. Mateschitz personally drove around town distributing free cans. His breakthrough came when he hired employees to infiltrate clubs, ask if venues served Red Bull, and discreetly place cans in visible locations to create the impression that somebody, somewhere, was drinking it. Gradually, the strategy worked. Red Bull entered European club culture, where drinkers mixed it with alcohol—a combination the cans explicitly warned against, but the company tacitly encouraged through strategic non-enforcement of marketing messages.

Being illegal in neighboring Germany, then Europe's largest economy, paradoxically made Red Bull cooler. Bootleggers smuggled cans across the Austrian border to Munich clubs, where the product's forbidden status sparked rumors that it contained secret ingredients like bull testicle extract or amphetamines (both false; the taurine was synthetic). Once Germany legalized Red Bull in 1994, demand exploded so massively that factories couldn't produce enough cans to meet it.

**The "Red Bull Gives You Wings" Slogan and American Expansion**

Mateschitz hired his college friend Johann Kastner to devise a marketing slogan. After rejecting nearly 50 proposals, Kastner called Mateschitz at two in the morning with the phrase "Red Bull verleiht dir Flügel" (Red Bull gives you wings). The slogan became iconic. Red Bull was introduced to the American market in 1997, initially targeting college campuses with marketing teams driving Volkswagen Beetles and distributing free cans. The strategy wasn't immediately successful.

The breakthrough came when Red Bull partnered with Butter, an ironic dive bar in San Francisco that specialized in enormous mixed drinks with absurd names. Butter mixed Red Bull with vodka, calling it RV, and charged $8 per drink at a time when typical mixed drinks cost $2–3. It became one of the most expensive non-alcoholic bar mixers ever. The vodka-Red Bull combination exploded in popularity across American clubs by the early 2000s, perfectly timed for the dot-com boom and the emergence of extreme sports like snowboarding and windsurfing—sports the alcohol and tobacco industries hadn't yet monopolized.

**Red Bull as a Pure Marketing Enterprise**

Mateschitz's genius lay in recognizing that Red Bull is functionally useless as a beverage. It doesn't quench thirst, doesn't replace fluids (the caffeine causes dehydration), and offers no advantage over coffee or espresso. Austria's business registry lists Red Bull's stated corporate purpose not as selling an energy drink, but as "making marketing for the brand of Red Bull" to generate wealth for Mateschitz and his partners. The company manufactures nothing; it contracts all production, canning, and distribution to other firms. Red Bull spends virtually all revenue on marketing and brand positioning. As competitors aged out of the extreme-sports demographic, Red Bull pivoted to buying entire soccer teams and Formula One racing franchises—sports watched by older audiences—to maintain relevance across the lifespan of its core consumers.

**The Explosion of Competitors and the Four Loko Disaster**

Red Bull's success inspired countless imitators. Monster (launched 2002) captured the value market with larger cans, aggressive branding, and cheaper pricing. Celsius, Alani Nu, and Prime followed, with celebrities like Logan Paul and Kim Kardashian launching branded energy drinks. The U.S. energy drink market ballooned to $85 billion, with Americans consuming approximately 8 gallons per person annually.

Some competitors attempted to remove the inconvenience of mixing caffeine and alcohol separately. Four Loko, introduced in 2008, combined both in a single 23.5-ounce can—the equivalent of four beers plus a Red Bull plus an espresso shot, sold for under $3. The product was an immediate sensation, particularly among young drinkers. It also caused deaths. At least four teenagers died from alcohol poisoning after consuming Four Loko, and thousands visited emergency rooms with acute cardiac and neurological symptoms. Emergency room visits for energy drink consumption doubled from 10,000 to 20,000 in just four years.

The FDA determined in 2010 that adding caffeine to alcoholic beverages violated federal safety standards. The fundamental problem: alcohol is a depressant that naturally causes drowsiness and self-limiting behavior (passing out). Caffeine is a stimulant that blocks that natural safety valve. A drinker combining both can stay awake and alert while consuming vastly more alcohol than they normally would, dramatically increasing risk of alcohol poisoning, impaired judgment, drunk driving, and other harm. Four Loko was reformulated without caffeine and survives today as a standard sweet alcoholic beverage.

**Caffeine Content Far Exceeds Medical Recommendations for Teens**

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends zero caffeine for children under 12 and a maximum of 100 milligrams daily for teenagers. By comparison: a standard brewed cup of coffee contains about 100 mg of caffeine; a Starbucks tall coffee has roughly 100 mg; a soda has about 40 mg; green tea has about 30 mg; black tea and matcha have about 50 mg. A single can of Monster contains 160 mg of caffeine. A single can of Celsius, Alani Nu, or Prime contains 200 mg—twice the entire daily recommendation for teenagers in one drink. Many energy drinks also contain guarana, a Brazilian seed extract with naturally occurring caffeine, that is often not fully disclosed on packaging, pushing total caffeine intake even higher.

One-fifth of middle schoolers and nearly one-quarter of 12th graders consume energy drinks regularly. The problem is compounded because energy drinks' marketing specifically targets young people—Monster sponsors e-sports and professional gamers (whose audiences skew heavily toward children), and brands use flavor names and social media influencers to appeal to adolescents.

**Unique Cardiovascular Harms Beyond Caffeine**

Studies comparing energy drinks directly to coffee show markedly different health outcomes. John Higgins, a sports cardiologist, conducted research on 50 healthy young medical students (average age 24) who consumed a single energy drink. Within 90 minutes, the students showed significant reduction in vascular function—their arteries could not open properly. This matters because arterial flexibility in the arm predicts arterial health throughout the body, including in the heart. Other studies found that energy drinks reduce blood flow to the brain, prompting the U.S. Navy to prohibit pilots from consuming energy drinks 24 hours before flying.

Additional cardiovascular effects include sustained elevation in blood pressure (lasting up to one week after a single drink), increased platelet stickiness (making blood more clotted and "sludgy"), and elevated heart rate and irregular rhythms. The combination of reduced blood flow, thickened blood, elevated pressure, and increased heart rate forces the heart to work substantially harder. Emergency room cases document instances of blood clots in coronary arteries and heart attacks triggered by energy drink consumption.

These harms appear to stem from the combination of ingredients—not caffeine alone. Taurine (an amino acid), B vitamins, guarana, and other proprietary compounds interact in ways that differ sharply from the effects of coffee or tea.

**Deaths in Teens with Underlying Conditions**

Anise Fournier, 14, of Maryland, died after consuming two 24-ounce cans of Monster. The coroner determined her cause of death was cardiac arrhythmia. She had a mild underlying heart condition that had never caused problems in her life until she drank the energy drink. A 15-year-old on ADHD medication (Ritalin or Adderall) died during a school ski trip after a single can of Red Bull. The coroner's investigation concluded the combination of caffeine and his stimulant medication likely triggered the fatal cardiac event.

These deaths, while rare, represent the most visible tip of a much larger problem. Thousands of teenagers visit U.S. emergency rooms annually with energy drink–related symptoms—racing heartbeat, chest pain, collapse, arrhythmia—even without an underlying condition. Some of these adolescents have undiagnosed cardiac syndromes. Others are on medications (decongestants, asthma inhalers, pseudoephedrine) that interact dangerously with stimulants. Many simply pushed their healthy but developing cardiovascular systems beyond safe limits.

**Sleep Disruption and Mental Health Effects**

Caffeine disrupts sleep at all ages, but adolescents require more sleep than adults and are more sensitive to stimulants. Energy drink consumption creates a vicious cycle: the drink prevents sleep at night; the student feels exhausted in the morning; they consume another energy drink to function; sleep worsens; academic performance declines. Studies show that teenagers who consume energy drinks have lower GPAs than their peers—an effect driven not by the drink itself but by the sleep deprivation it causes.

Caffeine consumption is also linked to increased anxiety and depression in adolescents. Energy drinks have been identified as a marker for risky behavior—not necessarily a cause, but a correlate with higher rates of alcohol use, nicotine use, and other substance experimentation.

**Regulatory Approaches and Public Health Recommendations**

England banned the sale of energy drinks (those with more than 150 mg caffeine per liter) to anyone under 16, effective April 2027. Canada implemented a similar ban in Quebec. John Higgins and Karen Pasch, the two experts interviewed, both recommend a U.S. ban on sales to anyone under 18 (or at minimum 16), with legal teeth—meaning that purchasing energy drinks on behalf of minors carries penalties, as it does in Canada.

Both experts also call for warning labels explicitly stating that energy drinks can cause serious cardiovascular harm. Higgins advocates reclassifying energy drinks as drugs, triggering the FDA's safety and efficacy review process. This would require manufacturers to prove—rather than assume—that energy drinks are safe for their marketed populations.

Marketing restrictions are another priority. Higgins is campaigning to remove energy drink advertising from college sports broadcasts because millions of youth watch those events, normalizing consumption. Both experts note that exposure to marketing, especially in retail and social media, creates a perception that energy drink consumption is normal behavior everyone engages in.

**The Bottom Line**

No one physiologically needs an energy drink. If a person can safely consume caffeine, coffee or tea offers the same stimulant effect without the unique cardiovascular harms. For children who feel an afternoon energy slump, Karen Pasch's advice is straightforward: drink water. Proper hydration often resolves fatigue without any of the risks associated with energy drinks. Mateschitz's invention—a pure marketing vehicle selling an expensive, useless drink that happens to be dangerous to adolescents—represents a remarkable business success and a genuine public health problem.

# The Ezra Klein Show — "The Winning Democrat Who Wants to Break Up Political Parties"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Rob Sand (Iowa governor-elect; former state auditor and assistant attorney general; prosecutor of financial crimes and public corruption; champion of anti-party electoral reform).

## TL;DR

- **Rob Sand won Iowa governor by 11 points, running 24–30 points ahead of other Democrats** — in a state Democrats had lost almost entirely since 2006, Sand's core pitch was destroying the two-party system and ending what he calls "special clubs" that prioritize winning over problem-solving.
- **Sand traces corruption to the two-party duopoly, not primarily to money** — while Citizens United is "the worst Supreme Court decision," the real soil for corruption is a system where politicians only need to be "the lesser of two evils," eliminating pressure to actually solve problems or compete for votes.
- **Specific electoral reforms: single open primary + approval voting** — all candidates on one ballot advancing top 3–5 to general; voters then approve any candidates they support (not ranked choice). Sand credits this system for making politicians "act like normal people" and cooperate instead of demonize.
- **Iowa radon, water quality, and wetlands restoration as test cases** — Sand outlined how a less partisan system enables agreement on shared solutions: mandatory radon disclosure in home sales (reduces lung cancer without cost), massive wetlands restoration (filters water, creates outdoor recreation, retains young people).
- **Religious faith grounds Sand's anti-corruption work and politics** — inspired by Jesus flipping money changers' tables, Sand prosecuted "crimes against gratitude" and frames the two-party system as leading politicians "into temptation"; he explicitly rejects narrower definitions of corruption as only financial.
- **Singing "America the Beautiful" at 200 town halls broke partisan walls** — by having Republicans and Democrats applaud each other and sing together, then handing Q&A microphones to Republican audience members, Sand created visceral moments of unity-despite-disagreement that moved people to tears.
- **On divisive issues like abortion, agreement-building comes first** — rather than forcing compromise on values-based disagreements, Sand prioritizes building trust and demonstrating results on shared priorities, then leveraging that trust to find reasonable common ground on harder issues.
- **Sand will invite Republicans into his administration and to all bill-signing ceremonies** — he intends to hold 100 town halls per year as governor, same as auditor, and explicitly pressure GOP legislators in his districts (11 state senators won his districts) to pass his campaign agenda by reminding them of his landslide mandate.

**The Through-Line: Public Corruption and Lost Trust in Institutions**

Sand's career animating principle is public corruption—both the financial kind (embezzlement, tax fraud) and the structural kind (failure to serve the public trust). He prosecuted the Iowa film office tax credit scandal in the late 2000s, where legislators created unlimited refundable transferable tax credits so riddled with loopholes that someone could buy a 747, use it in a single film shot, sell it for 95 cents on the dollar, then capture 50% of the purchase price in tax credits and resell those credits at 88 cents on the dollar. Sand remembers an exhibit from trial: a shovel rented for $250 for six weeks. The scandal was not what was illegal—it was what was legal, written into statute by people with no accountability.

Sand frames this as a "crime against gratitude": a person with a secure government job and income, stealing or exploiting public resources for personal enrichment, violating not just fiscal law but the fundamental compact of public service. He views the two-party system through the same lens. The structure allows politicians and candidates to escape real consequences for inaction or laziness; they only need to convince voters they are the lesser of two evils. This creates systemic corruption—not primarily bribery, but the corruption of purpose. Politicians stop trying to solve problems and start trying to win the next election immediately after the current one, creating a grinding cycle of partisan destruction.

**Why the Two-Party Duopoly, Not Just Money**

Ezra Klein pressed Sand: If the core generator of corruption is money—the need to raise $58 million for a gubernatorial race—why center the two-party system? Sand acknowledged Citizens United was catastrophic but argued the party duopoly is the deeper problem. Money matters, but a two-choice system allows both corruption and laziness simultaneously. If voters have only two options, both parties can tell voters the other is worse; neither has to actually deliver. By contrast, introducing competition—even within the same party—forces candidates and officeholders to earn re-election by solving problems, not just smearing opponents.

Sand cited Michael Porter and Catherine Gallon's *The Politics Industry*, which asks: We love markets; we know duopolies don't work in business. So why do we accept them in politics? In Iowa specifically, independents—about a third of voters—cannot vote in primaries without signing a sheet joining a "private club," despite the fact that taxes pay for the election. Sand sees this as un-American and corrupting.

**Electoral Architecture: Open Primaries and Approval Voting**

The core reform Sand champions is a single open primary (all candidates on one ballot, independents included) with top 3–5 advancing to a general election using approval voting (voters approve any candidates they support; highest approval totals win). This inverts the incentive structure. In a traditional primary, candidates appeal to the party base and demonize opponents. In a general election, they attack the other party's nominee. Approval voting changes the math: if you demonize an opponent and their voters won't vote for you, you lose their approval. Sand walked audiences through it: If I'm running against Susan and Ezra, and I insult Ezra, his supporters won't approve of me—even if they also approve of Susan. So I'll be nice, respectful, and honest. People in town halls would shout back exactly that realization.

Sand noted that Alaska's experience shows candidates adapt. Sarah Palin's campaign behavior in Alaska's first election under their new system (top-four primary, ranked choice general) differed sharply from her second campaign once she understood the incentives had changed.

**Governance-Level Outcomes: Radon, Water, and Wetlands**

Sand walked Klein through three concrete examples of how a less partisan system enables problem-solving. Iowa has the highest radon levels in the country; radon is the second-leading cause of lung cancer after smoking. A recent bill mandated radon mitigation in new construction—useless to the vast majority who will never live in a new house. Sand's alternative: require home inspectors and realtors to have clients initial attestations that they know radon causes lung cancer and must choose yes-or-no on radon testing and $2,000 mitigation systems. No cost to taxpayers, just friction that forces conscious decision-making. Sand believes the vast majority of Iowans would choose safety.

Water quality: Iowa has high nitrate levels (cancer risk). Sand proposes the largest wetlands restoration project ever undertaken. Wetlands filter water, reduce cancer risk, provide outdoor recreation (field trips, hunting, photography, boating—"four and a half F's"), and help retain young people leaving Iowa for states with better outdoor opportunities. Klein noted the critique: a Republican might say this is a corporate power problem and a GOP problem. Sand reframed: the real problem is that the system is designed to reelect politicians rather than solve problems. Iowa has a reform tradition—the first state to create an independent redistricting commission (still the gold standard), and a unique judicial selection process requiring public interviews of candidates before governors appoint them.

**Faith, Corruption, and the Sermon on the Mount**

Sand's work is animated by Christian faith. He cited Jesus flipping money changers' tables in the temple—anger at people in positions of trust abusing power for self-enrichment. He frames his prosecution work as rooted in that image: crimes against gratitude, crimes against the public trust and self-governance. When asked about the return of a religious left in Democratic politics (pointing to Rashida Tlaib, Adam Hamilton, Abdul El-Sayed), Sand expressed joy. He sees most Christians haven't been represented by recent political Christianity—the voice of Matthew 25 (treating others as you'd want to be treated) and the Sermon on the Mount are absent. He wants to bring that back.

Sand also treats faith as a window into who he is as a person. Voters don't just want to know his policy positions; they want to know who he is across a thousand unforeseen issues. His faith is central to that identity. He contrasted openness about faith—learning from a Sikh wedding, finding a MAGA supporter appreciating Sikhism through exposure—with political tribalism.

**Breaking Partisan Walls Through Song and Shared Microphones**

Sand held 200 town halls in his gubernatorial campaign. At each, after outlining the broken two-party system, he pivoted to action. He asked Republicans, independents, and Democrats to raise their hands, then had the room applaud each group—literally asking them to love their neighbors who disagreed. Then he led the first verse of "America the Beautiful." He sang this at every county in Iowa twice. People cried, he said, because "it was like it was the first time they had invited [Democrats, Republicans, independents] not to pretend that they agree, not to pretend that we are one, but to acknowledge that we are different and then unite." A friend told Sand mid-campaign that singing causes brainwave synchronization (delta waves, closest to deep sleep), which explains why people report feeling profoundly connected after singing together.

For Q&A, Sand handed the microphone to a registered Republican in the audience—sometimes they asked softball questions, sometimes hardball ones. Klein asked if it ever backfired. Sand said no, because the action itself—a Democrat visibly trusting a Republican with the microphone—communicates more than any words could. He ended his victory speech by saying every bill the Republican legislature sends him will have a bill-signing ceremony to which all supporters are invited. He called it "common sense."

**On Divisive Issues: Agreement First, Then Trust-Building**

Klein pressed Sand on abortion. Iowa has one of the strictest bans in the country (six weeks). These are not technocratic problems with positive-sum solutions; people genuinely disagree on values. How does Sand's framework apply? Sand argued that if politicians are incentivized to work together and find practical solutions in places of agreement, they will discover where the vast majority actually agrees and can make progress. The goal is for elected officials to earn re-election by producing results on shared priorities, then leverage that trust to find reasonable common ground on divisive issues. This assumes good-faith actors—which is why he emphasizes changing the system to remove temptation toward bad faith.

Sand also compared this to marriage. You could pick a fight about one of four things you know will start an argument, but if you do, you poison everything that comes after (dinner plans, etc.). Better to cooperate on what you agree on, build trust, and approach disagreements from a place of mutual respect.

**Strategy for Governing with a Republican Legislature**

Sand faces a Republican state legislature (he has no veto power and no referendum option in Iowa). Eleven GOP state senators are in districts he won 10 days ago. Klein asked how he navigates this if Republicans simply refuse his agenda. Sand replied he will hold 100 town halls per year—including in their districts—reminding their constituents that he won by 11 points with an unprecedented mandate, got more votes than any Iowa gubernatorial candidate except one since WWII (1964), and ran an explicitly anti-partisan, problem-solving campaign. He raised $58 million with an average donor gift of $150 and 3,000 Republican donors. He'll tell GOP senators: adopt my entire agenda now so you have different ground to fight on in 2028. If you refuse, I'll keep campaigning against inaction in your districts.

He also said Republicans haven't moved to strip the governor's office of power during a special session, which he credited as good-faith willingness to work. Klein noted that Obama ran into a different reality with Mitch McConnell's stated goal of making him a one-term president. Sand acknowledged the difference but emphasized that his entire campaign message was predicated on willingness to work with Republicans—a signal that he means it.

**A Return to Obama's "One America" Vision**

Klein and Sand both noted that Obama's 2004 DNC speech and 2008 campaign centered on transcending red-America/blue-America divisions. But the lesson many Democrats drew from his presidency was the opposite: the country really is deeply divided; Mitch McConnell will block everything. That sense that Americans are united more than divided became seen as naïve. Sand represents a return to that vision but with structural reforms attached. He believes Americans are more united by what they share than divided by differences, but that belief requires a system that surfaces agreement rather than encourages division. Without changed incentives, rhetoric alone fails.

**Book Recommendations**

Sand's wife Christine recommended two: *Tinkers* by Paul Harding (fiction) and Doris Kearns Goodwin's *Team of Rivals* (classic about Lincoln's cabinet of rivals—reinforcing Sand's theme of bipartisan leadership). Sand added the four gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John), not asking people to read the whole book but suggesting that readers would be surprised to see what Jesus actually said about love, service, and care for the vulnerable—so different from how Christianity is often portrayed in media and politics.

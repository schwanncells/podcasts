# The Indicator from Planet Money — "So ... how long until these oil prices get REALLY bad"

**Host:** Waylon Wong, Darian Woods. **Guest:** Carl Larry (Inveris; provides energy market analysis and advice to major oil companies including BP, Shell, and Exxon).

## TL;DR

- **Analysts predicted $200 oil but current price hovers near $100** — Despite the U.S.-Israel war in Iran stretching into its third month, oil remains significantly below worst-case forecasts made a few months ago.
- **U.S. fracking exports cushion the market** — America's shale revolution shifted the global oil market; the U.S. now exports oil instead of relying entirely on Middle East imports via the Strait of Hormuz.
- **Strategic reserve drawdowns are providing relief** — The U.S. and possibly China are tapping strategic oil reserves, temporarily suppressing prices but creating an unsustainable buffer that cannot last forever.
- **Multiple supply sources are coming online** — Venezuela exports hit 550,000 barrels per day (highest since January 2019), Canada is ramping production, and Russia has eased sanctions—together offsetting Strait of Hormuz disruptions.
- **Demand is dropping across multiple vectors** — Oil consumption fell globally due to refinery buying less, drivers choosing alternatives, and outright rationing in countries like Sri Lanka and Myanmar; China's drop is mysteriously large and unexplained.
- **The breaking point arrives by end-of-year if war continues** — Carl Larry warns that if the conflict persists and reserves deplete further, the system could face a "catastrophic" financial crisis; the International Energy Agency reports only weeks of commercial inventory left.
- **Historical precedent: $147 oil in 2007–2008** — Oil has spiked higher before; the 2008 peak differs from today partly because the U.S. had no fracking capacity then and China was driving massive demand.

**Why Oil Prices Haven't Exploded Despite War**

Three months into the U.S.-Israel war in Iran, analysts are puzzled. A couple of months ago, some warned oil could spike to $200 per barrel if Middle East conflict continued through spring. Instead, oil hovers around $100. Carl Larry from Inveris attributes the surprisingly stable pricing to several structural changes in global energy markets since the previous major oil crisis.

**The Fracking Revolution Changed Everything**

The first major reason prices aren't higher is the U.S. shale revolution. The fracking boom transformed America from an oil importer holding hostage to the Strait of Hormuz into an exporter. The world is no longer totally dependent on that chokepoint. However, there is a caveat: American frackers have been slow to increase production since the war started. The industry was burned by the COVID pandemic oil collapse, and many wells are only profitable at sustained higher prices. Producers are cautiously increasing output, accepting that "this might be higher for longer," but incremental rather than aggressive expansion is happening.

**Strategic Reserve Releases and New Supply Sources**

Along with sluggish U.S. production growth, the U.S. and other countries are drawing down strategic petroleum reserves—a second major supply source cushioning prices. Simultaneously, multiple other producers are ramping up: Venezuelan crude exports reached 550,000 barrels per day last week, the highest level since January 2019. Nigeria and Saudi Arabia are increasing exports via pipeline, and easing Russian oil sanctions are bringing additional supply to market. These coordinated supply increases have taken the edge off any price shock from Strait of Hormuz blockades.

**Demand Is Falling Too**

Economic theory predicts that as prices rise, demand falls. Oil consumption dropped worldwide due to a combination of factors: refineries buying less, consumers choosing to drive less, and explicit rationing in countries like Sri Lanka, Myanmar, and Slovenia. China's imports fell dramatically—so huge that it has puzzled analysts. The drop is larger than what solar panels and electric vehicles alone would explain. One theory is that China may be drawing down massive underground strategic reserves, though satellite imagery has not confirmed large visible drawdowns. The Chinese government keeps such information tightly restricted, so the reason remains a mystery.

**The Unsustainable Buffer and the Breaking Point**

If the world has been spared even higher gasoline prices partly because of strategic reserve releases, that reprieve cannot last indefinitely. Carl Larry issues a stark warning: another large strategic reserve release would be "very, very unnerving" because reserves are dwindling toward limits. By the end of the year, if the conflict persists in the same situation, it would be "catastrophic financially." The International Energy Agency head underscored the urgency this week, telling G7 finance ministers that only several weeks of commercial inventories remain and that financial markets are underestimating how fast reserves are depleting. Other analysts debate whether the system can adjust supply and demand enough to avoid this breaking point.

**Historical Context and Precedent**

Oil has climbed higher before. In 2007–2008, oil prices reached $147 per barrel—higher than today. The difference was that China was driving surging demand back then, and the U.S. had not yet scaled up fracking. So while current prices feel unprecedented to some, they have historical precedent. The current spike feels particularly worrying partly because the conflict's endpoint remains unknown. However, the broader lesson is that oil prices spike frequently, and this one—though serious—may not be uniquely dire if the geopolitical situation stabilizes.

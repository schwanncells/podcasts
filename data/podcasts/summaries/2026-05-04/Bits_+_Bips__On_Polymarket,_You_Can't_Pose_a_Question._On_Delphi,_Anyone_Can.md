# Bits + Bips — "On Polymarket, You Can't Pose a Question. On Delphi, Anyone Can"

**Host:** Austin Campbell (Zero Knowledge Group). **Guest:** Jensen (co-founder/CEO, Delphi; building decentralized information markets on-chain).

## TL;DR

- **Polymarket and Calshi cannot accept user-created markets** — only the platforms themselves can create markets, making them "prediction markets" rather than true "information markets"; users can suggest but not pose questions.
- **Delphi is bidirectional** — any user can create a market (pose a question), turning prediction markets into a "new UX over financial markets" accessible to small actors who can't afford traditional financial hedging instruments.
- **The orange farmer analogy** — a small California farmer could hedge drought risk via a Delphi information market ("will it rain this month?") the same way large corporations hedge via options baskets, but at a cost accessible to individuals.
- **Machine learning will dominate information market trading** — Jensen predicts human participation will quickly give way to ML models that scrape data faster, reach certainty sooner, and trade within milliseconds of a market being posted.
- **Delphi is Uniswap to Polymarket's Coinbase** — underlying contracts are on-chain and permissionless; the front end is regulated and filtered, but the base protocol is censorship-resistant technology, not a centralized gatekeeper.
- **Regulation at the application layer, not the protocol layer** — Jensen argues misuse should be handled via existing laws applied to front-end applications, the same way illegal content is regulated on the web without banning encryption.

---

**Prediction Markets vs. Information Markets**

Jensen draws a sharp distinction between "prediction markets" like Polymarket and Calshi, and "information markets" like Delphi. On Polymarket, users cannot create markets — only the platform can, though users may suggest them. This means users can only answer questions, never pose them. Delphi inverts this: any user can create a market by posting a question and seeding it with initial liquidity. Jensen sees this bidirectionality as the core architectural difference that determines whether a platform can achieve meaningful scale.

The practical consequence, Jensen argues, is that Polymarket and Calshi will perpetually fight over the same high-volume flagship markets — presidential elections, sports outcomes — because those are the markets that attract liquidity when a central curator picks the questions. Delphi targets the long tail: markets that don't exist yet because no one at a centralized platform decided to create them.

**Information Markets as a UX Layer Over Financial Markets**

Jensen's thesis is that information markets, at sufficient scale, become a "new UX over the financial markets" rather than an alternative to them. The mechanism: as liquidity in a given information market deepens, traditional financial actors (brokers, market makers) become incentivized to bring their instruments into that market as counterparty trades. The farmer who posts "will it rain in California this month?" eventually stops needing to seek out a broker — the options come to the market.

He uses a small-scale orange farmer as a running example. A large agricultural company can hedge drought risk by commissioning a custom options basket from a broker; a small farmer cannot. An information market over the same underlying risk gives the small farmer access to the economic function of hedging at a cost structure that makes sense. Jensen frames this as democratization of financial market access, not a replacement of those markets.

**Machine Learning and the Endgame**

Jensen expects the long-run equilibrium of information markets to be dominated by machine learning models rather than human traders. A human who sees an open market might spend a day aggregating meteorological data before placing a trade. An ML model scrapes the same sources in milliseconds, arrives at a high-certainty position, and trades instantly. Jensen sees the arc moving from "days or hours of human research → overnight trade" to "milliseconds from market creation → instant resolution," essentially becoming high-frequency trading of information rather than pure financial instruments. He views this not as a threat but as the design intention — machines are the most efficient information aggregators and price setters.

**Governance and Abuse Prevention**

The host pushes on misuse scenarios — specifically, markets that could expose sensitive information like a politician's child's location or a dignitary's travel itinerary. Jensen's answer is that regulation should operate at the application layer, not the protocol layer. Delphi's underlying on-chain contracts are analogous to encryption: too broadly useful to regulate at the infrastructure level. The front end Delphi operates itself applies abuse filters that automatically delist inappropriate markets. Third-party front ends built on Delphi's contracts are subject to existing laws, the same way a website distributing illegal content can be shut down without banning the underlying web protocols.

He draws the explicit analogy: Delphi is to Polymarket what Uniswap is to Coinbase. Coinbase lists what it chooses and bears responsibility for those listings. Uniswap's pools are permissionlessly created; the protocol doesn't curate. Delphi takes the Uniswap position — censorship-resistant at the contract level, regulated at the application level.

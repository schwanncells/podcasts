# Code Switch — "DACA recipients are trapped in Trump's limbo"

**Host:** Gene Demby. **Guests:** Ximena Bustillo (NPR immigration correspondent; covers Department of Homeland Security and immigration policy).

## TL;DR

- **DACA recipients now average 31 years old** — roughly half are between 31 and 44, not the high school students the program was originally framed around; the first cohort eligible in 2012 is now approaching or past age 40.
- **Processing delays are trapping DACA renewals in limbo** — work permits expire on fixed dates with no grace period; when USCIS processing slows, people become undocumented overnight with no legal ability to work or get protection from deportation.
- **Trump administration using policy tools, not rescission** — they cannot legally rescind DACA (courts blocked the first attempt), but they've stripped health care access, launched investigations into colleges aiding DACA recipients, told people they should self-deport, and increased deportations of DACA holders.
- **DACA recipients are now facing deportations and detentions** — some individuals have been arrested, detained, and deported; federal judges have ordered some deported individuals returned, but the government has not always complied.
- **500,000+ people live with permanent uncertainty** — DACA recipients have jobs, houses, mortgages, kids, and degrees, but face the constant threat of losing everything if their renewal lapses or the program weakens further.
- **Refugee admissions reshaped toward white South Africans** — the Trump administration increased slots from 7,500 to 17,500 but filled them almost entirely with Afrikaners; only three non-Afrikaner refugees admitted so far this fiscal year.
- **DACA has become mythical to younger generations** — the program is essentially closed to new applicants; people under 25 often don't know what it is, making it harder to sustain political support for immigration reform.
- **DACA recipients feel betrayed by the government's shifting promises** — people brought documents in good faith under Obama-era assurances that the data would not be used against them; the Trump administration is not respecting that promise and is using data to target people.

**The USCIS Crackdown and Policy Changes**

Gene Demby and Ximena Bustillo explain that the Trump administration has not legally rescinded DACA—courts prevented that in the first term, and there was significant public backlash—but has tightened access through policy. USCIS issued a May memo giving officers discretion to deny green cards to people who did not return to their home country to apply, even though the underlying law has not changed. The administration has also paused processing for applications from people born in 39 countries under the travel ban, re-vetted refugees already admitted, and stripped health care marketplace access that DACA recipients previously had under Biden. There were also public investigations into colleges providing financial aid to DACA recipients and an explicit messaging campaign telling people without legal status to self-deport.

**DACA Processing Delays and the Lapse Trap**

A critical difference between DACA and other immigration categories is that work permits expire on exact dates and require renewal every two years. When USCIS processing slows, DACA recipients can become completely undocumented on their permit's expiration date if their renewal has not been approved—not deferred, but stripped of all legal status. This is not a gradual process; it is an overnight shift from legal to undocumented. Bustillo visited Arizona and spoke with people at various stages of renewals: some who have already experienced lapses and gotten renewed, some currently in lapses unable to work, and others like Reina Montoya and Jose Patino whose renewals are not due until early 2027 but who are already nervously monitoring the calendar.

**Who DACA Recipients Are Now**

When DACA launched in 2012, it reached people in high school and college. Now, almost 15 years later, the average DACA recipient is 31; more than half are between 31 and 44. These are not "kids" or "dreamers" in the sense of young people with futures ahead—they are middle-aged adults with established lives. Many graduated college before DACA existed (and were legally undocumented despite being old enough to drink). They have jobs, some run nonprofit organizations, they have kids who are teenagers, aging parents they care for, mortgages, and houses. The shift is significant: going from legal ability to do those things to complete loss of legal status in one day is extremely sudden.

Reina Montoya, 35, and Jose Patino, 37, both run Aliento, an immigrant youth advocacy organization in Arizona. They have been involved in immigration advocacy for years and remember the original DACA announcement in June 2012. Reina had just graduated from Arizona State University and was discouraged about her future; she and others had been pushing President Obama to do something after the DREAM Act failed in 2010. Jose was 21 at the time and had been advocating for a program like DACA for years. When Obama announced the policy, it was bittersweet—it had limitations and many people in the community didn't qualify—but for those who did, including both of them, it was transformative.

Obama was clear at the time that DACA was not amnesty, not immunity, not a path to citizenship, and not a permanent fix; it was a temporary stopgap to give Congress time to find a permanent solution. But that permanent solution never came. In the past, DACA recipients had bipartisan support and operated in a "protected bubble" where they were not proactively targeted or harassed. That has changed under the Trump administration.

**Shifting Rhetoric and Enforcement**

The Trump administration justifies the crackdown on national security grounds, claiming prior vetting was insufficient and that USCIS needs more time to review applications. This rationale appears across DACA processing delays, the pause on travel-ban countries, and refugee re-vetting. However, the administration has also repeatedly signaled that it believes the country should be a country for white people and has taken a hardline anti-immigrant stance. The USCIS mission statement no longer includes the phrase "nation of immigrants"—it now emphasizes "protecting American workers" and states "America is not a nation of immigrants. It is a nation of citizens."

The refugee system illustrates the direction: the administration increased refugee slots from 7,500 to 17,500 but allocated them almost entirely to white South Africans known as Afrikaners. So far this fiscal year, only three refugees admitted were not white Afrikaners.

**The Emotional and Professional Toll**

For Jose and Reina at age 37 and 35, the uncertainty carries new weight. Jose leads a nonprofit; his employees depend on his ability to work and generate revenue for them to feed their families. He explicitly worries about the day his DACA status lapses and he can no longer do his job or be compensated. He does not want to be a 40-year-old undocumented person with a master's degree and professional accomplishments.

The fact that Trump was elected twice—the second time with a popular vote and significant support from Latino voters, including some of Reina's own family members who benefited from DACA or adjusted their status—is deeply painful. Reina tried to warn her relatives that voting for him could affect her and others in the family, but they voted for him anyway. At some point, a country can tell a person so many different ways that it does not want them there. Reina has increasingly thought about leaving the United States for countries like Canada or Spain that might hire her in other professional fields. But she is aware that as she ages—if she is 55 and undocumented—she will be far less desirable to another country seeking to bring her in legally.

**Living with Persistent Uncertainty**

Bustillo found that DACA recipients have varying emotional responses to the uncertainty. Some describe it as a baseline level of anxiety they have always lived with; they have other things—jobs, kids—so the worry is there but not paralyzing. Others are more panicked. The level of awareness about the instability has worsened in 2026 as lapses have increased and the administration has utilized every tool at its disposal: delays, detentions, arrests, and court challenges to ensure the program becomes weaker.

A central betrayal for many is that the Obama administration promised that people who came forward in good faith, providing all their documents, would not have that information used against them. The Trump administration is not respecting that promise; it is actively using the data to target people. Reina notes that it is still the U.S. government making promises and breaking them, and the administration has made clear that previous promises no longer matter.

**The Myth of DACA and Hope**

The word "dreamer" was originally used to refer to young people brought here as children who dreamed of becoming astronauts, teachers, or scientists—the hope was advocacy for their future. When asked whether the dream had been achieved, many DACA recipients said yes, but the dream doesn't end. There are new dreams: falling in love, getting married, finishing a PhD, reaching the next stage of life, or simply maintaining the stability and independence already achieved (like living in one's own apartment downtown).

At the same time, almost all DACA recipients expressed that while they achieved many things because of DACA, they now live with the constant fear that it could all be taken away. Many said they almost regretted building their lives on DACA's foundation because the fall feels harder when it collapses. DACA provides both tremendous opportunity and an invisible anchor that pulls people down when the status is revoked.

DACA has become a mythical program to younger generations. The program is essentially closed to new applicants (eligible applicants had to enter before 2007); people under 25 often don't know what it is. Teachers and counselors are shocked when Reina and Jose explain that there are no DACA recipients in high school. This makes it harder to sustain political will for immigration reform because the constituency most helped by DACA—the "kids" originally framed by the program—is no longer entering it.

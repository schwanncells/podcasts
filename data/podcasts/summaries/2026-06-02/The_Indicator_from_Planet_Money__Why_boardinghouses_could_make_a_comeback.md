# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Why boardinghouses could make a comeback"

**Host:** Waylon Wong and Darian Woods. **Guest:** Rebecca Baird Rembar (housing researcher and journalist; reported on New York housing for more than a decade; studied single room occupancy housing).

## TL;DR

- **In the 1950s, New York City had more than 200,000 SRO (single room occupancy) units, accounting for more than 10% of the rental housing stock** — these boarding houses were a critical rung on the housing ladder for workers and migrants fleeing rural areas and newly arrived immigrants.
- **A million SRO rooms were eliminated or converted between the 1970s and today, primarily driven by urban renewal policy** — cities wrote increasingly stringent housing regulations and gave landlords incentives to convert buildings, motivated by a mix of genuine safety concerns and classism/racism.
- **About half of men entering homeless shelters in New York City in the 1980s said they had previously lived in SROs** — the loss of cheap boarding houses coincided with rising homelessness, though other factors like deinstitutionalization of psychiatric facilities also contributed.
- **If SRO construction had grown at the same pace as other housing, there would be 2.5 million more rooms today** — far above the current homeless population, suggesting the housing shortage is partly a self-inflicted policy wound.
- **Recent moves to bring SROs back are "fairly piecemeal and ineffective," but some momentum is building** — Washington state and Oregon have passed zoning changes to legalize new SROs; New York's mayor released a housing plan in May promising legislation to allow more shared housing.
- **SROs are challenging environments for aging but work as a transitional housing option for younger people** — Paul Freitag (Westside Federation for Senior and Supportive Housing) supports them for people getting established, though they come with drawbacks like disease transmission and limited space for medical equipment.

**From Common Housing to Effective Ban**

In the late 19th century, as rural Americans flocked to cities and immigration rose, landlords began converting warehouses, commercial buildings, and large apartments into boarding houses. These ranged from high-end long-term hotel stays to bare-bones cubicles with chicken wire dividers to prevent theft. The cheapest rooms cost 5 to 10 cents a night—roughly $100 a month in today's dollars even accounting for inflation. By the 1950s, New York had more than 200,000 SRO units, and they were equally common in Chicago and San Francisco. Single room occupancy buildings offered a foothold for people entering cities: workers, elderly residents, and people living independently within a structured environment.

By the 1950s and 1960s, however, many SRO buildings had fallen into disrepair and poor maintenance. There was a widespread sense that the buildings themselves were causing disease, theft, and violence. Under the guise of urban renewal, cities took action. Landlords received incentives to convert buildings to other uses. Housing regulators wrote increasingly stringent codes around sunlight, heating, fire safety, and minimum unit sizes. Some of this regulation reflected legitimate safety concerns. Some reflected "not in my backyard" sentiment and deeper issues of classism and racism.

**The Destruction of Boarding Houses and Rising Homelessness**

The 1970s saw the most aggressive wave of SRO destruction. A million rooms were eliminated or converted during this period. The International Hotel in San Francisco became a symbol of the era: in 1977, the sheriff and hundreds of riot police evicted over 100 tenants at 3 a.m., sparking protests from more than 2,000 people trying to stop the evictions. The building housed mostly elderly Filipino and Chinese residents. The evictions at the International Hotel prompted a congressional report months later, which documented how SRO closures had contributed to a rise in homelessness. Around the same time, psychiatric deinstitutionalization—the federal government's shift of mental health responsibility to state and local levels—further strained housing availability for vulnerable populations.

Baird Rembar points to this period as a turning point for American homelessness. About half of men entering homeless shelters in New York City in the 1980s reported they had previously lived in SROs. Policy makers eventually began asking what urban renewal had done wrong. The answer was clear: by encouraging the destruction of extremely cheap, independent housing, they had eliminated a crucial safety net that the shelter system could not replace.

**Current Efforts and the Numbers**

Baird Rembar's research quantifies the loss. If SRO construction had grown at the same pace as other U.S. housing since the 1950s, there would be 2.5 million more rooms available today—well above the current homeless population. This suggests that the housing shortage is partly a policy-induced shortage.

Recent efforts to legalize SROs have been sparse. Zoning changes in Washington state and Oregon represent the strongest moves so far. In May 2026, New York's Mayor Zohran Mamdani released a housing plan that promised legislation to bring back more shared housing, signaling renewed attention to the issue.

**Trade-offs and Future Role**

Paul Freitag of the Westside Federation emphasizes that SROs are challenging environments for aging residents. As people age, they need medical equipment, special beds, walkers, home health aides, and struggle with incontinence—all difficult to accommodate in a dense shared-bathroom setting. Residents in shared housing are also more prone to communicable diseases, a problem Freitag says the organization witnessed acutely during COVID and annually with the flu.

Despite these limitations, Freitag sees value in SROs for younger, shorter-term residents. They can be built inexpensively and offer people a place to stay while they're getting established in a new city. For Vera Hill, a 77-year-old former Mount Sinai Health System worker who fell into homelessness in her 60s when rent became unaffordable, the experience is positive. Living at Euclid Hall—an SRO run by the Westside Federation—gives her safety, community ("the staff here is amazing"), and meaningful work helping other residents, even though she has to share a bathroom and lacks a private kitchen.

# Business History — "Can the U.S. Go 'Cashless?'"

**Host:** Jacob Goldstein (Business History). **Guests:** Katherine Ann Edwards (economist, Optimist Economy); Robin Rousey (editor, Optimist Economy).

## TL;DR

- **80% of Americans still carry cash despite pandemic shift** — cash usage dropped during COVID but has stabilized; 70% of people under 50 believe the U.S. will go cashless in their lifetime, but current data suggests otherwise.
- **6% of adults are unbanked; 14.2% of households are underbanked** — people excluded from traditional banking are forced into predatory alternative financial services (payday lenders, check cashers, pawn brokers) that charge far higher fees.
- **Black and Latino borrowers face systematic discrimination** — Federal Reserve data shows disparate denial rates for credit applications between Black and white households even within high-income brackets ($100,000+); PPP loan testing found Black callers received worse advice than white callers for identical businesses.
- **Banks extract wealth through fees and transaction reordering** — $12–$35 monthly account fees, overdraft penalties multiplied through deliberate transaction reordering, and usurious rates on alternative products disproportionately harm low-income households.
- **Nine states ban cashless businesses; Payment Choice Act of 2025 would require cash acceptance up to $500** — bipartisan support reflects concerns about financial privacy, government surveillance, and preventing forced dependence on banks that have demonstrated predatory practices.
- **Privacy and control concerns drive cash demand** — Cleveland Federal Reserve focus groups revealed deep mistrust of banks among both unbanked and underbanked populations; people fear surveillance of their spending by banks and government.
- **Postal banking (France, Germany, Japan) offers a public alternative** — U.S. had postal savings system for 50 years; reviving it could provide small-dollar deposits, check cashing, and fee-free services without credit products, serving people excluded from private banking.
- **Solution requires public financial services, not private bank monopoly** — private banks need not serve everyone, but government should provide basic banking infrastructure to prevent full financial exclusion and predatory dependency.

**Who Still Uses Cash**

Edwards opens with surprising data: despite the pandemic's shift to digital payments, cash usage has actually stabilized at meaningful levels. About 80% of Americans still carry cash. Moreover, cash hoarding—people keeping large amounts at home—has *increased* since the pandemic. Yet survey data from Gallup shows 70% of people under 50 believe the U.S. will become cashless in their lifetime. This disconnect between perception and reality frames the episode's central question.

**The Unbanked and Underbanked Crisis**

According to the Federal Reserve's Survey of Household Economics and Decision Making, 6% of adults are entirely unbanked—excluded from the banking system entirely. This number has remained stable over the past decade. A separate FDIC survey measures it by household and finds 4.2% completely unbanked and 14.2% underbanked, meaning they have a bank account but rely on alternative financial services. Edwards emphasizes that underbanked is roughly three times more common than unbanked. The correlation is stark: households earning under $25,000 annually have a 22% unbanked rate; those under $50,000 still see 8% unbanked rates.

Rousey explains that ChexSystems functions as a parallel credit bureau specifically for banking history, where people can be "blacklisted" from opening accounts—a system many people don't realize exists separately from Equifax or Transunion.

**Racial Discrimination in Banking**

Edwards presents Federal Reserve data showing that Black and Latino households face systematic disparities in credit access. When comparing borrowers with similar incomes, Black households earning over $100,000 have significantly higher credit denial rates than white households in the same income bracket. This disparity persists even without direct human interaction in modern account-opening processes.

A striking case study: the National Community Reinvestment Coalition conducted paired testing during the 2020 Paycheck Protection Program rollout. They had scripted callers—half Black, half white, with identically sized businesses and identical financial profiles—contact banks asking for PPP loans. Banks gave the Black callers consistently worse advice: smaller loans, suggestions to pursue home equity lines of credit instead of business loans, and overall inferior guidance. This occurred even though the government was essentially handing out the money through the banks.

**Bank Fees and Predatory Practices**

Edwards shares personal stories illustrating systemic extraction. In 2005, she deposited a paycheck on a Friday before a July 4th holiday weekend, then made small purchases over three days ($2–$10 each). The bank deliberately reordered transactions—processing all her purchases *before* the deposit—so each transaction incurred a $35 overdraft fee. She was charged $35 per transaction across roughly 20 purchases, turning what should have been a 500-dollar deposit into a $600+ debt. The bank, pressed on the phone, only reversed the fees as a "one-time exception."

A second story: Edwards closed a bank account before moving to Russia but a charge hit two days later. After she left the country, the bank charged overdrafts, then interest, then compounded interest—ultimately sending it to collection. The original charge was $1.95 for a Dr. Pepper. It took her parents' intervention to settle for the original overdraft amount.

Rousey notes that average monthly fees for bank checking accounts now exceed $12, and overdraft fees routinely hit $35. These may seem small to wealthier people, but for someone depositing $500, $144 in annual fees represents a 29% fee rate.

**The Usury Problem and Executive Orders**

Edwards introduces the concept of "ex post facto" usury—charging fees after the fact that constitute usurious effective interest rates. While most states have usury regulations to cap interest rates and combat payday lending, the fee-and-reordering structure effectively circumvents those protections for bank customers.

Rousey proposes an executive order that airline luggage fees should be charged by weight, not by item count, as a common-sense alternative to junk-fee extraction. Edwards also advocates for regulating youth sports scheduling to reduce the financial and logistical burden on families.

**Cashless Society: Who Benefits, Who Loses**

Rousey emphasizes that going cashless would concentrate power in the hands of banks and financial institutions that have demonstrated predatory behavior. Edwards counters that the problem isn't necessarily that private banks *should* serve everyone, but that there needs to be an alternative—otherwise people are forced into a system that exploits them.

Nine states and Washington D.C. have enacted statewide bans on cashless businesses. The Payment Choice Act of 2025, with 21 bipartisan co-sponsors, would require brick-and-mortar retailers to accept cash for purchases up to $500 (with exceptions like arcade-style card machines). The bipartisan support reflects two concerns: financial privacy and preventing forced dependence on institutions vulnerable to surveillance.

Edwards points to Cleveland Federal Reserve research with focus groups of banked and unbanked consumers. Among people who've been mistreated by banks, the hatred runs deep—not just distrust of privacy, but fundamental belief that banks are unsafe and exploitative. For others, banks represent security. The divergence is stark and rooted in lived experience.

**Public Banking: The Postal Solution**

Edwards proposes reviving postal banking as a proven public-sector alternative. The U.S. had a Postal Savings System for roughly 50 years, established after the panic of 1907. It was originally powerful because, before the FDIC existed, postal deposits were backed by the federal government while private banks were not. After the FDIC began insuring private deposits in the 1930s, postal banking's role shifted to small-dollar savings.

Today, France, Germany, and Japan all maintain postal banking systems. These are not cure-alls, but they allow people to hold small deposits, cash checks, and avoid credit cards and predatory products. The Postal Workers Union has pushed for revival; proposals were last floated in 2022. Postal banking could also stabilize the struggling U.S. Postal Service through expanded service revenue.

Edwards imagines postal banking as a tool for children's savings accounts—a safe place to park money for kids without fear of losing it to fees. She emphasizes that the optimal policy isn't forcing private banks to serve everyone; it's providing a public option that serves the excluded and reduces banks' leverage over vulnerable populations.

**The Case for Keeping Cash**

Both hosts conclude that cash serves as a check on banking power. If people have no way to participate in the economy except through banks, those institutions gain total leverage over financial participation. Cash isn't a perfect solution, but it represents optionality and autonomy—a pressure valve if banking becomes too exploitative.

Edwards states her ideal future: a robust slate of public and private financial services where low-income people aren't simply handed over to predatory lenders. If that existed, she might accept a cashless society. But absent such protection, maintaining cash as an alternative is the "ultimate $20 bill you find in the dryer"—a simple insurance policy against total institutional control.

# Time To Say Goodbye — "Are the Dems Already Wasting Their Momentum with Aaron Regunberg"

**Host:** Tyler (unnamed co-hosts). **Guest:** Aaron Regunberg (New Republic writer; former Rhode Island state representative; expert on climate policy, environmental law, and Democratic strategy).

## TL;DR

- **BAJA (Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act) eviscerates U.S. environmental law** — Chuck Schumer and 21 other Democrats voted to fast-track a 500+ page GOP bill eliminating core protections: the Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), and historic preservation rules. Nearly 200 climate scientists oppose it.
- **84% of Americans support the Endangered Species Act** — the law restored bald eagles from 700 nesting pairs to over 300,000; also saved alligators, peregrine falcons, manatees, wolves, and humpback whales. BAJA guts these protections weeks before Democrats take control of Congress with their largest majorities in 30 years.
- **BAJA's real purpose: accelerate data centers and fossil fuel infrastructure** — the bill specifically targets two bottlenecks: cheap natural gas access and streamlined permitting. It opens public lands to data centers and creates "forever permits" that cannot be revoked even by future administrations, cementing Trump and Stephen Miller's ability to build ICE detention camps and other projects.
- **The strongest pro-BAJA argument fails under scrutiny** — supporters say clean energy will win a level playing field because costs are falling. But Trump is already waging war on renewables ("no wind turbines"), tariffs raise clean energy costs, the IRA tax credits are under threat, and even optimistic modeling shows only 3–4% climate pollution reduction by 2040. Fossil fuel firms have spent decades suppressing the clean energy transition.
- **Democrats had far better leverage after January 2027** — with 56 Senate Democrats and control of both chambers, they could demand genuine compromises (transmission improvements, shorter environmental review timelines) and get Republican votes without eviscerating laws. Fast-tracking now, during a GOP lame duck, surrenders that power.
- **Data center backlash is climate policy** — U.S. data centers produce more climate pollution than Denmark; nearly all run on fracked natural gas. Voters rejected data centers in the 2026 midterms; this bill reverses that mandate weeks after Democrats won on an anti-Big Tech platform.
- **Labor union divisions expose political motives** — building trades unions support BAJA for construction jobs, but the NEA (largest U.S. labor union) opposes it because the bill accelerates Trump's ICE detention warehouse buildout. Opponents are not anti-labor; they're discerning about which labor interests matter.
- **An excess profits tax on Big Oil is a climate game-changer** — taxing windfall profits (e.g., triple earnings after the Iran war) and rebating to Americans levels the playing field. Fossil fuels earn double-digit returns; clean energy earns single-digit returns. This policy addresses affordability *and* accelerates the clean energy transition without opening environmental protections.

**The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act and Democratic Betrayal**

The episode opens with Regunberg explaining BAJA, a 500+ page bill being rammed through the GOP's lame-duck session without hearings, stakeholder input, or transparency. The bill is framed as a compromise enabling both clean and fossil fuel energy construction, but Regunberg argues the climate benefits are "extremely unknown and dubious." Nearly 200 climate scientists have signed a letter opposing it. The costs are "generationally significant"—the bill represents the largest negative changes to U.S. environmental law in decades. It eviscerates the Clean Water Act, Endangered Species Act, NEPA, and the National Historic Preservation Act, plus tribal sovereignty protections. This legislation is the top priority of Big Oil, Big Tech, and Donald Trump. The bitter irony: Democrats just won a mandate to oppose exactly these interests in the 2026 midterms, yet 22 Senate Democrats are now fast-tracking the bill weeks before they take full control of Congress.

**Why BAJA Targets Environmental Review**

Regunberg contrasts the abundance-movement's claim that permitting is "red tape" strangling American construction with what permitting actually is: the mechanism by which communities have a say in projects affecting their future. A peer-reviewed study of 88,000 Endangered Species Act consultations found that not a single project was ultimately stopped; average review times were two weeks to two months. NEPA reviews are similarly fast. The real bottlenecks BAJA targets are two: access to cheap natural gas for data centers, and rapid approval for infrastructure projects on public lands. The bill is not solving a permitting crisis; it is dismantling community input as an obstacle to Big Oil and Big Tech.

**The "Forever Permit" and Stephen Miller's Power**

A particularly alarming provision in BAJA creates "forever permits" that cannot be rescinded or revoked by any future administration. This is not abstract: the Trump administration has just received tens of billions in federal funding to build ICE detention camps across the country. Without NEPA reviews, Clean Water Act protections, and the ability to revoke permits, the detention buildout will proceed unimpeded. Regunberg cites Detention Watch and ACLU opposition to BAJA specifically on these grounds. The bill is essentially handing Stephen Miller permanent keys to construct whatever infrastructure the Trump administration desires—data centers, pipelines, detention facilities—and handcuffing future administrations from ever rolling it back.

**The Strongest Pro-BAJA Argument and Its Failure**

Regunberg steelmans the pro-BAJA case: (1) we desperately need clean energy buildout because of the climate crisis; (2) transmission and other structural barriers slow clean energy deployment; (3) even though BAJA makes fossil fuel infrastructure easier, clean energy economics are improving and will win a level playing field; (4) Democrats have no leverage to get Republican support for a better deal with Trump in office. 

The first two points are true. The last two fail. On the third: Trump is already waging war on renewables (he has publicly stated "no wind turbines"), his tariffs raise clean energy costs, and the IRA tax credit benefits are under threat from his team. The equation Regunberg highlights is not static—Trump's policies shift it decisively toward fossil fuels. Moreover, even the most optimistic modeling shows only 3–4% climate pollution reduction by 2040 compared to baseline. Major fossil fuel firms—API, Exxon Mobil, Shell, Chevron, BP—are not unsophisticated actors; they have spent decades learning how to suppress the clean energy transition. Betting they will simply allow BAJA to be used for clean energy is naive.

On the fourth point: Democrats will have 56 Senate votes and control of both chambers by January 2027. They will have far more leverage to demand genuine compromises—transmission improvements, shorter review timelines, no evasion of core environmental law—and still find four Republican votes from clean-energy states. The claim that they have zero leverage to get a better deal *after* winning elections is not credible. Trump will sign bills if they have his name on them, include provisions benefiting the Trump organization, or simply call it what he wants (Regunberg jokes about calling it the "Trump Medicare").

**Data Centers, Climate, and the Reversal of a Mandate**

A new study shows the largest U.S. data centers produce more climate pollution than Denmark. The 2026 midterms saw overwhelming backlash against data centers: voters in Texas, Ohio, and elsewhere ran on explicit platforms of bans and moratoriums. Yet this bill—the first major legislative fight after that mandate—accelerates data center buildout by ensuring cheap natural gas and rapid permitting. The bill is a direct response to Big Tech's terror at the election results. Regunberg notes that multiple Bay Area cities have independently passed ordinances banning data center construction, with the implicit attitude: "I don't care about your charts; I just don't like you, and I don't want you in my city."

**Labor Divisions and Political Authenticity**

Building trades unions back BAJA because more projects mean more construction jobs. This is their mandate, and Regunberg does not begrudge them that. But the NEA—the largest labor union in the United States—opposes BAJA because it will accelerate Trump's detention camp buildout. This reveals something: those now trumpeting "pro-labor" support for BAJA have often opposed public-sector unions and teacher unions their entire careers. The sudden concern for labor only appears when that labor aligns with their political interests. Regunberg's own experience as a Rhode Island state representative—he opposed a natural gas power plant and lost building trades support—illustrates the tension. Being discerning about which labor interests matter is not anti-labor; it's clarity.

**Climate as an Affordability Issue**

One of the hosts pushes back on the framing that climate and affordability are separate. The highest voter frustration, he notes, centers on gas prices and energy costs. Trump started the Iran war, prices spiked, and voters blamed him for it. That opens an opportunity: voters could be receptive to an argument that dependence on fossil fuels leaves them hostage to geopolitical shocks, and that diversifying to clean energy insulates them. This reframes climate action as economic security, not sacrifice.

**An Excess Profits Tax as Climate Policy**

Regunberg's core policy proposal: a permanent excess profits tax on Big Oil. After the Iran war, major oil firms earned three times as much in six months as they had in the equivalent period the prior year. An excess profits tax would impose a threshold return on capital or profit and rebate anything above it to the American people. This is both politically salient (addresses inflation and affordability) and transformative for climate. The structural reason fossil fuels crowd out clean energy is profitability: fossil fuel investments earn double-digit returns, while clean energy infrastructure earns single-digit returns. By leveling returns via taxation, clean energy becomes more attractive to investors. Moreover, when geopolitical shocks spike oil prices and profits, the tax automatically prevents windfalls, removing the incentive shock that makes investors flee to fossil fuels.

**New Climate Champions and Moral Governance**

The new Democratic Congress includes Will Lawrence, founder of the Sunrise Movement, who ran connecting climate action to anti-data-center populism and opposition to oligarchic control. Regunberg notes that climate action is not about individual sacrifice ("just buy an electric car") but about governance and control: Do communities decide their futures, or do billionaires? The fundamental disagreement is philosophical—between technocratic "abundance" approaches (the government should not get in the way of market actors) and populist approaches (communities and democracies should decide what happens in their places). Regunberg cites George F. Will's *Statecraft as Soulcraft* to argue that legislation is always a moral endeavor; there is no neutral governance. Rejecting data centers because "I don't like you and I don't trust you" is not an empirical failure; it's a moral stance about what kind of society we want.

**The China Threat Narrative**

The hosts critique the dominant narrative justifying AI acceleration: if the U.S. does not build AI weapons first, China will, and America will lose. This logic demands massive wealth transfer to billionaires (the top 0.0001%) under the guise of national security. Regunberg notes that recent AI developments—recursive self-improvement—are "legitimately scary," and the responsible response is to slow down, not accelerate. BAJA's acceleration of data center buildout in service of AI development is precisely backward.

**What a Democratic Climate Agenda Could Look Like**

Regunberg sketches alternatives. A Democratic transmission bill—improving grid infrastructure to enable renewable buildout—already exists, authored by experts like Sean Casten and Mike Levin in the House. This could get Republican support from clean-energy states without eviscerating environmental law. Jared Huffman, incoming chair of the House Natural Resources Committee, has already published a list of conditions under which he might support BAJA-like legislation—a signal that negotiation is possible if conducted in the light of day, not behind closed doors. The core insight: the problem is not permitting; it is profitability. An excess profits tax, combined with transmission bills and clean energy incentives, addresses affordability and accelerates the transition without surrendering environmental protections or community voice. The Democrats' strongest majorities in 30 years should be used to advance that agenda, not to sign away their leverage before they even take power.

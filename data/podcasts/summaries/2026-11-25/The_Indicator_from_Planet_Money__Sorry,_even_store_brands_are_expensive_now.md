# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Sorry, even store brands are expensive now"

**Host:** Adrienne Ma and Waylon Wong. **Guest:** Alberto Cavallo (professor, Harvard Business School; economist studying inflation inequality across 10 countries).

## TL;DR

- **Cheapflation discovered:** Between January 2020 and May 2024, cheaper food and beverages in the U.S. rose 30%, while premium versions rose only 22%—a gap that widened globally (Italy saw cheap products rise 28% vs. 15% for premium).
- **Low-income shoppers hit hardest:** Consumers already buying the cheapest varieties have nowhere cheaper to switch, so they face disproportionate inflation when demand from other budget-conscious shoppers drives up store-brand prices.
- **Two drivers of cheapflation:** Increased demand from shoppers trading down to budget brands pushes prices up; companies with thin profit margins on cheap products have less room to absorb cost increases, so they pass them along faster to consumers.
- **Tariffs squeeze budget brands more:** Companies with slim margins "basically cannot afford" to absorb tariff costs in the short run and pass increases to consumers quicker than brands with higher profit margins.
- **Government stimulus may have been erased:** Low-income households receiving pandemic relief payments may have seen income gains wiped out by cheapflation, raising questions about whether policy adequately addressed their actual purchasing power.
- **Utility cost compounds the squeeze:** Beyond price inflation, some shoppers become so squeezed they abandon preferred brands entirely—an unmeasured "utility cost" of inflation inequality.

**What Is Cheapflation?**

Adrienne Ma and Waylon Wong introduce the episode by following Maria Ronan, a food blogger and content creator known as Meals with Maria, who focuses on family cooking on a budget. As she plans Thanksgiving for 14 people, Maria describes her strategy of shopping throughout the month to find the best prices. However, over recent years, switching to cheaper store brands has offered less price relief than it once did.

Alberto Cavallo, a Harvard economist, investigated whether low-income consumers face more inflation than others—a question of hidden inflation inequality. Working with a colleague at the Bank of Canada, Cavallo analyzed price data for over 2 million products across 10 countries (including the U.S., the Netherlands, and Argentina) from 2018 through May 2024. Initially searching for shrinkflation, the researchers found no meaningful evidence. Instead, they discovered a striking pattern: between January 2020 and May 2024, cheaper U.S. food and beverages rose 30%, while premium versions rose only 22%. The gap was even wider internationally—in Italy, cheap products rose nearly 30% while premium products rose just 15%. Cavallo and his colleague named this phenomenon "cheapflation."

**How Consumer Behavior Drives Cheapflation**

Cavallo explains that both shoppers and companies play a role in cheapflation. When inflation surges, many consumers rationally shift to cheaper brands—trading Häagen-Dazs for store-brand ice cream, or cage-free eggs for the budget option. This surge in demand for cheaper products drives their prices upward. The dynamic is particularly harsh for low-income shoppers already buying the cheapest varieties: they cannot trade down further, so they face all the new demand-driven inflation without an escape route.

**How Companies Contribute to Cheapflation**

Profit margins on budget products are inherently slim because companies price them low to compete. When input costs rise—whether from inflation or tariffs—budget brands have far less financial cushion than premium brands. Cavallo notes that companies with thin margins "basically cannot afford" to absorb tariff costs in the short run and "will pass whatever increasing cost they get" to consumers faster than companies with higher profit margins. This dynamic means budget-conscious shoppers bear disproportionate price increases when costs rise.

**The Broader Implications for Low-Income Households**

Cavallo raises a critical policy question: pandemic-era government support—stimulus payments, enhanced unemployment, child tax credits—aimed to help low-income households, yet cheapflation may have erased much of that purchasing power gain. If low-income households face systematically higher inflation on the goods they actually buy, then government income support may have been insufficient to maintain living standards. Cavallo warns policymakers that "you might think you have done enough and you're not doing enough" without accounting for inflation inequality.

**Real-World Impact**

Maria Ronan, the food blogger, recalls that canned goods once cost 33 cents each in office food drives; now a can of corn approaches a dollar—even the store brand. While Maria sometimes chooses slightly more expensive preferred brands (her husband's cottage cheese choice), Cavallo notes that when financial pressure intensifies, shoppers abandon their preferred varieties altogether. This "utility cost"—losing the ability to buy products you actually want—compounds the sting of cheapflation but remains largely unmeasured in inflation statistics.

Egg prices have recently come down, offering at least one small relief. However, prices for gas, groceries, and staples like bread, rice, and peanut butter remain elevated, keeping affordability a top concern for U.S. households.

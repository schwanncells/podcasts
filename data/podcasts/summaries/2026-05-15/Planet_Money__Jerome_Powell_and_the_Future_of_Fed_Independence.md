# Planet Money — "Jerome Powell and the Future of Fed Independence"

**Host:** Kenny Malone. **Guests:** Burton Abrams (economic historian; expert on Fed independence and presidential pressure; studied Nixon tapes documenting Federal Reserve manipulation); Lael Brainard (former Federal Reserve Board of Governors member, nominated by Obama; served during first Trump term and as Powell's vice chair under Biden; now heads Biden's National Economic Council).

## TL;DR

- **Three Fed chairs pressured by presidents in the independence era** — William McChesney Martin resisted Lyndon Johnson (who physically shoved him against a wall); Arthur Burns capitulated to Nixon's pressure, contributing to 14.6% peak inflation in the early 1980s; Jerome Powell was investigated by DOJ, likely due to his rate-setting decisions rather than following Trump's preferences.
- **Powell explicitly stated DOJ investigation aimed at punishing Fed independence** — In a January 2026 video statement, Powell said the threat of criminal charges was a consequence of the Federal Reserve setting interest rates based on public interest rather than presidential preference.
- **Senate confirmed Powell was being retaliated against** — Republican Senator Tom Tillis led Senate effort to block new Fed chair confirmation until DOJ dropped the Powell criminal investigation; DOJ ultimately closed it.
- **Cook Supreme Court case tests "for cause" firing limits** — Trump attempted to fire Biden-nominated Fed board member Lisa Cook using alleged mortgage fraud, which many saw as political attack; Supreme Court heard oral arguments and justices' questions gave Lael Brainard optimism about protecting Fed independence.
- **Congressional legislation could clarify "for cause" standard** — Current 1913 law allows presidents to fire Fed board members "for cause" but doesn't define what that means; Brainard advocates for concrete definition (corruption, negligence) to protect against political removals.
- **Federal Reserve dissent is increasing but may signal health, not weakness** — Trump-nominated board member Stephen Moran dissents at nearly every meeting; Brainard sees this as potentially healthy check on chair's power, similar to Bank of England practice, though U.S. Fed historically maintained unified voice.
- **Powell's story fits into rare pattern of presidential pressure** — Only 3 of 8 Fed chairs since Fed independence (1951) faced historic pressure; Jerome Powell's criminal investigation may be unprecedented and more alarming than physical confrontation McChesney Martin endured.

**Fed Independence as a Concept**

Kenny Malone explains that Fed independence emerged as a formal principle on March 4, 1951, in what the episode calls "Fed Independence Day"—when the Treasury and Federal Reserve issued a memo establishing that the Fed would no longer take orders from the president (who had previously worked through Treasury). This was a response to post-World War II inflation hitting 21 percent when President Truman forced the Fed to keep rates low. The independence principle rests on the understanding that governments tend toward inflationary policy (low interest rates, money printing) when given control of their central banks, whereas independent central banks produce lower inflation over time.

**The McChesney Martin Precedent**

William McChesney Martin was Fed chair during the early Cold War and the Vietnam War. Lyndon Johnson, known for manipulation and grudge-holding, initially cultivated Martin with a warm phone call but became furious within two years when Martin raised interest rates. Johnson summoned Martin to his ranch in Texas and, according to biographer Bob Bremner (cited by Burton Abrams), became so angry he pushed Martin against the wall. Martin responded: "Mr. President, I do have the very strong conviction that this is one of those few occasions where the Federal Reserve decision has to be final." He stood firm against the pressure. Abrams calls this "the moment when the Federal Reserve defined its independence."

**The Arthur Burns Cautionary Tale**

Arthur Burns served as Fed chair under Richard Nixon. Burton Abrams spent decades studying the Nixon tapes, which contain detailed recordings of Nixon strong-arming Burns to juice the economy before Nixon's 1972 re-election. Burns is heard on tape sounding like "a lapdog," reporting rate reductions to Nixon as if seeking approval ("Look, I wanted you to know we reduced the discount rate today"). Burns caved to the pressure, and the resulting monetary expansion contributed to inflation peaking at 14.6 percent in the early 1980s. Abrams notes Burns was "a very well-respected economist" before his Fed tenure but "wound up creating one of the worst inflationary experiences in the last 100 years."

**Trump's First Term and Fed Independence Tests**

During the first Trump administration (2017-2020), President Trump publicly pressured Jerome Powell with tweets calling interest rate hikes "incredible" mistakes. Lael Brainard, who was on the Fed Board during this period, describes how the Fed responded not by ignoring the president but by communicating their dual mandate (low inflation at 2%, full employment) and their data-driven reasoning more clearly. The Fed increased transparency to show Congress, financial markets, and the public that decisions were based on facts, not politics. Brainard notes there was no internal policy to shield board members from seeing presidential tweets; instead, the approach was to demonstrate unwavering commitment to the Fed's actual mandate.

**Powell's Criminal Investigation**

On January 11, 2026, Jerome Powell released a video statement shocking to observers. The Department of Justice had served the Federal Reserve with grand jury subpoenas relating to testimony Powell gave about cost overruns on a Fed construction project. Powell explicitly connected the investigation to his rate-setting decisions, stating: "The threat of criminal charges is a consequence of the Federal Reserve setting interest rates based on our best assessment of what will serve the public rather than following the preferences of the president." For Lael Brainard, this moment paralleled McChesney Martin's confrontation with LBJ—it was Powell's public statement defending Fed independence from executive pressure. However, a criminal investigation into a sitting Fed chair is, Brainard and Abrams agree, more unprecedented than any prior case.

**Congressional Response: The Senate Reasserts Checks and Balances**

When Trump's administration nominated Kevin Warsh (a former Fed board member, appointed by George W. Bush) as Powell's successor, the Senate took action. Republican Senator Tom Tillis specifically led an effort blocking confirmation of the new Fed chair until the DOJ dropped the Powell investigation. The DOJ ultimately closed the investigation, though they said they would reopen it if new evidence emerged. Brainard calls this Senate action a sign that checks and balances reasserted themselves—Congress defended Fed independence on a bipartisan basis, even against a president of the same party.

**The Cook Case and "For Cause" Clarity**

Last year, Trump attempted to fire Biden-nominated Fed board member Lisa Cook, alleging mortgage fraud—a move many observers viewed as a political attack. Cook sued. The case has reached the Supreme Court, and Brainard is watching how the Court defines "for cause" for Fed board removals. The 1913 Federal Reserve Act says presidents can fire board members "for cause" but never defined the term. Brainard advocates for Congress to pass legislation clarifying that "for cause" means corruption, negligence, or other specific workplace failures—not political disagreement. If the Supreme Court allows at-will removal or weakens "for cause" protections, Brainard warns, there would effectively be no independent Federal Reserve anymore.

**Dissent on the Board**

In recent Fed meetings, Trump-nominated board member Stephen Moran has dissented from nearly every decision, stating publicly that rates should be lower. This marks a break from decades of practice, during which dissent was rare and board members worked to present a unified voice to the public. Brainard notes that when she was on the board in 2015 and disagreed with a rate hike, she still voted to support the chair in order to communicate a clear message to the American public. However, other central banks, such as the Bank of England, routinely publish dissenting votes. Brainard sees the emerging American dissent as potentially healthy rather than a sign of weakness, signaling stronger checks on the chair's power, though it represents a shift in Fed culture.

**The Broader Historical Pattern**

Kenny Malone concludes with an observation that contextualizes Powell's tenure: since Fed independence was formalized in 1951, there have been only eight Fed chairs. Three of them—McChesney Martin, Arthur Burns, and Jerome Powell—faced historic pressure from a president. That means 37.5 percent of post-independence Fed chairs experienced major presidential interference. The 40 years of relative peace between Burns and Powell may have felt like the norm but may, historically, be the exception. Powell's story is unique in that he faced not political tweets or taped phone calls but a criminal investigation—a response he explicitly attributed to his commitment to price stability over political pressure.

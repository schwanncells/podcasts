# The Ezra Klein Show — "This Is Why I Find Pema Chödrön So Essential"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Pema Chödrön (Buddhist teacher; author of *Comfortable with Uncertainty*, *When Things Fall Apart*, *Welcoming the Unwelcome*, *Another Kind of Freedom*).

## TL;DR

- **The central question is not how to avoid discomfort, but how to relate to it** — discomfort and uncertainty are inevitable, and Buddhism's approach is "the only way out is through," becoming intimate with what you're feeling rather than trying to eliminate it.
- **Befriending discomfort starts with pausing the storyline** — when anxious or upset, interrupting obsessive thoughts (via meditation) and then finding where the emotion lives in the body (solar plexus, throat, stomach) allows you to send "unconditional warmth" toward it instead of resisting.
- **Pain and suffering are distinct: pain is direct experience, suffering is optional storyline** — a person with back pain faces physical pain, but layers of worry ("this will get worse," "I won't be able to work") create unnecessary suffering that can be worked with separately.
- **Leaning in, not struggling** — when facing discomfort, instead of tensing against it (like fearing getting wet in rain), physically relaxing with what's happening ("stop resisting") makes it workable; Chödrön's teacher Chogam Trungpa called this "collaborating with reality."
- **Growth capacity correlates directly with discomfort tolerance** — comfort zones produce no growth; expansion happens at your edge, and even pausing before acting on impulses (refraining) opens new neurological pathways and transforms habitual patterns over time.
- **Meditation as befriending yourself, not fixing yourself** — the practice reveals habitual patterns, emotional reactivity, and confusion; the instruction is to agree with what you see rather than reject it, recognizing that "you are perfect just as you are" while leaving room to grow.
- **Nowness and the bigger perspective** — developing a relationship with the present moment puts daily struggles in perspective (like astronauts seeing Earth as one small dot); contentment comes from not struggling against life's unfolding rather than achieving a particular outcome.
- **Distinguishing abiding from acting: effectiveness requires detachment from strong emotion** — when protesting injustice or navigating difficult relationships, you must first work with your own emotional reactivity so you can act clearly and communicate; some situations (abusive relationships) require immediate action, while others benefit from sitting first.

---

**The Problem of Uncertainty and Resistance**

Klein opens by sharing how *Comfortable with Uncertainty* resonated with him—he realized how much fear of uncertainty had held him back from places, conversations, and experiences. Chödrön affirms that discomfort is "an ongoing thread" in modern life, and the cultural instinct is to eliminate it. Instead, Buddhism teaches that the path forward is intimate familiarity with discomfort rather than avoidance. She introduces the practice of recognizing what uncertainty triggers physically: a contraction in the solar plexus, chest tightness, or stomach tension. When you can contact that physical sensation and meet it with "unconditional warmth" rather than rejection, "it all becomes very workable." The key verb she emphasizes is "abiding"—staying present with what's arising rather than the quick verbs of surrender or letting go.

**Befriending the Feeling: From Pause to Warmth**

Chödrön walks through a concrete example: someone in a fight with their partner is angry, rehearsing arguments, chest tight, mind spinning. The first move is a pause—meditation trains you to interrupt the storyline and see that you don't have to follow every thought down the rabbit hole. Then you locate the feeling in the body. She cautions that opening a "floodgate" all at once is risky; instead, you "put a little hole in it" for gradual opening. Once you find the contraction, the attitude is not to eradicate it but to send tenderness—"unconditional love" or "unconditional warmth." When Klein admits he hasn't found warmth for his own anxieties but has become better at attending to them and recognizing them as signals to notice rather than eliminate, Chödrön validates this as real progress. She notes a physical gesture—touching your heart or placing your hand on the contraction—can be more powerful than trying to conceptually generate love.

**Why It's Hard to Feel What We're Feeling**

Chödrön attributes much difficulty to trauma: people close down at a young age around certain experiences, and the fear of opening "the floodgate" keeps them stuck. She illustrates with a teaching from her own teacher, the Zen master Kobun Chino Roshi, who when asked how he related with fear said simply "I agree. I agree. I agree." This shorthand captures the essence—agreeing rather than disagreeing, accepting rather than rejecting, staying with rather than running away.

**Pain, Suffering, and the Storyline Layer**

Klein pushes Chödrön on a distinction he struggles with: pain versus suffering. She clarifies that pain is direct experience—putting your finger on a burner, back pain, physical sensation. Suffering is the layer of stories on top: "this will get worse," "I'll be disabled," "I won't be able to do my work." These disaster narratives are optional and create what she calls "unnecessary suffering." Klein admits he feels this layer intensely—the impulse to solve every problem or perfect every moment runs deep. In response, Chödrön references her teacher's teaching in *Another Kind of Freedom* about "negative negativity": there's nothing wrong with back pain, but the judgment about worrying, then judgment about the judgment, compounds the problem. She suggests meditation—keeping awareness on the breath and gently returning when worry thoughts pull you away—can shift anxiety levels by the time you land at your destination.

**The Energy of Allowing Versus Doing**

Klein articulates something crucial to his own experience: the exhaustion of trying to solve problems versus simply allowing them to be there. He works in politics and wants to effectuate change, so he's deeply trained in the "energy of doing." Yet he's noticing how unfamiliar and untrained he is in "just letting things be." Chödrön affirms this is very unfamiliar territory. She then asks: are you attracted to it? Klein says they wouldn't be having this conversation if he weren't. She suggests starting small—meditating on the breath, staying present with whatever arises without trying to fix it.

**Leaning In and Collaborating with Reality**

Klein reflects on walking home in the rain with his young kids. He tenses against getting wet, worrying about puddles and shoes, while his kids jump in puddles with joy. Chödrön nods: his kids are "collaborating with reality" while he resists it. She then shares her own experience of walking in winter snow, learning from Chogam Trungpa's teaching to "lean in"—physically stop tensing, relax with what's happening, and find it "totally fine." The contrast between resisting (everything pushing away, "fruitless") and relaxing (letting it be what it is) is so tangible that once you feel the difference, it's not hard to choose. Klein notes the shift happened when his resistance became funny—he felt like he was in a New Yorker cartoon, which made it enjoyable rather than a battle.

**Comfort and Growth: The Edge**

Chödrön explains that the comfort zone is necessary—your nervous system needs soothing (ocean swimming, music, rest). But growth happens at your edge, where you're slightly uncomfortable. If your edge today is the sidewalk, by next year you want to walk five blocks. Someone once told Klein that growth capacity correlates directly with discomfort tolerance, and Chödrön affirms this completely: "to the degree that we can feel discomfort, to that degree we can grow." Growth means letting natural change and evolution happen rather than freezing in fixed views.

**Meditation as Befriending Yourself**

When Klein asks what meditation is for, Chödrön answers: "a way to get to know yourself deeply, intimately, fearlessly, with an attitude of friendliness." People come to retreats expecting calm and bliss but find painful memories, regrets, and flashbacks instead. She recounts raising her hand with Chogam Trungpa to say she'd been meditating for years and now had "ammunition and proof" that she was "a pretty messed up person." His reply: "Move closer to the feeling of messed up." This captures the instruction—you're not trying to fix yourself but to befriend all of yourself, including the unresolved parts, selfishness, pride, and rage you thought you'd worked through. One teacher, Sonia Rinpoche, has a phrase she loves: "being okay with not being okay." Meditation provides a forum to see yourself clearly, then to agree with what you see rather than reject it.

**The Gap and the Background**

Klein asks about Chogam Trungpa's teaching on the gap—which Chödrön didn't understand until she heard a fan stop for a second during a meditation retreat and recognized the silence he meant. Trungpa described it as a pinprick of light through black plastic covering the mind—a background of stillness, openness, or freshness behind all the chatter. You're not trying to prefer the gap; you're discovering that if discursive thoughts are foreground, there's also a background you can connect with at any moment. Klein pushes back on what he perceives as a paradox: sometimes Trungpa and Chödrön talk as though nothing is better (all thoughts and silence are the same), yet sometimes something is being described as better. Chödrön acknowledges this directly and says "sense of humor" and "ability to be okay with paradox" are essential. She cites Suzuki Roshi's famous line: "You are all perfect just as you are. And you can use a little work." Fundamentally, everyone has Buddha nature—the potential for awakening—but we live in confusion. The work is uncovering what's already here by becoming familiar and intimate with yourself as you are, not by trying to get rid of the ego.

**Abiding and Acting: When to Stay, When to Go**

Klein raises a concern: if you drop storylines and work only with the feeling, don't you risk accepting situations that shouldn't be accepted (e.g., abusive relationships)? Chödrön is unambiguous: "Get out of there as fast as you can." She describes a woman in an abusive relationship for years who was trying to work with the feeling; Chödrön told her to forget the spiritual practice and leave—take the kids and go. She later wrote to thank Chödrön. For activism and protesting injustice, Chödrön emphasizes that you must first work with your own emotional reactivity. When caught in strong emotions like anger, you can't be effective: "Someone's only going to hear your anger. They're not going to hear your words." Experiment with expressing yourself both angry and not angry to find what actually communicates. Then, having worked with your khalayas (strong destructive emotions), you can walk through the door and have a conversation from a place of curiosity and openness rather than fear.

**Nowness and the Bigger Perspective**

Toward the end, Klein asks what it means to have a relationship with "nowness" (from a teaching: "your whole practice should be based on the relationship between you and nowness"). Chödrön says being present can tune you into a bigger perspective. She draws an analogy to astronauts seeing Earth from space—a vast perspective that puts everything in context. When you're caught in thoughts, you're small and trapped; when you look at the sky or get perspective, you see your life against a larger backdrop. Chödrön notes that this awareness can shift how you relate to daily struggles. The promise of the path is not "radiant joy or equanimity" but contentment: "being okay with how things unfold, even if it's disturbing." This doesn't mean passivity—it means not struggling against what's happening while still acting. She emphasizes that this contentment comes from working with the mind through meditation, not from outer circumstances, because "mind has so much power to make you suffer or to help you stay awake and alive to your life."

**Three Book Recommendations**

Chödrön recommends: *Shambhala: The Sacred Path of the Warrior* by Chogam Trungpa (relevant to current world events); *Zen Mind, Beginner's Mind* by Suzuki Roshi (foundational); and *Enlightened Vagabond* by Matthew Ricard, a collection of stories about a 19th-century eccentric Buddhist master with moral lessons and humor.

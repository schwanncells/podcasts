# The New Yorker Radio Hour — "Pete Buttigieg on the Democrats' Sweep of Congress, and Where They Go from Here"

**Host:** David Remnick. **Guest:** Pete Buttigieg (U.S. Secretary of Transportation; 2020 Democratic presidential candidate; widely expected to run in 2028).

## TL;DR

- **Democrats swept both chambers of Congress in the midterms**, delivering a "resounding, extraordinary defeat for Republicans" that exceeded Buttigieg's expectations, driven by widespread rejection of Trump's policies and Republican corruption.
- **The election was a repudiation of Trump, not necessarily an endorsement of Democrats** — Democratic policies remain more popular than Democratic politicians or the party brand itself, creating an opportunity to align messaging with actual policy positions.
- **J.D. Vance is trying to distance himself from Trump despite owning the administration's failures**, including disastrous economic policies and the war in Iran; Vance's earlier comparison of Trump to Hitler makes his current alignment "worse, not better."
- **The priority is kitchen-table economics, not retribution** — Buttigieg opposes focusing on impeachment; instead, Democrats should pursue accountability through illuminating corruption patterns that directly relate to bad policies, and focus on supporting victims.
- **Political reform is inseparable from economic relief** — gerrymandering and broken democratic systems directly undermine progressive policies (Missouri voters approved minimum wage increases only to have legislatures override them), and fixing both requires integrated action.
- **Money in politics remains a major problem despite Elon Musk's $500M investment failure** — members of Congress spend most of their working hours fundraising, and fear of industry super PACs shapes policy regardless of electoral outcomes.
- **This era resembles the mid-20th-century New Deal moment** — the 2020s present an opportunity to rebuild institutions and norms designed for 21st-century challenges (AI, China, Russia, urban design) rather than recreate mid-century frameworks now falling apart.
- **Buttigieg will approach a 2028 presidential run the same way he's approached all offices** — by aligning what the moment calls for with what he uniquely brings to the table, while weighing the personal toll on his family after experiencing swatting and false child protective services reports.

---

**The Election as Opening Rather Than Solution**

Buttigieg characterizes the Democratic sweep as "the beginning of a change in the era we're living in" and an "extraordinary" reversal after weeks of uncertainty about whether free elections would even take place. However, he emphasizes repeatedly that this is "more of an opening than it is an answer." The American people rejected Trump and Republican policies wholesale, seeing corruption and economic frustration as linked. The president's explicit invitation for voters to see him on the ballot succeeded, but the Democratic Party's brand itself did not receive high ratings. Buttigieg notes that for years, Democratic policies have been more popular than Democratic politicians, a reversal from his youth when positions on taxing the wealthy and healthcare were unpopular. The opportunity now is for the party and its politicians to align with policy commitments that already unite two-thirds of Americans and nearly 100% of the party.

**Republican Denial and Fox News Resistance to Reality**

Buttigieg describes Fox News hosts and commentators as treating the election defeat like "a weather event that there's just nothing they could have done about." They seem unable to recognize that unpopular Republican policies — tariffs, tax cuts for the rich — led to bad results without delivering promised benefits like manufacturing jobs or economic growth. These are not difficult policies that voters accept for greater good outcomes; they are unpopular policies that failed to deliver. Buttigieg hopes the "absolutely overwhelming, comprehensive, thoroughgoing rejection" eventually spurs introspection in the Fox newsroom, though he acknowledges seeing only modest signs online so far.

**The J.D. Vance Dilemma and Vice Presidential Distance**

J.D. Vance's Twitter statement criticizing the Iran war and distancing himself from Trump's presidency presents a political paradox. Vance shares the administration's name and is inseparable from its record — tariffs, economic failures, the war itself. Buttigieg argues that if Republican Congress members struggled to shed "the Trump stink," Vance's task is exponentially harder because he is actively part of the administration. Worse, Vance originally compared Trump to Hitler before boarding the Trump train. As Remnick quips, "I have to think he didn't mean it in a good way." Buttigieg concludes that Vance's knowledge of better alternatives only compounds his moral complicity, not mitigates it.

**Accountability Without Retribution: Finding the Right Focus**

When Remnick raises the risk of Democrats overinterpreting the election and pursuing a retribution agenda, Buttigieg agrees there are many ways Democrats could get this wrong. His primary concern is appearing more interested in revenge than in putting money in people's pockets. However, he advocates for a "smart accountability agenda" that serves kitchen-table priorities by illuminating the direct connection between bad policy and corruption. Examples include the delayed bridge between Michigan and Canada (held up by a Trump super PAC donor's influence), victims of pardoned fraudsters, and exemptions for toxic chemicals or dangerous products. Buttigieg opposes impeachment hearings focused on individual Trump officials; instead, he wants investigations and hearings centered on behaviors and victims. This approach unifies rather than divides, because when done right, accountability to standards Americans universally value appeals across partisan lines.

**Political Reform as Economic Issue**

Buttigieg pushes back against consultant conventional wisdom that political reform discussions undermine kitchen-table messaging. He argues they are inextricably linked. Missouri voters approved minimum wage increases and paid family leave by ballot referendum; the next year, elected representatives overrode them, and those legislators largely kept their jobs due to gerrymandering. "There's a direct connection between people struggling to put food on the table and these basic flaws in our democratic system." A "great democratic movement" — likely led by Democrats, though ideally not requiring party alignment — must simultaneously put money in voters' pockets and get politics out of their face.

**Money in Politics: Still Deadly Despite Musk's Failure**

Despite Elon Musk's unprecedented $500 million investment in the Texas race alone (roughly $200 million) yielding nothing, Buttigieg insists money in politics remains a "major problem." It shapes policy outcomes regardless of election winners. Members of Congress spend a literal majority of their working hours on the phone soliciting one- or two-thousand-dollar checks. The real danger is that people fear speaking their beliefs lest they anger a given industry super PAC. Buttigieg cites historical precedent: a century ago, Bill Clark (the Copper Kings' equivalent of Musk) corrupted Montana's legislature to reach the U.S. Senate, triggering the Corrupt Practices Act, which stood as law for 100 years until overturned by the Roberts Supreme Court. Now, Montana voters have just passed the Montana Plan by bipartisan vote to ban corporate money in politics, signaling renewed frustration across the ideological spectrum.

**Rebuilding for the 21st Century, Not Restoring the 20th**

On the historical parallels, Buttigieg rejects the notion that Democrats should "tape back up" the shards of the pre-Trump status quo. Instead, he sees this moment as comparable to the mid-century New Deal era — a period whose institutions and norms persisted for 60-90 years. Today's institutions (the UN Security Council structure, congressional committee layouts, the 40-hour work week, automobile-centric city design) were built for mid-20th-century conditions and are showing their age. The 2020s present the chance to build government, institutions, norms, and social patterns suited to the 21st century: artificial intelligence, assertive China and Russia, transformed media landscapes, reimagined urban mobility. The late 2020s and early 2030s could be remembered as a formative period whose results shape the next 60-90 years.

**Buttigieg's Presidential Calculus and Personal Cost**

Remnick asks what goes into a presidential decision, invoking Obama's observation that it requires "an element of insanity" and an extraordinary leap of imagination. Buttigieg describes his process as consistently comparing two things: what the moment and office call for, and what he uniquely brings to the table. He has applied this to many offices — mayor, Congress, presidency, DNC chair, treasurer — resulting in wins and losses. Two years ago, he applied it to Michigan's governor and Senate races and decided against running despite caring about those offices. Personal toll matters; he and his family weigh what they will endure. Buttigieg's experience with swatting and false child protective services reports this summer shows the abnormal price now exacted for public service. He hopes Americans collectively agree that harming a public figure's family is unacceptable, noting disgust around Vivek Ramaswamy's attacks on Amy Acton suggests this consensus is possible. Fierce and furious politics must not become normalized abuse.

**Addressing Demographic Doubts: Black Voters and South Carolina**

When Remnick notes Buttigieg's historically low support among Black voters (2% of the vote in South Carolina's 2020 primary), Buttigieg acknowledges the context: he was a newcomer competing against Joe Biden, whom South Carolina voters knew and trusted. His relationships have since deepened across the country. As Transportation Secretary, he has delivered federal funds to communities; campaign appearances have been less frequent. Whether running in South Carolina or elsewhere, his standing differs from 2020. His surrogacy efforts this cycle, including in deep-red areas, have strengthened those bonds.

**DSA, Socialism Labels, and Policy Consensus**

On the Democratic Socialists of America and right-wing "socialist" attacks, Buttigieg argues that such labels generated media excitement disproportionate to the DSA's actual party influence. Republicans deployed "socialism" accusations aggressively in this cycle with no effect whatsoever. Policy decisions will converge on things vast majorities of Americans support and nearly all Democrats support — starting there makes sense. He highlights the minimum wage as a concrete example: not touched by Congress since 2009, meaning real wages for minimum workers have fallen one-third. Raising and indexing it to inflation would benefit tens of millions earning above minimum through ripple effects, and polls show even most Republicans favor it. Why not begin there?

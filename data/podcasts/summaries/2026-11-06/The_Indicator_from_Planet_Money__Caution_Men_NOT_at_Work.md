# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Caution: Men NOT at Work"

**Host:** Adrienne Ma and Waylon Wong. **Guests:** Laura Ulrich (director of economic research, Indeed); Drew McCaskill (workforce analyst, formerly LinkedIn).

## TL;DR

- **Men now hold fewer jobs than women for the first time in recent history** — women added more jobs than men in recent months, reversing decades of male workforce dominance; this shift last occurred briefly after the Great Recession and just before the pandemic.
- **Male labor force participation plummeted from 87% in the 1940s to 67% today, while female participation rose from 32% to 57%** — a generational reversal driven by different factors across age groups.
- **Young men (late teens/early 20s) saw a 13% decline in labor force participation since 2000** — the steepest drop across any male age group, creating particular concern for workforce engagement.
- **Multiple distinct causes drive male workforce exit depending on generation** — manufacturing decline for older Gen X men, opioid crisis and Great Recession impact on construction for millennials, and research suggesting video game quality correlates with reduced work hours for younger cohorts.
- **Male workforce decline combined with women's historically lower wages could reduce overall household income** — financial stress increases if male participation continues falling while female workers earn less on average than men.
- **Only 10% of nurses are men despite nurse practitioners being one of the fastest-growing occupations with 40% expected growth over the next decade and median pay around $132,000 annually** — cultural stigma prevents men from entering high-paying female-dominated fields.
- **Expanding career paths beyond college is critical** — apprenticeships, community college, certifications, and skilled trades create more direct routes from school to work for young men disengaged from traditional college pathways.
- **The labor market is not zero-sum; women entering the workforce actually grew the economy and helped men overall** — increased household incomes and consumer spending created jobs that benefited everyone, refuting claims that women's employment came at men's expense.

**The Historical Reversal in Male and Female Labor Force Participation**

Throughout the 1940s, approximately 87% of men participated in the U.S. labor force, either holding jobs or actively seeking them, while only about 32% of women were in the workforce. Over the subsequent decades, these numbers moved in sharply opposite directions. Today, approximately 67% of men participate in the labor force compared with 57% of women. This reversal coincided with waves of economic and social change. After World War II, a thriving economy created demand for "pink-collar" jobs such as secretaries, nurses, and teachers. Between the 1960s and the 1990s, rapid expansion of women's educational and employment opportunities accelerated through the civil rights movement, which barred sex discrimination in employment, universities opening to women, and the Supreme Court expanding access to contraception and abortion. Simultaneously, household economics shifted such that many families required two earners rather than relying solely on a male breadwinner.

**Generational Drivers of Male Workforce Disengagement**

The causes of male workforce exit vary significantly across generations. For older Gen X men, the decline in manufacturing jobs dealt a particularly heavy blow. For millennials, two major shocks—the opioid crisis and the Great Recession—hit male-dominated sectors like construction especially hard. For younger generations and Gen Z men, additional factors compound these macroeconomic headwinds. Research published in the Journal of Political Economy a few years ago suggested that increasing quality of video games has contributed to younger men working fewer hours. As Laura Ulrich notes, "the thing that stands out to me is whatever the trend has been, whatever the cause has been attributed to, it just seems to hit the male labor force harder than it does female."

**Impact on Young Men and Household Economics**

The decline in labor force participation among young men—those in their late teens and early twenties—is particularly pronounced. A recent analysis by the Society of Human Resource Management found these young men experienced a 13% decline in labor force participation since 2000, the steepest drop across any male age band. This widespread disengagement from the workforce has broader economic implications. Ulrich explains that because women on average earn lower wages than men, if male employment continues declining while female employment rises, overall wages could fall, putting financial stress on households. The concern is not merely statistical but touches on household stability and economic security for families depending on labor income.

**Addressing Male Workforce Disengagement Without Blame**

Drew McCaskill, a workforce analyst, emphasizes that understanding male labor force disengagement requires diagnosis without moralization. "We can't just say, oh, you guys are really, really lazy," he argues, noting that if institutions fail to provide young men with constructive explanations for why their lives feel harder, "somebody else online will." McCaskill advocates a multi-pronged approach. First, society should promote career paths beyond traditional college, including apprenticeships, community college, certifications, and skilled trades, creating "fewer dead ends between school and work." Second, cultural notions of men's work must expand. While society has become comfortable encouraging girls to pursue medicine, law, and engineering, the same cultural support does not yet exist for boys entering teaching, nursing, or other female-dominated professions. Ulrich herself observes that despite her own encouragement to pursue a PhD in economics, men entering early childhood education or other female-dominated fields still face social stigma in ways women entering male-dominated fields do not.

**The Labor Market Is Not Zero-Sum**

McCaskill directly counters the argument that women's workforce participation has come at men's expense. He emphasizes that workforce strategy is "not a zero-sum, sort of empathy contest" and that the labor market is not a fixed pool of jobs—economists call the assumption that it is the "lump of labor fallacy." Economic studies demonstrate that women entering the workforce over time actually helped men overall by increasing household incomes and growing the overall economy. Workers are not only workers; they are also earners and consumers. Increased earning power generates increased demand for products and services, which leads to job creation. The goal is not to restore the labor market of 1948 but to prepare men for the labor market of the 2030s by expanding their access to well-paying fields that have traditionally been coded as female, such as nursing, which offers significant demand and strong compensation.

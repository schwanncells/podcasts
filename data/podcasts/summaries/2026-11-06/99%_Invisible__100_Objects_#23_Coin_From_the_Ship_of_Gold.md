# 99% Invisible — "100 Objects #23: Coin From the Ship of Gold"

**Host:** Roman Mars. **Guest:** Annie Brown (editor and executive producer, 99% Invisible; producer of the 100 Objects series).

## TL;DR

- **SS Central America sank in 1857 with ~3 tons of gold worth ~$400 million today** — the ship carried 600 passengers and crew, plus commercial gold destined for New York banks; a hurricane destroyed the vessel off the Carolinas, killing 400+ people including Captain William Herndon, who went down with his ship.
- **The shipwreck triggered the Panic of 1857, the first global financial panic** — New York banks had been relying on California gold to back their notes; without it, banks failed within weeks, railroads collapsed, and the North's economy crumbled while the South's cotton-based economy barely flinched.
- **Southern invincibility from the panic helped spark the Civil War** — the South felt vindicated by their economic resilience, leading Senator James Henry Hammond to declare "Cotton is King" and argue the North wouldn't dare fight; this false confidence encouraged Southern secession.
- **The California Gold Rush (1848–1857) revolutionized the American economy** — 300,000 people migrated west; gold supply shot up, fueling railroad construction, bank expansion, and industrial growth; miners could earn $75 per day versus $6 per month in the East.
- **Bartenders were hired based on thumb size** — saloons paid customers in gold dust, and bartenders' commission was a "pinch" of gold; larger thumbs meant larger pinches and higher tips.
- **Tommy Thompson discovered the wreck 130 years later using historical probability mapping and a custom remote-operated vehicle** — in October 1988, his team found the SS Central America at 7,500 feet depth; the gold coins were in pristine condition, still showing mint marks and the eye of Lady Liberty.
- **Thompson's discovery and subsequent legal battles destroyed his life** — after recovering ~$50 million in gold, he faced lawsuits from investors, insurance companies, and courts; when ordered to reveal 500 coins' location, he refused, went on the run for three years, and served 10 years in jail accumulating millions in fines.

**The California Gold Rush and Its Economic Impact**

On January 24, 1848, James Marshall discovered a thumbnail-sized nugget of gold in the American River while inspecting a sawmill he was building. After testing it with nitric acid and weighing it, he confirmed it was real gold. The discovery spread quickly, and by 1849 alone, 85,000 people flooded into California. The population of San Francisco, which had been under 1,000 people, swelled dramatically. Between 1848 and 1855, roughly 300,000 people traveled to California seeking gold.

The economic incentive was extraordinary: miners could earn $75 in a day versus $6 per month in regular work. One soldier explained the dilemma: "The struggle between right and $6 a month and wrong and $75 a day is a rather severe one." This wage differential drained the army of soldiers and crews of ships as men deserted their posts for the hills. In the early years, San Francisco had no mint, so transactions happened in raw gold dust. Saloons paid for goods in pinches of gold dust, and bartenders were specifically hired based on the size of their thumbs—larger hands meant larger commissions.

This massive influx of gold revolutionized the American economy. The U.S. had relied on gold and silver to stabilize the monetary system but had no major gold source before California. With suddenly abundant gold supply, whole industries emerged: railroad construction accelerated dramatically, banks multiplied, financial institutions expanded, and industrial production boomed. Money was available to loan out, and people had spending power.

However, no one was regulating the economic impact. The banking system in the 1850s was unstable—Andrew Jackson had killed the central bank, so there was no federal regulation, no control of interest rates, and no mechanism to prevent recessions or depressions. By 1857, the Gold Rush was winding down, and investors worried the economy had become overheated and too dependent on California gold. Banks, operating as independent institutions, had accepted deposits of gold in exchange for bank notes but didn't hold enough gold in their vaults to return what they owed. They were desperately dependent on steady shipments of actual gold from California arriving via steamship to maintain the illusion of solvency.

**August 1857: The SS Central America and the Storm**

The SS Central America was a workhorse of the gold rush, ferrying about a third of all gold on the Panama-to-New York route since 1852. On August 20, 1857, the ship departed San Francisco with approximately 500 passengers, 100 crew members, and around 3 tons of gold worth approximately $400 million in today's currency. Among the passengers were Ansel and Adeline Easton, a newlywed couple; Adeline was still in her wedding dress when boarding.

The miners in steerage were returning to the East Coast with their fortunes. Gold is extraordinarily dense—a brick of gold the same size as a standard red brick (weighing 4 pounds) weighs 43 pounds instead. Many miners carried 50+ pounds of gold in money belts and purses, keeping it close to their bodies. The ship stopped in Cuba, then departed for New York.

The first night out of Havana, Captain William Herndon—a 29-year veteran of the sea who resembled a professor more than a sea captain—dined with the Eastons and told stories of the sea. When conversation turned to a recent shipwreck scandal where captains saved themselves first, Herndon stated firmly: "I'll never survive my ship if she goes down. I'll go under her keel." That night, the wind picked up. By the next afternoon, waves were turning into mountains, and the ship began heaving violently. Nearly everyone became seasick; even the ship's doctor became ill. The dining saloon was deserted except for a few passengers bracing themselves with their elbows to keep their plates from sliding.

**The Leak and the Sinking**

After two days, the ship's engineer discovered a leak on the starboard (right) side of the ship's hull. The crew stuffed it with blankets and old sails, but each time the ship turned right, water pushed the sails out. The core problem, however, was that steam-powered ships relied on coal as ballast to anchor them in the water; the coal weighted the ship down so the side wheels could gain purchase on the water. The ship had been fighting a headwind and rough seas for days, burning coal at an unsustainable rate. As the coal depleted, the ship rode higher in the water, gained less control, and was pushed off course—constantly heaving to the right, which forced water through the leak into the bilge. The pumps ran continuously, but water rose faster than it could be pumped out. If water reached the furnaces, the fires would be extinguished, steam would drop, and the engines would stop—leaving the ship powerless in the storm.

By noon on Friday, water overflowed the coal, hitting the furnaces with an audible hiss and filling the hold with steam. Captain Herndon announced the situation: 8 to 10 feet of water in the hold, and the crew would have to bail the ship bucket by bucket. Approximately 500 men formed bailing lines—long chains passing buckets hand-to-hand through the ship's gangways. The work was grueling; the ship heaved, men slammed into each other, buckets slipped and spilled, and waves crashed over the rails. After 24 hours of continuous bailing without food, the crew spotted another ship on the horizon: the Marine, which had also been battered by the storm. It agreed to stand by and attempt a rescue.

But there were only three lifeboats left; the others had been torn away by the hurricane. And there was the gold: approximately 500 miners and returning gold rush participants had accumulated thousands of dollars each in gold coins and dust. They were advised to carry only two $20 gold pieces. When told that women and children would board the lifeboats first and that gold couldn't be taken, many passengers made a remarkable choice. Some women shook $11,000 (equivalent to $400,000 today) of gold onto the floor, weeping. One man ripped open a bag of gold dust and sprayed it into the air "like fairy dust." The miners, after years of risk and effort to accumulate their wealth, were letting it go—watching it disappear into the ocean.

Approximately 150+ women and children successfully evacuated in the lifeboats. The Eastons' newlywed trunk was left behind; it would later be recovered from the ocean floor, still containing their fine clothes wrapped in a New York newspaper dated 1857. Captain Herndon, true to his word, went down with the ship. Ansel Easton survived by clinging to a wooden plank and was rescued by another passing vessel. In total, 400+ people died; it was the worst commercial maritime disaster in American history at that time.

**The Economic Collapse and the Panic of 1857**

All three tons of gold went to the bottom of the ocean. The New York banks, running on fumes, had been counting on that gold. The railroads' stocks were slipping, land speculation bubbles were deflating, banks had issued too many loans that wouldn't be repaid, and in August—just weeks before the ship sank—a major financial institution in Ohio had collapsed. New York investors began withdrawing their money, but there wasn't enough gold or silver in reserve. The banks expected the SS Central America's gold to stabilize everything. When the ship sank and the gold didn't arrive, the banks held on for a few weeks, then stopped paying out gold altogether. A run on the banks ensued.

The news of the shipwreck—the massive loss of life and gold—spread rapidly via telegraph, making it the first global financial panic in history. Fear could now be communicated instantly around the world. Everyone knew the situation; every banking institution worldwide knew it. Everyone tried to withdraw their money from banks simultaneously. Banks were suspended; some closed entirely. Railroads went under, grain prices collapsed, foreclosures spread, and land sales dried up. Western expansion essentially stopped. The panic reverberated globally.

However, the South was barely affected. The South had fewer railroads and fewer financial institutions, but more importantly, cotton prices only dipped slightly and recovered quickly. Southern planters emerged from the Panic of 1857 feeling invincible. They attributed their survival to the strength of the slave-based economy and cotton production. This false sense of invincibility led to a famous Senate speech by James Henry Hammond, titled "Cotton is King," in which he declared: "No power on earth dares to make war upon it. Cotton is King. Who can doubt that has looked at recent events when thousands of the strongest commercial houses in the world were coming down and hundreds of millions of dollars of supposed property evaporating into thin air?" Hammond concluded that it was cotton production that "brought you up" from the crisis. This ideological confidence—that the North couldn't and wouldn't fight the South because it depended on Southern cotton—became a psychological catalyst pushing the South toward secession and contributing to the outbreak of the Civil War.

**Tommy Thompson's Discovery and Obsession**

The SS Central America remained lost for 130 years. The exact wreck location was unknown; the ship had been pushed off course for days, so no one aboard knew where they were, and no one on shore could pinpoint it. In the early 1980s, Tommy Thompson, a 31-year-old oceanic engineer at a respected research institute in Columbus, Ohio, became obsessed with finding it. He convinced 161 investors to fund the search with $12.5 million and took on enormous personal debt.

Thompson innovated in two ways. First, he used historical accounts—survivors' diaries, maritime documents, and historical weather data—to create a probability map of the wreck site, mapping competing historical scenarios and weighing their likelihood. Second, he built a remote-operated vehicle called the Nemo that could survive at crushing depths of 7,500 feet, manipulate objects, and retrieve items.

In October 1988, after exploring 59 different locations, Thompson's team found the wreck. The ocean floor at that depth had so few currents and such cold water that objects remained in pristine condition. Gold coins—called eagles ($10 coins) and double eagles ($20 coins)—still had their ridges intact. The dates and the eye of Lady Liberty were clearly visible. The team also found early photographs (daguerreotypes), jars of shaving cream and beauty products, a cup inscribed "To My Mother," the oldest known pair of jeans, and the Eastons' steamer trunk filled with fine clothes still wrapped in the 1857 New York newspaper.

Thompson developed a technique where a robotic arm placed a silicone frame around gold on the ocean floor, shot silicone around it, let it harden, and brought up bricks of silicone filled with coins.

**The Curse of Gold: Legal Destruction**

When Thompson sailed into Norfolk, Virginia on October 4, 1989, with the recovered gold, he received a hero's welcome—a marching band played "When You Wish Upon a Star," and crowds cheered. That same day, 39 insurance companies sued him in federal court, claiming they had insured the original cargo in 1857 and deserved the money.

Years of legal battles ensued, costing millions in legal fees. Eventually, a judge ruled in Thompson's favor under the law of fines, determining that 92% of the gold belonged to him as the discoverer. He sold much of the gold for approximately $50 million, which he claimed went toward legal debts and discovery costs. His investors, however, believed they should have received a share. More importantly, they felt misled and strung along through multiple rounds of broken promises.

The conflict focused on 500 specific coins Thompson had minted from recovered gold. These coins weren't worth much comparatively—about $2.5 million—but investors sued for them anyway in the mid-2000s. Around this time, friends reported that Thompson began to "decompensate." When a judge ordered him to reveal the coins' location, he refused. Instead, he went on the run, becoming a fugitive for three years while living in a Hilton in Florida with a female companion, using disguises like floppy hats when leaving the hotel.

Eventually caught, Thompson still refused to reveal the coins' location, claiming he had developed chronic fatigue and amnesia. The judge didn't believe him, held him in contempt of court, and sentenced him to jail—with the condition that he'd remain there each day he didn't disclose the location, charged $1,000 per day in fees. Tommy Thompson stayed in jail for 10 years, accumulating millions in fines. A few years ago, the judge concluded that contempt of court wasn't going to force compliance and released him. No one has ever found the 500 coins.

**The Cycle of Gold**

The episode concludes by reflecting on the pattern of the gold's curse. Gold promised fortune to 49ers, and while some found riches, most found disappointment and left their families behind for years. The gold revolutionized the American economy, but its overabundance overheated it. When it sank off the coast, it dragged the nation's confidence with it. It convinced the South of their invincibility, ultimately contributing to the Civil War. A century later, it gave meaning to Tommy Thompson's life before arguably driving him mad. The pattern is always the same: the gold saves you before it ruins you.

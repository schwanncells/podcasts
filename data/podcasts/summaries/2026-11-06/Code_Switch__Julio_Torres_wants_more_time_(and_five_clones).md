# Code Switch — "Julio Torres wants more time (and five clones)"

**Host:** B.A. Parker and Jean Demby. **Guest:** Julio Torres (comedian, actor, writer, and queer Latino artist; creator of *Color Theory*, *My Favorite Shapes*, *La Bola Negra*, and *The Perfect Gift*).

## TL;DR

- **Julio has divided himself into five clones with distinct roles:** J1 consumes art and manages the body's basic needs; J2 and J3 execute projects in film and television; J4 handles miscellaneous work like jewelry design and gift guides; J5 does press and networking.
- **Time is the greatest gift for artists** — Torres feels a constant scarcity despite success, frustrated by years lost to visa bureaucracy. "The best gift for an artist, I think, is time."
- **Freedom isn't the end goal — it's the beginning** — in *La Bola Negra*, queer characters finally have the liberties their ancestors longed for, yet remain unhappy. "The problems are the dream," Torres observes. Once basic rights are secured, real life begins.
- **Media consolidation by "a handful of men" terrifies him** — controlling TikTok, news, TV shows, and everything else. He feels a responsibility to find "alternative routes" and "other roads tucked away through the forest" outside the consolidated media highway.
- **His Salvadoran identity anchors his work without requiring him to "perform" ethnicity** — Characters naturally grounded in El Salvador feel real rather than forced, giving his work a tangible anchor despite never having felt at home in the country.
- **Gifts are bridges between people** — Torres designed *The Perfect Gift* as a soothing picture book for adults structured around gift guides, where thoughtful giving reflects the relationship between giver and receiver, not consumer convenience.
- **He's in an interdisciplinary experimental phase** — recent projects include a performance piece for the Whitney, film work on *La Bola Negra*, and the upcoming book; he's deliberately creating outside mainstream channels.

**About Julio Torres and His Work**

Torres is a comedian, actor, and writer whose work spans stand-up specials (*Color Theory*, *My Favorite Shapes*), film (*La Bola Negra*), and literature (*The Perfect Gift*). His comedy uses abstract shapes and colors to critique institutional power and human behavior — navy blue represents the demand for online RSVPs and bureaucratic precision, while ovals pine to be circles. Beyond comedy, Torres creates work that "interweaves his identity" as a queer Latino artist "in totally unexpected magical ways," as host Jean Demby notes. His art operates as what Demby calls a "thoughtful chaos agent" in an entertainment landscape still dominated by conventional comedy formats.

**The Five Versions of Julio**

Torres describes having calculated that he needs five versions of himself to accomplish everything he wants. J1 consumes art — watching films, reading, visiting museums — while bearing "the burden of the mortal body," handling chores, doctor visits, and the mundane necessities of existence. J1 generates ideas. When an idea emerges, it goes to J2, J3, or J4 for execution. J2 and J3 work in film and television. J4 is "miscellaneous projects" — jewelry design, *The Perfect Gift*, designing playgrounds, and other interdisciplinary work. Finally, J5 handles press and public-facing work: attending parties, accepting photographer requests, and selling ideas to industry gatekeepers. Torres jokes that "only one gets to live" — J1, out in the world experiencing life. The others live "on set, in front of the computer" (J2–J4) or "going from event to event to event" (J5). Currently, he notes, he *is* J5, speaking to Code Switch.

**Freedom Isn't Enough: La Bola Negra and Queer Longing**

*La Bola Negra* weaves together three stories of queer men across different eras in Spain, showing how contemporary queer characters live freedoms their historical counterparts desperately craved. Yet Torres's character and his on-screen partner are unhappy despite having everything — a beautiful apartment, a beautiful city, queer friends, and genuine liberty. Torres frames this not as a contradiction but as progress: "the rights of a minority, of a queer person, that's the basic minimum. And then there's life." Achieving legal equality and social freedom means "now I get to have the same kind of problems that anyone does" — relationship strain, depression, unfulfilled desires. "The problems are the dream," Torres concludes, because problems represent the luxury of having survived the struggle for survival.

**Time as Scarcity, Bureaucracy as Thief**

Torres expresses persistent anxiety about time despite significant achievement and recognition. He traces much of this to years spent navigating visa bureaucracy before settling in New York, feeling he lost valuable creative years to administrative systems. "If I hadn't wasted so much time trying to get a visa, I would have started earlier," he reflects, though he acknowledges the system, not personal failure, is at fault. This connects to a broader observation about how art gets made: artists usually come from privilege because "when you're coming from that experience, when the hell are you going to aspire to become an artist? You're just spending all your day trying to stay alive." Internships, for instance, presuppose time to work unpaid, effectively restricting creative paths to those with financial cushions. Torres believes time is "such a luxury" and "truly what the best gift for an artist" is.

**The Consolidation Danger and Finding Alternative Routes**

Torres expresses deep concern about media consolidation. "The fact that there's only a handful of men that own all of media, and by media, you know, that ranges from TikTok to the news to TV shows to, like, everything" troubles him fundamentally. He acknowledges his individual power is limited but feels a "great sense of responsibility" to create alternative infrastructure. His metaphor: the consolidated media is a highway that "we all can look ahead" and "none of us like the road that lies ahead." His answer is to explore "other roads tucked away through the forest around us." This year has been deliberately interdisciplinary — performances at the Whitney, experimental theater-like work, dabbling in unexpected mediums — as part of testing "how do I create work outside of the main highway?" His next project is called *The Off Ramp*.

**Salvadoran Identity as Anchor, Not Performance**

Growing up in El Salvador, Torres never felt rooted there. He was always imagining a life elsewhere, specifically in New York, which he obsessed over because he wanted to see movies three months before they reached other cities. He hasn't returned to El Salvador in a very long time and wouldn't move back. Yet his Salvadoran identity appears naturally in his characters — not as a performative marker of authenticity but as grounding reality. "That is honestly where I am from. And I think that it anchors it. Like having an anchor in reality like that really helps," he explains. The specificity isn't an obligation; it's a practical device to give his abstract, fantastical work a tangible foothold.

**Gifts as Relational Objects**

Torres wrote *The Perfect Gift* as a soothing picture book for adults — a gift guide structured to explore the philosophy of gift-giving. He frames gifts as "a bridge between the gift giver and the gift receiver. You have to think of your relationship to that person. Otherwise, it's just completely impersonal." The gift becomes a "totem for your relationship with that person." When asked what he wants, Torres advocates for handmade presents: "I would personally say make me something. Create something. I like that. And it's so personal. I love a drawing from a person who doesn't know how to draw or who never draws." The book itself embodies this philosophy — offering not consumer recommendations but meditations on thoughtful connection.

**Temporal Responsibility and Artistic Urgency**

Torres feels called to act now because "these are very crucial years in the history of humanity and art making." He sees his responsibility as twofold: creating work that resists consolidated media control and experimenting with forms and mediums outside established channels. This urgency partly derives from a sense that he's running out of time — a feeling he acknowledges may be irrational given his success, but persistent. He's drawn to artists who keep evolving (like the painter who recently started making jewelry), and frustrated by his own inability to pursue every creative impulse simultaneously. The five clones, ultimately, are his solution to an unsolvable problem: wanting to be everywhere, do everything, and create across disciplines, none of which is possible within a single mortal lifespan.

# Freakonomics Radio — "694. Does Democracy Really Need Elections?"

**Host:** Stephen Dubner. **Guests:** Terry Bericius (co-founder of the Labor Party, Citizens Party, and Vermont Progressive Party; author of *Democracy Without Politicians*; former director of election reform at FairVote), Hélène Landemore (professor of political science, Yale University; author of *Politics Without Politicians*; leading advocate for citizens' assemblies), DJ Hairston (public defender, New York City; served on jury trial involving Dubner).

## TL;DR

- **43% of American voters are dissatisfied with both parties** — a New York Times Siena poll shows nearly half of voters reject both major parties; roughly half of eligible voters don't vote at all, reflecting widespread frustration with the electoral system.
- **Ancient Athens filled offices by lottery, not election** — sortition was the norm for most governing bodies; Aristotle said "lotteries are democratic and elections are oligarchic," a consensus among political theorists for 2,000+ years until the Enlightenment.
- **Ireland legalized same-sex marriage and abortion via citizens' assemblies** — politicians delegated these polarizing issues to randomly selected bodies; same-sex marriage passed with over 60% support in a 2015 referendum, and abortion legalization passed by huge majority in 2018.
- **Connecticut Citizens Assembly on Property Taxes: 100 randomly selected residents, $1,200 pay, recommended a millionaires' tax** — members spent six weekends deliberating; required two-thirds majority to approve 10 final recommendations; all non-binding but structured to include people who would never attend a town hall.
- **Congress is 95% millionaires while 1 in 150 Vermont House members is a renter, despite nearly a third of Vermonters being renters** — elected bodies systematically exclude the perspectives of ordinary people; Landemore argues that random selection provides diversity that electoral systems cannot.
- **Sortition advocates propose replacing Congress with multiple specialized policy juries, review panels, and agenda-setting councils** — policy juries of ~500 people would vote on bills prepared by review panels; votes would be binding; a rules and oversight council would constantly improve the system, similar to how jury duty operates now.
- **British Columbia Citizens Assembly produced a ranked-choice voting proposal with 57% support but was rejected because politicians required 60%** — shows the challenge of asking entrenched beneficiaries of the current system to relinquish power.
- **Bericius and Landemore believe elections inherently attract narcissists, psychopaths, and those willing to stoke fear and hate** — competitive electoral incentives reward deception and emotional manipulation; sortition bodies, by contrast, have no ego investment in defending prior decisions and can correct course with each new jury.

**The Crisis in American Electoral Democracy**

Stephen Dubner opens with a jury trial he recently served on in Manhattan, a low-level drug case that turned into a meditation on how democratic deliberation should work. Twelve strangers from different backgrounds, none of whom volunteered, came together with a sense of civic duty, listened carefully, and collectively rendered a verdict based on evidence and conscience. That experience leads Dubner to ask: if jury duty produces this kind of deliberative behavior, why not apply the same logic to political office-holding?

The context is grim. A New York Times Siena poll found 43% of voters dissatisfied with both major parties. About half of eligible voters don't bother voting at all. The U.S. electoral system, in Dubner's framing, is "a thicket that gets thicker by the day" — layers of good intentions mixed with bad intentions, vested interests, and corruption. The system is deliberately hard to reform because the people with the most power to change it have the least incentive to do so.

**What Is Sortition and Citizens' Assemblies?**

Sortition means choosing public officials by lottery, drawing lots in the classical sense — the opposite of elections. A citizens' assembly is a large group (typically 100–1,000 people) randomly selected to deliberate on a specific policy issue for a sustained period, often a few weeks or months. Participants receive education on the topic, deliberate in small groups and large plenaries, and then vote on recommendations. Landemore describes it as "very large group of people, I would say at the minimum 100, up to maybe 500, 1,000, that are convened to deliberate about a policy issue for a sustained period of time, typically a few weekends distributed over a few months."

Terry Bericius explains that he was once a believer in electoral reform — he founded three political parties (the Labor Party, the Citizens Party, and the Vermont Progressive Party) and worked for FairVote advocating for ranked-choice voting. His conversion to sortition came in 2004 when he testified before the British Columbia Citizens Assembly on electoral reform. He was stunned to see everyday people—a substitute teacher, a cattle rancher—deliberating seriously without hidden agendas. "This is real government by the people," he realized. "In fact, this is what democracy looks like."

**Why Sortition Disappeared: A 2,000-Year Historical Reversal**

Hélène Landemore traces the arc: Aristotle wrote that "lotteries are democratic and elections are oligarchic." This was the consensus among political theorists for over 2,000 years—through Rousseau and Montesquieu. The American founders understood this too but rejected true democracy in favor of a system they thought would elevate a "natural aristocracy" of wealthy, educated men. Elections, despite their appearance of meritocratic selection, were always a tool of elite control.

Sortition disappeared in the 18th and 19th centuries for specific reasons. Bernard Manin, a French political theorist, points to social contract theory and the Enlightenment's elevation of individual reasoning over collective action. When democracy was "reinvented," it was equated with getting people's consent via the ballot box—a requirement sortition seems to violate. Additionally, the statistical science needed to understand how random samples could be representative didn't emerge until the 19th century. Politicians today, lacking that understanding, tend to be skeptical of ordinary citizens' capacity for governance.

**Real-World Experiments: Citizens' Assemblies in Action**

The modern sortition movement began gaining traction with concrete examples. The British Columbia Citizens Assembly on Electoral Reform (2004–2005) was striking: 158 randomly selected residents met over many months, deliberated seriously on voting systems, and proposed ranked-choice voting. The proposal received 57% support in a referendum—but politicians had set a 60% threshold, so it failed. The assembly dissolved, having produced no binding change.

Iceland tried something more radical after the 2008 financial crisis. Having jailed seven bankers and lost confidence in existing political structures, Iceland organized an election that *banned politicians from running* and selected a 25-member constitutional council based on random selection recommendations from a 950-person panel. The attempt to rewrite the constitution stalled when politicians reasserted control.

Ireland, by contrast, achieved major success. Facing the polarizing question of same-sex marriage in a solidly Catholic country, politicians declined to touch it and delegated the issue to a citizens convention, one-third of whom were politicians and two-thirds randomly selected. The body recommended legalizing same-sex marriage; the legislature sent it to referendum, and it passed with over 60% support in 2015. A similar process led to legalization of abortion in 2018, again with huge majorities. Ireland has since used citizens' assemblies to address drug policy, climate change, and the constitutional ban on blasphemy.

France convened a Citizens' Convention on Climate with 150 randomly selected citizens. President Macron initially advanced all but three of their 149 proposals. Landemore, who observed the process, notes that while the dilution at the ministry level was significant, polling showed 70% of the population knew what a citizens' assembly was by nine months after its creation, and two-thirds had a positive view. The impact on France's 2021 Climate and Resilience Bill was substantial, even if many recommendations were watered down.

Connecticut recently completed its own Citizens Assembly on Property Taxes. The state mailed 240,000 invitations to participate. One hundred people were selected by lottery, paid $1,200 for six weekends of deliberation, and asked to propose reforms to the state's property tax system. The assembly required a two-thirds majority for each recommendation. Among their final 10 proposals was a recommendation to tax income over $1 million per year. Members like Doris Gomez (a Verizon manager) emphasized that "this report was not drafted by politicians, lobbyists, or special interest groups. This assembly was entirely citizen-led." The recommendations are non-binding, now awaiting state legislature consideration.

**The Case Against Electoral Democracy**

Bericius articulates the pathologies of elections. Elections operate on competitive incentives that reward narcissism, psychopathy, and the willingness to stoke fear and hate. People who are ill-informed vote based on emotional appeals. Candidates optimize for winning, not governing well. In large electorates where any one vote is unlikely to decide the outcome, there's no incentive to become well-informed. As a result, "almost all voters are very ill-informed," and "candidates...stoking fear and hate is a very effective campaign tool."

Landemore adds a structural argument: Congress, despite nominal diversity, is ~95% millionaires. When Bericius surveyed Vermont legislators on a housing bill affecting renters, he found only one renter among 150 House members—while nearly a third of Vermonters are renters. The unrepresentative nature of elected bodies means they systematically lack perspectives critical to solving problems affecting ordinary people. Research on diverse groups suggests that "when you have a relatively homogeneous group, they tend to know similar sorts of things. And there can be whole domains of knowledge that they're completely unaware of." Random selection provides the diversity that elections exclude.

**How Sortition Could Scale: The Sortitian Architecture**

Bericius envisions a multi-tiered system. At the federal or state level, there would be several types of deliberative bodies. An **agenda council**, composed of randomly selected citizens, meets annually (say, for three months) to hear testimony about pressing issues facing the country or state and decides which topics the government should tackle over the next term. Their decisions go to **subject-specific review panels**—one for housing, one for defense, one for health care—which might serve for several years to develop expertise, draft bills, and accumulate knowledge. The review panels do not vote on bills; they only prepare them based on testimony and data.

From the review panels, bills go to **policy juries**: large bodies of several hundred randomly selected citizens (perhaps 500 at state level) who serve briefly—maybe a week—and vote. These votes are binding. By keeping the juries large, they statistically represent the broader population; by keeping service brief, members don't develop career incentives or ego attachments to prior decisions.

Finally, a **rules and oversight council** is continuously selected by lottery to audit and improve the system itself, deciding how many times each speaker can talk, what salaries these bodies should receive, and how procedures should evolve.

Critically, the executive branch would be fundamentally restructured. There would be no policy-making president; the executive would be purely administrative. As Bericius notes, the history of the "imperial presidency" and the office's aura of power make it dangerous to concentrate policy authority there. A ceremonial figurehead president might remain.

**Why Sortition Might Actually Work**

Landemore emphasizes that randomly selected people, when given time to study a complex issue, often become more competent than elected legislatures. "If you're not representatives in the way you think and approach problems, you're unlikely to generate smart decisions." She argues that putting enough people selected at random together ensures that "all kinds of perspectives on a social political issue...you don't necessarily get in a select group of the few who've been typically trained in the same schools, live in the same neighborhoods, experience only the world through a limited prism."

Bericius notes that the key advantage of sortition is that each new jury has no ego stake in prior decisions. Unlike politicians who must defend their record, sortition juries can openly say, "Those guys were wrong; let's fix it." This creates a self-correcting system. The theory of collective intelligence emerging from diverse ordinary people, he argues, is well-established in social science; politicians simply don't believe it because their personal experience with ordinary citizens is skewed toward the vocal, angry people who show up at town halls.

**Obstacles and Concerns**

For all its theoretical appeal, sortition faces immense practical barriers. First, the entrenched power problem: asking politicians to vote themselves out of office is nearly impossible. Bericius's strategy is "peeling"—starting with policy areas politicians are happy to delegate (no-win issues like housing or climate in polarized times) and gradually expanding sortition's scope. But this requires existing officeholders to voluntarily cede authority.

Second, accountability is uncertain. If a sortition body made a genuinely bad decision (e.g., voting to repeal the First Amendment), how would voters correct it? Bericius argues that because the jury is representative, their decisions reflect the people; if they're wrong, the next jury can overturn them without ego-driven defensiveness. But this seems to rely on the assumption that representativeness automatically produces good governance—a leap not all questioners accept.

Third, lobbyists and billionaires would not lose leverage entirely. Money might flood the airwaves with advocacy ads to shape public opinion before a policy jury votes. Sortition would need to be accompanied by strict limits on political advertising.

Fourth, scale and complexity. Mapping out a federal system with agenda councils, review panels, policy juries, and rules bodies across all domains is speculative. Who decides what issues go on the agenda? How do these bodies coordinate? The Devil is in the institutional details.

**Broader Cultural Perspective**

Landemore shares a personal anecdote that hints at sortition's deeper appeal. She attended a citizens' assembly in France where strangers who arrived skeptical and wounded by political alienation gradually transformed. "In just two weekends, the mood completely flipped and it became this place where people just treated each other like family, really, like hugged each other, exchanged gifts, listened patiently." Standing outside the venue, she experienced "all these emotions, and I didn't know what they were, but they felt like what people called love of country...love of my people."

For Landemore, sortition is not merely a technical fix; it's a vision for politics as something fundamentally different from the toxic, partisan environment that currently dominates. She argues that "politics by nature" doesn't have to be conflict-ridden and distasteful—that sortition communities demonstrate an alternative in which ordinary people, when treated with respect and given time, choose collaboration over tribalism.

New York Governor Kathy Hochul, when Dubner mentioned Bericius's book, expressed interest: "I'm intrigued by it," though she suggested starting at local levels like school boards before scaling up.

**Conclusion**

The episode poses a genuine question: in a democracy where voters are mostly uninformed, candidates optimize for fear and hate, and elected bodies systematically lack the diversity to represent their constituents' actual needs, is relying on random selection less absurd than it sounds? Sortition has already been tested in dozens of countries—Ireland's success with same-sex marriage and abortion; France's climate convention; Connecticut's property tax assembly—with results that range from genuinely influential to watered down but still meaningful. Whether the U.S. would adopt sortition at scale remains unclear, but as frustration with electoral democracy deepens, the idea appears to be gaining attention precisely because it is so radically different.

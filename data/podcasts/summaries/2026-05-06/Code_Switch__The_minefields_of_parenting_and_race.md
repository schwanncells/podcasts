# Code Switch — "The minefields of parenting and race"

**Host:** Gene Demby. **Hosts/Contributors:** B.A. Parker, Shereen Marisol Maragi. **Guests/Experts:** Dr. Cassandra Harewood (child and adolescent psychiatry), Amy Stewart-Wells (professor, Teachers College, Columbia University; expert in race and education), Jen Jackson (Ph.D. candidate, University of Chicago; studies blackness and gender), Mark Anthony Neal (chair, African American Studies Department, Duke University), Juliana Melzi (associate professor, applied psychology, NYU; expert in bilingual language development among Latinx children).

## TL;DR

- **White parents' anxiety about diverse spaces can't be left unchallenged.** A 12-year-old white boy's fear of Black people stems from lack of real-world exposure and media stereotypes; parents must actively broaden their social circle and confront their own comfort zones, as their friend choices signal to the child who is "valuable" and trustworthy.
- **Low test scores don't mean a school is bad.** Single-parent education level is the highest predictor of test scores, not school quality. When middle-class families leave public schools, outcomes worsen for lower-income students; parents should visit schools, talk to principals, and assess pedagogy before choosing private options, especially for families of color who were once "the only one."
- **Letting a Black boy paint his nails and cry is not "going against Black culture."** Restricting gender expression in Black families often stems from historical pressures to conform and counter white racism's attempts to emasculate Black men; policing gender happens across all races, but hypermasculinity requirements are a structural defense, not a cultural mandate.
- **Children internalize status hierarchies of languages very early.** English has global prestige; Spanish and other minority languages carry lower perceived status. Children as young as five avoid speaking home languages in public. Parents speaking to the child one-on-one in the home language is necessary but insufficient; they need peer groups, diverse contexts, and media examples (like Shakira) that signal the language's value.

**White Anxiety and the Obligation to Diversify**

A Philadelphia mother writes that her 12-year-old white son expresses fear of Black people despite having Black coaches, teachers, and classmates. When he encounters loud, gregarious groups of Black kids, he becomes frightened and attributes it to race. Dr. Cassandra Harewood, a child and adolescent psychiatrist, explains that the current climate makes many people anxious, including adults. The issue is not the boy's observation but his interpretation without context. When a child articulates anxiety ("Are there Black kids there?"), the mother must probe deeper rather than accept the association at face value. What specifically troubles him? What worries him?

Popular culture saturates viewers with images of Black men as violent, predatory, and aggressive. Without real-life counterbalances—friends of color in the home, diverse social circles—the child defaults to media stereotypes. Dr. Harewood is direct: the mother is "part of the problem, but it also means she could be part of the solution." Parents must consciously broaden their social circle, even if uncomfortable, "thinking, I'm going to do this for the good of my child." The signals children absorb about who is valued, trustworthy, and important to invite into the home are subtle but powerful. If every trusted adult in a child's life is white, those signals accumulate.

**School Performance, Test Scores, and Middle-Class Flight**

A Black and Asian mother in Aurora, Colorado faces a household divide: her white husband, an education policy expert, insists their biracial daughter will not attend the local public school district, which is 55% Latinx and 19% Black and has low college-placement numbers. The mother feels guilty, believing public schools improve only if middle-class families stay in them.

Amy Stewart-Wells, a professor of education at Teachers College, Columbia University, takes issue with the premise. Test scores, widely used to judge schools, reveal far more about race and class than actual school quality or learning environment. The single highest predictor of a child's test scores is parental education level—not the school's curriculum or teaching. Schools labeled "low performing" often contain teachers and programs worth investigating directly. Stewart-Wells advises parents to visit, talk to the principal, examine the curriculum and teaching philosophy, and understand what their child needs before deciding a school is "not a fit."

The research is clear: when middle-class children attend schools with lower-income students, the middle-class children's test scores do not decrease. The opposite is true: the presence of middle-class students improves outcomes for lower-income children. Stewart-Wells acknowledges that if a parent does due diligence and concludes the local school doesn't match their child's learning style, that's a different equation. But skipping that work perpetuates segregation and sends a message to the child that diverse schools are not worth their time. The mother should also remember: she is an expert too, by virtue of her lived experience growing up as a child of color—a perspective her education-policy husband does not have.

**Gender Expression, Respectability Politics, and Black Masculinity**

White foster parents in Raleigh, North Carolina allow their six-year-old Black foster son to paint his nails and watch Frozen. His biological family objected, claiming the parents were "going against his culture" by deviating from rigid gender norms (boys must be tough, not show emotion, avoid "girls'" movies). The foster parents worry they are ill-preparing him for a "hyper-masculine culture he may return to."

Jen Jackson, a Ph.D. candidate at the University of Chicago who studies Blackness and gender, is unequivocal: letting a Black boy paint his nails or cry is not contrary to Black culture. "Black people show up in all sorts of different shapes, sizes, forms, gender, sexes, sexualities and expressions. Blackness is full." Gender policing occurs across all races—white, Asian, Latino families enforce it too.

Mark Anthony Neal, chair of African American Studies at Duke University, contextualizes the biological family's concern within history. Black parents and families are often "very much invested in very normative gender standards because of the way that blackness has been treated as an abnormality." Historically, fitting in meant performing "very conservative and rigid gender performances so that they're not looked at as strange and unusual." This connects to a deeper history: white racism has specifically attempted to emasculate and castrate Black men (through lynching and systematic violence), creating an added pressure for Black families to counter with hyper-masculine performance. Neal calls this "respectability politics"—the idea that conformity insulates one from discrimination.

However, that dynamic doesn't make hypermasculinity a cultural truth; it makes it a structural defense. When Black men and athletes appear on television, their expression is magnified and coded differently than white expression. LeBron James is described as a "beast" or "different kind of physical specimen"—a superhuman attribution rarely applied to white athletes with identical behavior. The solution is not to police the foster child into rigidity but to surround him with Black people expressing different gender identities and expose him to Black media with diverse characters. Without representation, the child absorbs a narrow script.

**Bilingual Development and the Status of Languages**

A mother in New Mexico wants her daughter to develop fluency in Spanish and English but faces a husband who thinks speaking Spanish in public, around English speakers, is impolite. The mother worries that confining Spanish to the home will signal the child that it is shameful or inferior to English.

Juliana Melzi, an associate professor at NYU and researcher in language development among Latinx children, confirms the mother's worry: children are extremely attuned to the status of languages. English carries global prestige; Spanish and other minority languages often carry lower status. Children very early internalize that signal and lose motivation to use the "lower-status" language. Melzi's own daughter, at five years old, went through a phase of refusing to speak Spanish in the elevator around strangers she guessed didn't speak it.

Melzi's husband's concern about politeness is socially aware and valid, but the execution matters. When inside a broader group gathering with English speakers, the parents can use English to communicate with the larger group. But when speaking directly to the daughter, they should maintain Spanish—because the moment a parent switches to English with the child, the child loses the need to respond in Spanish. Over time, the child becomes "receptive bilingual" (understands but doesn't speak), a common outcome.

Research shows that around preschool, the strongest predictor of bilingual ability is how much the child actually uses the language. Parents must create opportunities for the child to use Spanish with peers, not just parents—playgroups and communities matter. Language is contextual; children need to see the language in diverse settings. Melzi gave an example: when her daughter became fascinated with Shakira, the mother encouraged and played her music; Shakira gave Spanish "cool factor" that shifted the child's willingness to use it.

Melzi pushes back on the idealized vision of bilingualism—the fantasy that a bilingual child will function identically in both languages. "Bilingual is not two monolinguals put together." If a child is functional in two languages, they are bilingual. Perfect balanced fluency is not a realistic target.

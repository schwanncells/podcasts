# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Is it time to abolish the MBA?"

**Host:** Waylon Wong, Adrian Ma. **Guest:** Chris Marquis (Cambridge University; previously Cornell and Harvard; focuses on sustainable business and social responsibility).

## TL;DR

- **Chris Marquis calls for abolishing business schools** — a University of Cambridge professor and business education insider argues the modern B-school model is "designed to promote dealmaking, simplification, and optimization," spreading this mindset as a "virus" to wider society.
- **MBA median ROI is $665,000 over 10 years** — despite strong financial returns, 80% of graduates from top B-schools funnel into three fields: consulting, finance, and tech, rather than pursuing idealistic goals they brought to school.
- **Case studies reinforce corporate success bias** — Harvard and other schools teach via 10–30 page case studies that must be approved by the companies they profile, structurally over-representing corporate winners and carefully packaged narratives (e.g., Enron cases published before the fraud became public).
- **Idealistic students get "squashed" by curriculum and career offices** — Marquis observes students arrive with civic intentions around public health and clean tech but face curricula focused on shareholder capitalism, peer pressure toward high-paying roles, and career services steering them toward lucrative fields.
- **Ethics courses are band-aid solutions** — Many business schools added ethics programs after the Enron scandal, but Marquis argues they don't address the core problem: inculcating students to see the world through profit and deal-making rather than public good and social costs.
- **Business schools need interdisciplinary redesign** — Both Marquis and Erica James (Wharton dean) agree that meaningful reform requires integrating other disciplines—government policy, social work, environmental studies—to prepare students for societal challenges like public health and climate change.

**The Case Against Modern Business Education**

Chris Marquis frames business schools as institutions fundamentally misaligned with the aspirations many students bring. Schools like Stanford and Harvard market themselves as incubators for world-changing leaders, and Marquis has taught thousands of idealistic students interested in public health infrastructure and clean tech innovation. Yet the structure and incentives of business education systematically redirect these aspirations toward corporate roles in consulting, finance, and technology.

The median return on investment for an MBA is roughly $665,000 over a ten-year period, a powerful draw for students carrying significant student debt. Marquis acknowledges this financial reality but argues it comes at a cost: as much as 80% of graduates from top business schools end up in one of three fields—consulting, finance, or technology—not because those align with their original interests, but because career offices actively recruit for them and peer pressure reinforces the choice.

**Case Studies and the Problem of Corporate Bias**

A cornerstone of business education is the case study method—10 to 30 page documents that present a real-world business scenario and ask students to make strategic decisions. Marquis himself wrote dozens of case studies while at Harvard Business School, but he identifies a critical structural bias: case studies must be approved by the companies they profile before publication, creating strong incentive to present companies in a favorable light.

This approval requirement systematically over-represents corporate winners and their carefully curated narratives. Marquis points to Harvard's publication of multiple case studies on Enron in the early 2000s, which presented the energy giant's business strategy positively—before the company's collapse revealed massive accounting fraud driving its apparent success. While the Enron scandal prompted many business schools to develop ethics courses, Marquis argues these remain surface-level fixes that do not address the deeper problem: a curriculum that teaches profit and deal-making as the primary lens for understanding business, while marginalizing public good and social costs.

**The Business School Response and Partial Agreement**

Erica James, dean of the University of Pennsylvania's Wharton Business School, characterizes Marquis's call to abolish B-schools as "salacious" and overstated. She emphasizes that core business skills—accounting, finance, marketing, management—are essential regardless of career path; most organizations require people who understand resources, personnel, and money. She also notes that modern business schools do offer courses beyond traditional corporate metrics, including classes on environmental sustainability and criminal justice reform.

James acknowledges that business schools have been "complicit" in funneling students toward consulting and finance through aggressive recruiting by those industries. She observes a generational shift: younger students arriving at business school increasingly expect exposure to business's role in social impact and societal contribution beyond profit-seeking.

**The Path Forward: Interdisciplinary Integration**

Despite their disagreement on the severity of the problem, Marquis and James converge on a solution: business education must integrate other disciplines. Marquis envisions business schools engaging more deeply with government policy, social work, and environmental studies—preparing students to tackle fundamental existential crises like public health and climate change, rather than churning out consultants and financial engineers.

James echoes this sentiment, suggesting that the gold standard is marrying a liberal arts mindset with business acumen. Business education is valuable for solving world problems, she argues, but it is not the only solution—and business schools must prepare students to work across disciplines to address them.

# Time To Say Goodbye — "OUR BIG MIDTERM ELECTION PREVIEW plus Cheesecake Factory and the Poors"

**Host:** Jay. **Guests:** Tyler (co-host; political analyst with focus on state races and grassroots strategy).

## TL;DR

- **Obama's "Saddam Hussein's Cheesecake Factory" joke** triggered right-wing backlash from Clay Travis and others claiming elitism, despite Travis later being caught lying about when Cheesecake Factory opened in Nashville (the chain wasn't there until he was 26).
- **California 22nd District (Central Valley):** Randy Villegas, a community college professor with a politics PhD, is running as a progressive in a 70% Latino, majority-Democrat district against incumbent moderate Republican David Valadao. Polls show a toss-up despite Valadao's agricultural authority and House committee positions.
- **Texas Senate:** James Tallarico, a seminary student and former middle school teacher, is in a toss-up race against Ken Paxton, a deeply corrupt Republican attorney general. First Democrat potentially to win a Texas Senate seat since 1988 if Paxton's unpopularity and ICE crackdowns alienate Latino voters.
- **Polling challenges and uncertainty:** Midterm outcomes will hinge on whether Republican unpopularity (gas prices, corruption, war fatigue) outweighs potential polling errors; close races could break either way depending on turnout models and whether low-propensity voters actually cast ballots.
- **Left-wing candidate strategy:** Grassroots turnout of unactivated voters via community organizing (seen in Maine with Plattner, New York with Mamdani) is a proven alternative to conventional moderate campaigning, though scaling it to rural/agricultural districts like CA-22 remains untested.
- **Post-election narrative risk:** Both left and moderate wings of the Democratic Party will claim any blue wave as validation of their strategy; the muddiness of close local races makes it hard to extract clear lessons about which approach wins elections.

**The Cheesecake Factory and Elite Out-of-Touchness**

Barack Obama made a joke at a rally comparing Trump's White House aesthetic to "Saddam Hussein's Cheesecake Factory," which Jay and Tyler both found funny. The right-wing response, led by media figures like Clay Travis, claimed the joke exemplified Democratic elitism and contempt for working-class Americans who regard Cheesecake Factory as an aspirational dining destination. Travis said he grew up going to Cheesecake Factory and found Obama's mockery offensive.

However, reporters later discovered that the Cheesecake Factory in Nashville didn't open until Travis was 26 years old; he subsequently claimed he was referring to a different Cheesecake Factory in Washington, D.C., when he was 18. This fact-check embarrassed Travis and suggested Republicans are "terminally online" and creating panicked counter-narratives rather than addressing substantive issues. Jay notes the dynamic is offensive not because Obama's joke was cruel, but because the right attributes to poor and working-class Americans a naïve conflation of Cheesecake Factory with fine dining, when in reality they're sophisticated consumers who know exactly what the restaurant is.

---

**California's 22nd District: Testing Progressive Populism in the Central Valley**

Villegas is running against Valadao in a district that is roughly 70% Latino and 42% Democratic-registered versus 26% Republican-registered, yet has elected a Republican for years. Valadao is a rare moderate Republican: he supports pathways to citizenship for undocumented immigrants, voted to impeach Trump, and has genuine agricultural expertise grounded in his dairy farm upbringing. He sits on the House Agricultural Committee and has successfully secured federal funding for farm-related projects.

By contrast, Villegas grew up on a farm, earned a politics PhD from UC Santa Cruz, and runs as a community organizer. His platform centers on Medicare for All, curtailing ICE, and ending military aid to Israel—a stance that drew heavy attack advertising in the primary but also brought endorsements from AOC and Bernie Sanders. The Working Families Party has invested significantly in his campaign. Villegas's strategy is to activate unregistered or low-propensity Latino voters who don't normally participate, betting that a populist left platform on healthcare and labor organizing will outperform the centrist approach that has repeatedly lost.

Jay is skeptical whether the turnout-focused strategy translates from urban areas like New York City (where Mamdani and others succeeded with grassroots organizing) to a sprawling, rural agricultural district where voters may be more concerned with concrete issues like farm policy and less aware of national left-wing movements. Tyler counters that grassroots turnout has proven successful in multiple races and that appealing to unactivated voters is worth attempting. However, he acknowledges that a community college professor running a national-level platform in a district where voters' primary concerns are agriculture and their livelihoods may struggle to articulate relevance. The race is currently a toss-up, and if Villegas performs better than previous moderate challengers (even if he loses), it would suggest the strategy has merit.

**Texas Senate: Tallarico vs. Paxton**

James Tallarico, a former middle school teacher and Presbyterian seminary student, is running against Ken Paxton, the sitting Texas Attorney General. Paxton has a long history of corruption allegations, fraud indictments, extramarital affairs, and ethical violations; he is widely regarded as one of the worst candidates in Republican politics. Trump endorsed Paxton over the incumbent moderate Senator John Cornyn, alarming the national Republican Party, which worried Paxton would make Texas competitive for Democrats.

Current polling shows the race as a genuine toss-up, with some polls showing Tallarico ahead. If Tallarico wins, he would be the first Democrat to win a Texas Senate seat since 1988. Tyler expresses skepticism about trusting the polls, citing historical patterns of Texas being too red and noting the Beto O'Rourke race as a cautionary example of a charismatic young challenger whose enthusiasm didn't translate to victory. However, Jay points to the Rio Grande Valley as evidence that something has shifted: the region, previously a Trump stronghold in 2024, has flipped back to Democratic in recent polling, suggesting that ICE crackdowns and immigration enforcement may have alienated Latino voters who previously swung right.

Republicans have mounted a culture-war attack on Tallarico, tying him to transgender ideology and citing past statements (like saying "God is non-binary" and supporting gender care for minors). Tallarico has moderated on some of these positions, particularly on sports and transgender participation. Jay argues that the trans attack is a sign of Republican desperation: Paxton is so corrupted and indefensible that the GOP has nothing positive to say about him, forcing them to manufacture scandals. The New York Times published an article about Tallarico's classroom size during his teaching career, investigating whether he taught 45 students or 30—a trivial difference that reinforced Jay's view that media scrutiny is disproportionately focused on manufacturing doubt about a clean candidate while the other side runs a serial fraudster.

Jay particularly admires Tallarico as a candidate because he makes a moral case for decency, healthcare, and human dignity rather than just attacking Republicans on economic grounds. Tyler is less enthusiastic about Tallarico personally but acknowledges his campaign is substantive and that his willingness to stake a moral position rather than rely purely on attacks on Paxton's corruption is refreshing.

**Polling, Turnout, and Uncertainty Heading into Election Day**

Both Jay and Tyler acknowledge the inherent unpredictability of close races, particularly given polling errors in recent cycles. The conventional wisdom has swung toward expecting Republicans to underperform in 2026 (the opposite of the "red tsunami" prediction in 2022), but that reversal of consensus may itself lead to overconfidence.

Gas prices, inflation, the ongoing Iran war, and Trump's personal unpopularity (corruption allegations, Epstein associations) are likely the primary drivers of Republican losses, not specific culture-war issues. Immigration enforcement and ICE crackdowns, while not the dominant media narrative, are salient in communities directly affected and may be shifting Latino voter attitudes toward Democrats in ways not fully captured in traditional polling.

---

**The Grassroots Turnout Debate and Its Limits**

Tyler and Jay discuss whether low-propensity voter turnout strategies that worked in Maine (Plattner vs. Mills) and New York (Mamdani) can scale to the Central Valley. Plattner's upset relied on exhaustive grassroots organizing and events in a statewide race; Mamdani's primary victory similarly emerged from intensive community organizing. However, turning out college-age voters hungover on election day in a major city is a different challenge than mobilizing agricultural workers, many non-English-speaking, some undocumented or fearful of ICE, spread across a vast rural district.

Both agree that the left must develop stronger working-class candidates beyond the "politics PhD from elite university + community organizing background" archetype that dominates progressive races. This requires building labor and organizing infrastructure that elevates working-class leadership, not just working-class voters—a multi-year project.

---

**Post-Election Spin and Muddied Lessons**

Tyler predicts a blue wave but warns that its causes and implications will be misinterpreted. If diverse Democratic candidates win—centrists like Janelle Steltzin in Pennsylvania's 10th district, progressives like Villegas, and moral-case makers like Tallarico—both wings of the party will claim vindication. The actual lesson (MAGA is cracking, Republicans are unpopular, gas prices matter) will be buried under competing narratives about what type of Democratic strategy "works."

Jay emphasizes that the Democratic Party must learn to articulate a moral case against Republican cruelty, corruption, and indignity—not just economic critiques. The country feels cruel, corrupt, and murderous to many Americans; any Democratic resurgence should be framed around decency and human dignity, with economic arguments in support, rather than the reverse.

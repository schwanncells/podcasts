# Unchained — "In an AI Agent World, Do Money Markets Win Over Stablecoins? - Bits + Bips"

**Host:** Austin Campbell (Zero Knowledge Group). **Guests:** Ramo Lawalia (Lumina); Chris Perkins (250 Digital Asset Management); Gordon Liao (Circle, economist).

## TL;DR

- **Hyperliquid won Coinbase and Circle partnership** — USDC replaces USDH as aligned collateral; Hyperliquid gets 90% of reserve revenue (~$180M annually on $5B USDC deposit at 3.8% yield); Coinbase becomes treasury deployer, Circle the technical deployer.
- **Money markets may outcompete stablecoins for agents** — Agents optimize for yield; a money market fund (tokenized via USYC) that pays interest beats a non-yield stablecoin unless the stablecoin itself yields. Circle bridges this with seamless conversion between USDC and USYC.
- **Activity-based yield survives Clarity Act compromise** — Senators Tillis and Brooks banned strictly passive stablecoin yield but permitted activity-based rewards; banks lobbied against this but senators said "not open for renegotiation."
- **OpenAI lawsuit ends on statute of limitations, merits untouched** — Federal jury rejected Musk's $134B claim; ruling turned on three-year statute expiration (Musk knew of for-profit shift by 2021, sued February 2024). Case never adjudicated breach of charitable trust or unjust enrichment.
- **Value capture in AI likely accrues to distribution, not models** — Speakers argue OpenAI/Anthropic have thin margin at foundation layer; Azure, Anthropic's distribution, and application layers capture more. Apple may control distribution via hardware; Meta and others investing heavily in open-source narrow the moat.
- **Warsh confirmation and long-end bond pressure** — 30-year yield hit 5.12% first time since pre-2008; term premium now 80 bps (supply/demand driven, not rate expectations); bond vigilantes pushing back against dovish Fed signals.
- **Digital Asset Clarity Act advanced to Senate floor on 15-9 vote** — Needs 60 votes to proceed; ethics concerns remain around Trump family divest requirements; bill still considered likely to pass narrowly.
- **Agents will flow between blockchain and traditional rails** — Winner will be the service that spans on-chain payments, money market exposure, bank details, and card rails. Treasury Secretary said stablecoins are marginal buyer of short-term treasuries; this limits long-end supply and pressures yields.

**Hyperliquid, USDC, and the Stablecoin Consolidation**

Circle and Coinbase announced that USDC will replace Hyperliquid's native USDH stablecoin, a reversal of governance that voted for USDH eight months ago. The deal restructures deeply: Coinbase becomes treasury deployer, Circle the technical deployer and a validator on Hyperliquid. Hyperliquid receives 90% of reserve revenue, projected at ~$180M annually on the current ~$5B USDC deposit at 3.8% yield. Chris Perkins frames this as fundamental shift—exchanges capture net interest income (NIM), the float deposited by users. Hyperliquid is no exception; by controlling the collateral and yield flow, the platform ties itself more tightly to Coinbase/Circle and locks in stablecoin fungibility at scale.

Ramo Lawalia emphasizes this as especially bullish for Hyperliquid: the platform becomes the dominant perp venue (already true), so the stablecoin used there radiates across the entire on-chain economy. For Circle and Coinbase, ubiquity is the win—more USDC in circulation means broader adoption and new outposts for deployment.

**Stablecoins, Money Markets, and the Yield Problem**

Chris Perkins poses the crux: agents will optimize aggressively for yield. If an agent can choose between holding USDC (no interest) and a money market fund (say, 4% yield), why hold the stablecoin? This creates a structural problem for stablecoins in an agent world, unless stablecoins themselves yield.

Gordon Liao explains Circle already operates both: USDC (the settlement asset) and USYC (the tokenized money market fund). But even then, friction exists. He points to historical precedent—$3 trillion of non-interest-bearing physical cash and savings accounts coexist even from the same institution because different functions demand different products. Agents, though, are not human; they are ruthlessly efficient and will flow to maximum yield.

Austin Campbell adds a critical detail: agents may not be "loyal" to one payment rail. Rather, they will optimize across flows. Sometimes blockchain is cheapest; sometimes a card; sometimes bank transfer. Winners will be those who let agents seamlessly move between all three without friction.

**The Clarity Act Yield Compromise**

The Senate Banking Committee advanced the Digital Asset Market Clarity Act on a 15-9 bipartisan vote. A key compromise: senators Tillis and Brooks banned passive stablecoin yield but permitted activity-based rewards. The American Bankers Association objected, but senators signaled finality: "not open for renegotiation."

Gordon Liao sees academic merit in this distinction. Money has multiple facets—unit of account, medium of exchange, store of value. Separating the store-of-value component (yield) from the settlement component (no-yield stablecoin) mirrors how new intermediation in DeFi works. Instead of balance-sheet-heavy banking, activity-based intermediation is mediated by smart contracts and agents. The yield distinction maps cleanly onto this divide.

Chris Perkins notes the deeper regulatory complexity: changing stablecoin yield rules without rewriting the 1940 Act and Banking Act is impossible. Money market funds are legally required to pay yield. As long as tokenized securities and stablecoins coexist with reserves that look like tokenized securities, the door cannot close—it is form over function. Congress has little appetite to rewrite foundational securities law for crypto.

The bill still faces two hurdles: ethics concerns (around Trump family divestitures) and potential amendments on the floor. Campbell believes it will pass narrowly, barely.

**The OpenAI Lawsuit and Foundation Model Value Capture**

A federal jury unanimously rejected Elon Musk's claims in his $134B lawsuit against OpenAI. The ruling turned entirely on statute of limitations: the jury found Musk knew of the for-profit shift by 2021 but sued in February 2024, exceeding the three-year window. The court accepted the verdict without ruling on the merits—breach of charitable trust, unjust enrichment, and other claims were never adjudicated.

Chris Perkins views the outcome as a win for OpenAI but notes the larger institutional question remains: how can a nonprofit mission pivot to become a closed, Microsoft-backed profit engine? The case exposed broken promises on openness and safety.

Rob argues (in minority) that Sam Altman is a villain who has repeatedly cut deals of questionable legality. The broader debate—do foundation models accrue value, or is it distribution?—comes into focus. Rob posits that value is thin at the model layer; Microsoft will own Anthropic's IP in six years after their licensing terms end, and even OpenAI burns tens of billions with meager value capture. Meta, by contrast, is rolling out compelling open models cheaply, narrowing any moat.

Gordon Liao sidesteps personalities and emphasizes competition. The AI space is competitive at every level—foundation models, applications, infrastructure. For the blockchain-plus-AI angle, building rails for agentic commerce (nano payments, agent marketplaces) offers enduring network effects that models alone do not.

**Fed Chair Warsh and Bond Vigilante Pressure**

Kevin Warsh was confirmed as Fed chair 54-45, the closest Fed chair vote in the modern era. Hours later, the 30-year Treasury auction broke 5%, hitting 5.12% at the high—the first time since before the 2008 financial crisis. The 10-year sits at 4.59%, the 2-year at 4.08%. CME FedWatch now prices 50% odds of a rate hike later this year, inverting expectations for cuts.

Gordon Liao, with background from the Federal Reserve Board in D.C., breaks this down as a term premium story. Using the ACM model, he isolates whether yield moves are driven by term premium (supply/demand compensation) or expected rate changes. Most of the 30-year rise is term premium—now ~80 bps, quite high relative to recent history but low vs. pre-2020 levels. This means the market is signaling supply/demand stress, not rate hike expectations per se.

Two demand-side factors: continued fiscal expansion and long-term loss of credibility in inflation control. On supply: Warsh is a proponent of Fed balance sheet reduction (QE reversal). As the long end pressures up, the Treasury Secretary has publicly noted stablecoins are now the marginal buyer of short-term treasuries. Stablecoins prefer T-bills and reverse repo (due to regulatory constraints), not long bonds. This reduces the supply of long-dated dollar duration and pushes yields up.

Chris Perkins adds that insurance companies and sovereigns are the traditional long-end buyers, and both preferences are shifting. Sovereigns are holding less long-dated U.S. debt (geopolitical), and insurers' demographics (aging Boomers out, Millennials in) reshape the curve.

Ramo Lawalia, from an investor angle, views term premium at 80 bps as reasonable compensation for inflation risk and Fed non-bias toward cuts. Rate-sensitive names (insurers, banks) have been hammered, but recent rallies in long-duration, high-free-cash-flow assets suggest the equity market believes the long end is topping out. If rates stabilize here, these assets are attractive again.

**Summary**

This episode knits together four major crypto/macro narratives: the consolidation of stablecoin infrastructure around Coinbase/Circle (and the winner Hyperliquid), the challenge that agents pose to stablecoins if money markets outcompete them on yield, regulatory progress (Clarity Act with yield compromise), and the long end bond repricing as term premium rises and stablecoin marginal buying skews short. The panel navigates competing visions—whether value in AI concentrates in models or distribution, whether agents flow across rails or lock into blockchains, and whether the long bond's rise signals real inflation concerns or asset allocation shifts. Common thread: infrastructure, distribution, and optionality matter more than any single product.

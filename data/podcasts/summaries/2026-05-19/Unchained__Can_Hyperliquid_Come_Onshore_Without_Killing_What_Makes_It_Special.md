# Unchained — "Can Hyperliquid Come Onshore Without Killing What Makes It Special?"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guests:** Walt Lukken (president and CEO, Futures Industry Association; leads global derivatives trade association with offices in Washington D.C., London, Brussels, and Singapore; represents listed and cleared derivatives industry); Chris Perkins (CEO, 250 Digital Asset Management; former derivatives trader at Lehman Brothers and Citigroup; ran three global derivatives businesses; former FIA executive committee member).

## TL;DR

- **CME and ICE requested CFTC oversight of Hyperliquid** — The two largest traditional derivatives exchanges asked regulators to bring the decentralized perpetuals exchange under US supervision, citing concerns about unregulated price discovery affecting global commodity benchmarks.
- **Hyperliquid's core advantage: 24/7 markets with blockchain transparency** — The offshore exchange publishes every transaction on-chain in real-time, making it hostile to insider trading and price manipulation while supporting continuous trading that traditional markets cannot yet offer.
- **Three regulatory paths forward** — Hyperliquid could stay offshore (current status), register as a US exchange (DCM) with clearing and intermediary licenses, or remain decentralized while allowing US persons access through principles-based regulation that focuses on behavior rather than entity control.
- **Post-2008 derivatives reform may be inadequate for decentralized markets** — After the 2008 crisis, regulators centralized all clearing through entities (FCMs, DCOs) to mitigate counterparty risk; decentralized exchanges challenge this assumption by distributing risk across participants rather than concentrating it.
- **24/7 trading requires structural changes to payment rails, clearing systems, and banking infrastructure** — Traditional exchanges cannot compete with Hyperliquid's continuous markets without solving technological and regulatory bottlenecks (clearing settlement, collateral management, money movement) that current systems run only on business hours.
- **Prediction markets face identical regulatory questions** — Polymarket and Kalshi operate under the same offshore model; the CFTC is preparing rule-making on prediction markets, making their resolution important for understanding how to regulate all decentralized markets.
- **CFTC's principles-based framework offers a pragmatic path** — Regulators can focus on outcomes (no manipulation, no customer theft, financial integrity) rather than requiring traditional intermediaries, allowing innovative structures to prove themselves under established legal principles.

**The Regulatory Backdrop: Markets Outpacing Rules**

Chris Perkins frames the current situation as a replay of the 2008 financial crisis but inverted. After Lehman Brothers, markets had gotten ahead of regulation—OTC derivatives were largely unregulated—so regulators had to catch up, centralize clearing, and impose strict intermediary oversight. Now, crypto markets have outrun regulation in the opposite direction. Hyperliquid and similar decentralized venues are operating offshore and delivering 24/7 price discovery while traditional US exchanges struggle to compete, still constrained by business-hours clearing and banking infrastructure. Walt Lukken notes that this is a natural market evolution; as new marketplaces mature, they eventually seek regulation for credibility and confidence.

**CME and ICE's Concerns: Commodity Benchmarks and Unregulated Price Discovery**

CME Group and Intercontinental Exchange formally requested that the CFTC oversee Hyperliquid, citing two major risks. First, price discovery for commodities like oil and agricultural products is increasingly happening in Hyperliquid's unregulated environment rather than in US-regulated futures markets; this undermines the integrity of prices that commercial hedgers and interstate commerce rely on. Second, there is concern about sanctions evasion—potential Iranian or Russian flows moving through Hyperliquid without the know-your-customer (KYC) and anti-money-laundering (AML) protections of regulated venues. Lukken emphasizes that the CFTC has broad enforcement authority even beyond its markets; it can police manipulation that impacts registered futures prices. However, he argues that having these markets come onshore and register would allow the CFTC to impose KYC, AML, and market integrity rules before-the-fact rather than chasing violations after they occur.

**Hyperliquid's Response: On-Chain Transparency as a Regulatory Solution**

Hyperliquid's policy center countered by highlighting the exchange's innovations. On-chain transparency—every transaction published in real-time on the blockchain—creates a uniquely hostile environment for insider trading and price manipulation. Regulators and law enforcement can see the entire transaction history instantly, faster than traditional trading records. Additionally, 24/7 trading eliminates the gaps and discontinuities between legacy market hours, improving price discovery for all participants. Lukken acknowledges that these innovations are beneficial, but he argues they don't eliminate the need for regulation; the technology cannot ensure that only appropriate participants (those with proper risk awareness) are accessing leveraged products. Chris Perkins counters that Hyperliquid's on-chain data and architecture can actually support principles-based regulation focused on detecting and preventing misconduct.

**Three Options: Offshore, Onshore, or Decentralized**

The conversation identifies three distinct regulatory paths. First, Hyperliquid and similar venues could simply remain offshore and continue excluding US persons (though Perkins and Lukken both note concerns about "backdoor" access). Second, Hyperliquid could come onshore, registering with the CFTC as a designated contract market (DCM), a clearing house (DCO/CCP), and potentially a futures commission merchant (FCM). This would subject the exchange to traditional regulatory oversight but would require Hyperliquid to fundamentally restructure its decentralized architecture—an intermediary would need to take on accountability for market integrity and risk management. Third, Hyperliquid could remain decentralized and attempt to operate under principles-based regulation that focuses on participant behavior rather than entity control. Perkins finds the third option most interesting and most innovative; if decentralized technology can demonstrate it meets the same regulatory principles (no manipulation, no theft, financial integrity), regulators may not need to force centralization.

**The Intermediary Question: When Is an Intermediary Necessary?**

Perkins, drawing on his experience running one of the largest FCMs in the world, argues that intermediaries were crucial after 2008 because regulators needed "a throat to choke"—someone accountable when things go wrong. However, decentralized systems change this assumption. He distinguishes between margined, leveraged products (where intermediaries add value through risk management) and fully collateralized products (where settlement risk is minimal and the intermediary's role is merely administrative). Most of Hyperliquid's volume is fully collateralized; Perkins questions whether an FCM is necessary in that case. Lukken agrees that fully collateralized products don't require intermediaries, but he maintains that when leverage and margin are involved, the intermediary provides essential risk management and balance sheet protection. They converge on the idea that principles-based regulation could allow Hyperliquid to choose its own structure—intermediary or direct—as long as it meets regulatory outcomes.

**Risk Waterfalls and the Failure of Ad Hoc Liquidation**

A critical technical point emerges: Hyperliquid uses "auto-deleveraging" (ADL) to manage counterparty risk, but this places risk on the wrong party. Perkins spent years designing "risk waterfalls" in traditional derivatives—structures that ensure when you execute a hedge, it remains protected even if your counterparty fails. Instead of liquidating the hedge itself when a counterparty goes bankrupt, the waterfall ensures that risk is absorbed in a predetermined order, protecting the hedger. Hyperliquid inverted this by putting ADL (automatic liquidation of losing positions) at the bottom of the waterfall, meaning hedgers can lose their protection when they need it most. Perkins argues that decentralized technology could actually reconstitute these sophisticated risk waterfalls in a distributed way, potentially delivering better counterparty protection than centralized clearing.

**24/7 Trading: Technology vs. Infrastructure**

Chris Perkins argues that traditional exchanges should simply go 24/7 and compete. Walt Lukken counters that 24/7 trading requires more than just technology; payment rails, clearing systems, and the banking system all need to operate continuously. Right now, the back-office infrastructure—collateral settlement, margin calculations, risk management—doesn't run on weekends or after hours. However, Lukken notes that several innovations are already underway: tokenization, stablecoins, and projects like the Federal Reserve's FedNow are moving toward 24/7 payment capability. He points to ICE's partnership with OKX and CME's partnership with Google as examples of incumbents sharpening their pencils. The FIA published a paper on 24/7 trading; Lukken stresses the importance of doing it responsibly—rushing to 24/7 before clearing infrastructure is ready could leave hedgers vulnerable to illiquid markets on nights and weekends.

**The CFTC's Opportunity: The Clarity Act and Principles-Based Rule-Making**

CFTC Chair Michael Selig, himself a crypto lawyer, has publicly acknowledged that Hyperliquid could influence prices on registered futures markets and stated the CFTC's goal is to "onshore those markets." Perkins highlights that the Clarity Act includes a mandate for the CFTC and SEC to conduct rule-making on DeFi exchanges without necessarily requiring traditional intermediary structures. This rule-making offers a chance to explore whether principles-based regulation—focused on outcomes like preventing manipulation—can replace the entity-based control model that emerged after 2008. Lukken expresses optimism that the CFTC is willing to engage; the Commodity Exchange Act already allows outsourcing of functions to third parties as long as the regulated entity retains responsibility. A similar construct might apply to decentralized exchanges: someone or some set of protocols must be accountable, but that accountability might not require a single legal entity.

**Hyperliquid's Path: Education, Product Maturity, and Two Strategic Options**

If Hyperliquid's policy team in DC (led by Jake Chervinsky) wants to make a move, Perkins suggests two approaches. First, educate regulators about what a decentralized perpetuals DEX actually is, separate from the existing regulatory categories. Ensure the product itself—perpetual contracts—is not blocked by regulatory impediments. Then choose a direction: either argue for a decentralized pathway (US persons can connect to the technology layer without intermediaries, governed by principles-based oversight), or make a business decision to come onshore, select which licenses to pursue, and work with regulators on a tailored structure. The end goal would be "how do I service the US market in a way that allows legal 24/7 risk transfer while meeting principles on manipulation, counterparty risk, and financial integrity?"

**Prediction Markets: Identical Problem, Different Product**

Polymarket operates under the same offshore model as Hyperliquid and faces identical regulatory pressure. Lukken notes that prediction markets have a longer history; the CFTC approved the first one, HedgeStreet, in 2004, and has issued no-action letters for low-dollar prediction markets. What's new is scale: Polymarket has achieved massive volume through blockchain and has attracted institutional capital (ICE has invested $2 billion). The CFTC is preparing rule-making on prediction markets, and Lukken believes Polymarket should follow the same path as Hyperliquid: come onshore to bring credibility and ensure fair access. Chris counters that market manipulation is already illegal—the Commodity Exchange Act hasn't changed. He worries that regulation might focus on post-hoc enforcement rather than pre-emptive structure. Both agree that law enforcement prefers blockchain-based investigations due to transparency; regulatory rules should enable that instead of blocking it.

**The Broader Vision: Regulation Evolving to Meet Innovation**

Perkins frames this moment as the most interesting in 30 years for derivatives. The Pittsburgh communique of 2009 (G20) demanded transparency, clearing, and electronic execution for all derivatives—a mandate that required massive centralization because decentralized technology didn't exist. Now it does. If regulators can apply those same principles—visibility, counterparty risk mitigation, fair execution—but allow decentralized systems to prove they deliver on those goals, the market could evolve without requiring a regression to pre-2008 unregulated OTC markets or a lock-in to centralized monopolies. Lukken expresses hope that the CFTC will remain open-minded; the Clarity Act gives the agency a platform to start that dialogue. Both speakers converge on the idea that competition—whether from Hyperliquid, Polymarket, or other innovations—sharpens incumbent exchanges and makes the overall market stronger.

# The Ezra Klein Show — "How to End the Gerrymandering Doom Loop Forever"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Lee Drutman (senior fellow, political reform program, New America; author of *Breaking the Two-Party Doom Loop* (2020); advocate for proportional representation and multiparty democracy).

## TL;DR

- **Democrats lose 7-10 seats from recent redistricting** — Supreme Court's Calais decision gutted Voting Rights Act racial gerrymandering protections; Virginia's court-invalidated maps, combined with Texas mid-cycle redistricting and blue-state counter-gerrymandering, shift the 2026 House landscape substantially.
- **Mid-cycle redistricting has created escalation spiral** — Texas's aggressive 2025 redistricting (gaining ~5 GOP seats) triggered California to abandon its independent commission; states now ping-pong maps back and forth, disenfranchising minority-party voters everywhere.
- **Supreme Court is reducing competitive House elections to 15 out of 435** — 20 years ago there were roughly 50 competitive districts; partisan sorting + gerrymandering combined have created a near-total electoral lock where House outcomes are predetermined by geography.
- **Proportional representation eliminates gerrymandering risk** — If Louisiana were one 6-member district instead of six single-member districts, there would be "no lines to draw" and no possibility of manipulation; proportionality ensures opposition parties get representation even in winner-take-all states.
- **Proportional representation increases voter engagement** — Systems like those in Germany show 10–15 percentage point higher voter turnout because parties compete everywhere for every vote (not just swing districts); voters in safe districts know their votes matter.
- **Party system rigidity has destroyed intra-party ideological variance** — Senate election correlation with presidential results rose from 0.2 (2000) to 0.9 (2024); House correlation is now 0.98; liberal Republicans and conservative Democrats have vanished, collapsing political dimensionality into pure partisan binary.
- **Two-party system structure is the root cause, not the symptom** — Single-member districts mechanically produce two-party monopoly; third parties become "spoiler votes"; under proportional representation, a conservative party could emerge without throwing elections to Democrats, creating genuine coalitional competition.
- **Key barrier is incumbent self-interest** — Congress would vote to give up seats; solution is to expand House size alongside proportional representation (country is 3x larger than 1911 when House capped at 435) so incumbents don't lose seats.

**What Gerrymandering Is and Why the Supreme Court Enabled It**

Drutman explains that gerrymandering—the practice of manipulating district lines for partisan advantage—originates from 1812 when Massachusetts Governor Elbridge Gerry drew bizarrely shaped districts, one of which resembled a salamander. While the term describes line-drawing for partisan advantage, the real harm is disenfranchisement: voters are sorted such that the minority party in a state effectively cannot win, no matter how many votes they receive.

For decades, the Supreme Court prohibited the worst racial gerrymandering under Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act. But the 2019 Rucho decision refused to scrutinize partisan gerrymandering, declaring it a "political question" not for courts. Then the recent Calais Supreme Court decision gutted Voting Rights Act enforcement, essentially removing the bar for intentional racial gerrymandering—the standard is now so high (explicitly stating "I don't want Black people to vote") that proving it is nearly impossible.

**The Mid-Cycle Redistricting Arms Race and Its Consequences**

Under historical norms, districts were redrawn only after each decennial census. Trump changed that by encouraging Texas to redraw maps mid-cycle in 2025, yielding about five additional Republican seats. Gavin Newsom responded by dismantling California's independent redistricting commission via ballot initiative, allowing California to draw heavily Democratic maps. Virginia passed a similar measure but had its maps invalidated by state courts; the U.S. Supreme Court is currently ruling on whether those maps can stand.

The result is an escalating "maximum warfare" where blue states and red states take turns maximizing their advantages. Klein notes Democrats are estimated to lose 7–10 seats in 2026 from these shifts. More broadly, Southern states have begun targeting approximately 19 majority-minority districts held by Democrats post-Calais. Florida redraws from expected 20–8 Republican to 24–4. South Carolina is moving to 7–0 Republican. Tennessee is going all-Republican, eliminating the one Democratic district (Memphis).

Unless Congress bans mid-decade redistricting—which it likely won't—this ping-pong will continue into 2028 and 2030. Drutman observes that the term Hakeem Jeffries uses, "maximum warfare everywhere all the time," describes the transformation of the House into something like the Electoral College: the party controlling the state legislature maximizes its representation regardless of the actual vote share.

**Competitive House Elections Have Collapsed**

Twenty years ago, roughly 50 House races were genuinely competitive. Today the latest analysis suggests only 15 out of 435 are meaningful toss-ups. Drutman attributes this to two factors: gerrymandering itself, and "partisan sorting," the geographic divergence of Democrats and Republicans over 20 years. Blue dog Democrats vanished from Republican-trending districts; liberal Republicans disappeared from Democratic-trending ones. Presidential vote correlation with Senate elections has risen from 0.2 (2000) to 0.9 (2024); House correlation is now 0.98.

This collapse in electoral diversity means individual candidate quality matters almost nothing. John Tester in Montana, despite generational talent, cannot outperform the Democratic Party's vote share in a Republican state. The very last outliers like Joe Manchin are vanishing. Klein observes this is an argument Drutman makes: moderation might be worth a few points, but the ability to diverge from party has weakened dramatically.

**Proportional Representation: The Core Fix**

Drutman advocates proportional representation (PR), where a party gets seats in a legislature in direct proportion to the share of votes it receives. Instead of New York State's 26 single-member districts, imagine three larger districts—one with 8 members, one with 9, one with 9—where if Republicans get 50% of the vote, their top four candidates go to Congress, and Democrats get the other four.

The immediate benefit for gerrymandering: if Louisiana were one 6-member proportional district, there would be no district lines to draw and therefore no possibility of manipulation. Even in larger states like California, where lines still exist, the consequence of partisan line-drawing changes. If Republicans win 40% in a five-member district, they get two seats. This means "anything over 50 percent gives you 100 percent" disappears; results are forced into proportionality within districts.

Beyond gerrymandering, proportional representation fundamentally changes electoral incentives. In a safe Democratic district, Democrats have no reason to appeal to skeptical voters—they're already at 60%, 70%. In PR, every vote matters. Parties must compete for voters on the margins. This drives higher voter turnout (10–15 percentage points higher in systems like Germany) and draws underrepresented groups into the electorate because parties have incentive to seek them out.

**The Collapse of Ideological Diversity and the "Two-Party Doom Loop"**

Klein presses Drutman on a deeper concern: the two-party system itself may be broken, not just gerrymandering. Drutman responds with the "maximalist case" for PR. In 1965, when the Voting Rights Act passed, both liberal and conservative Republicans supported it; liberal and conservative Democrats split. The parties contained ideological breadth. Over three decades, that structure has collapsed. Liberal Republicans (John Lindsay, Jacob Javits) and conservative Democrats (Southern Democrats before the realignment) vanished. Both parties are now internally rigidly aligned.

This rigidity traps voters. Progressive Democrats feel the party is too corporate; moderate Democrats feel progressives are destroying the party; blue dogs can't run independently because spoiler-vote logic means they help Republicans. Under single-member districts, third parties cannot gain representation. The two major parties maintain a monopoly because any vote for an alternative is "wasted." Trump's takeover of the Republican Party exemplifies the trap: dissatisfied Republicans had no alternative party and no way to credibly challenge him without handing power to Democrats.

Proportional representation breaks this. If you need only 20% in a five-member district to win a seat (not 51%), a conservative alternative to Trump could emerge and actually represent conservative voters without being a spoiler. Similarly, progressives could run as a distinct party and build power without being locked in the "vote Democratic or get Trump" binary. This creates genuine coalitional competition—parties must negotiate and compromise rather than entrench in permanent enmity.

Klein raises skepticism by pointing to multiparty democracies' current struggles: the UK's far-right Reform Party, Israel's extreme coalition, Germany's AFD surge. Drutman counters that those aren't failures of multiparty systems per se; they're either running under wrong electoral mechanics (the UK still uses first-past-the-post single-member districts, which combined with multiparty is the worst system) or, in Germany's case, the AFD is kept out of government coalitions by design. He notes Brazil's multiparty system actually enabled the jailing of Bolsonaro after his coup attempt because coalition partners could abandon him; the U.S. Republican Party couldn't push back against Trump because the binary psychology of two-party politics made defection equivalent to handing power to "the enemy."

**Practical Barriers and Political Pathways**

Klein notes the core political problem: current House members would have to vote to eliminate safe seats. Drutman proposes expanding the House size by 150 members (from 435) alongside implementing PR. The country is more than three times larger than in 1911 when the House was capped at 435; representation per member is now 765,000 constituents. Expansion allows existing incumbents to keep their seats while districts are redrawn into multimember proportional ones.

Historically, systems that have shifted to PR did so when (1) public dissatisfaction with the status quo was intense and visible, (2) there was agreement on an alternative, and (3) politicians saw electoral advantage in changing. Drutman argues Democrats could make this case: the 2030 midterms will be brutal under current rules; every subsequent redistricting will worsen the spiral. Alternatively, a Democratic presidential candidate running on systemic reform—campaign finance, Supreme Court term limits, PR—could make this a centerpiece.

He also argues Democrats internally should embrace this: progressives could run as progressives, moderates as moderates, blue dogs as blue dogs, competing within a coalition rather than obliterating each other in primaries. This could actually address the institutional failures Klein observes in center-left parties worldwide—they're poorly led because they've become undifferentiated muddled coalitions with no way to signal distinct visions.

**Books and Closing Thoughts**

Drutman recommends Lonnie Guinier's *Tyranny of the Majority* for its argument that proportional representation serves minority communities better than majority-rule systems; Sam Huntington's *American Politics: Promise and Disharmony* for its thesis that Americans reform institutions in roughly 60-year cycles and we are due (last major reform was the 1960s); and William Gaddis's novel *The Recognitions* (1955) about authenticity and forgery in an era of AI and epistemological crisis.

Klein and Drutman close on the Madisonian point: the U.S. was designed for multiplicity of factions constantly recoalescing, not two permanent entrenched coalitions. Proportional representation isn't a silver bullet but a structural fix that would restore genuine electoral competition, re-enfranchise voters in safe districts, and force parties to govern in a way that represents broader coalitions rather than pure partisan maximization.

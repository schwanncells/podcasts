# Time To Say Goodbye — "A New History of Radicalism, Organizing, and the Jewish Bund plus AI Robs Artists Again with Molly Crabapple"

**Hosts:** Jay and Tyler (hosts of Time To Say Goodbye, described as "two grouchy dudes at fancy magazines"). **Guests:** Molly Crabapple (artist, activist, author; *Here We Live Is Our Country: The Story of the Jewish Bund*, out April 7, 2026; longtime illustrator and anti-AI advocate).

## TL;DR

- **The Jewish Bund is a 7-years-in-the-making personal and political history** — Crabapple started the book before COVID, before Ukraine, before the second Trump term, and before Gaza; she wrote ~1,000 pages and cut 600 of them, and learned Yiddish to complete it.
- **Her great-grandfather Samuel Rothport, a bohemian painter from Belarus, was the entry point** — a painting of a woman throwing a rock labeled "It Go the Bundist" on the back sent Crabapple into years of research; she found him "the most important clue, the doorway."
- **The Bund's closest American analog is the Black Panther Party** — both Marxist groups from a racialized "other," both running free breakfast programs, eviction defense, DIY schools, and armed self-defense while insisting on internationalist solidarity.
- **Crabapple's critique of the contemporary left: too "grad school pilled"** — the Bund in interwar Poland ran labor unions, teen movements, summer camps, a tuberculosis sanatorium, the most popular sports club in the country, and clubhouses with music and dancing alongside organizing; the left wins when it builds that kind of irresistible culture.
- **The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising is the book's moral crux** — the Polish Home Army gave the Bund 50 guns but refused to let the uprising spread to the rest of Warsaw, and as a result all their friends burned alive on the other side of the wall while people watched from the street; Crabapple argues solidarity didn't fail because Polish socialists were evil but because the conditions for two groups were structurally different.
- **Zionism wins hegemony through the betrayal of solidarity** — Crabapple frames Zionism as a "philosophy of profound cynicism" that took traumatized refugees and "promised them redemption through violence," and argues it is the only way to understand how a violent supremacist ideology becomes dominant — "the only fucking thing that can save us" from that outcome is human solidarity across difference.

- **Public AI approval is "rock bottom"** — bipartisan opposition to data centers (electricity bills tripling, farmland loss, noise) is Crabapple's evidence that regular people aren't buying Silicon Valley's promises; she compares the AI hype cycle to every prior broken tech promise since social media.

**Origins: A Painter's Grandson and a Rock Through a Window**

Crabapple grew up obsessed with her great-grandfather Samuel Rothport, a bohemian painter who came to America from Volkovisk, Belarus in 1904. Rothport left notes that said "reject spineless authority," went bankrupt trying to run a no-kill egg farm during the Depression, and once took a painting back from the Brooklyn Museum because he didn't like where they hung it. Among his roughly 600 paintings of Volkovisk life — the rabbi reading Torah, himself setting a rabbi's shoes on fire as a child, women lighting Shabbos candles — was one of a young woman in a Gibson-girl hairstyle throwing a rock through a window, with her boyfriend offering her more rocks. On the back, Rothport had written: "It Go the Bundist." That painting sent Crabapple into years of research.

The project accelerated after a 2015 trip to Palestine with the Palestine Festival of Literature. Crabapple saw "what life was like in this prison, this repeatedly shelled, deliberately impoverished prison that Israel had made" — and also how cool people were, how they tried to make life beautiful despite it. Afterward she discovered that the Bund was anti-Zionist. "Not only is this cool revolutionary underground group that my beloved great-grandfather was part of, but it's also against the project that locked my friends in Gaza in a cage. That's interesting." She wrote an article about the Bund in 2018, old people whose parents and grandparents were members got in touch with stories, and the book was underway.

**Process: A Thousand Pages, Yiddish, and the Cullman Fellowship**

Crabapple describes the writing process as a "form of madness." She wrote ~1,000 pages and cut 600. She rewrote the book roughly 15 times, and read the entire manuscript aloud to her boyfriend at least five or six times. She learned Yiddish to write it — a language so underserved that researchers must use a dictionary called Wörterbuch that charges per lookup. She traveled to Ukraine during the war to visit Bund locations, went to Lithuania and other Eastern European countries, and translated entire 100-page Yiddish pamphlets found only in archives just to understand what people said. "I would spend like a week chasing up some fact for a sentence that I would then cut."

The bulk of the writing happened after she received the Cullman Center Fellowship at the New York Public Library starting in September 2023 — which she describes as "the best writer's fellowship in the world" because it provides enough money not to work, access to 50 million books on demand, and a panopticon of glass-walled offices that docks your stipend if you don't come in. She spent 10 months there, eating fruit trays and fun-sized potato chip bags, chasing footnotes while simultaneously participating in pro-Palestine protests happening downstairs and trying to raise money for friends in Gaza.

**The Bund as Organizing Model: Irresistible Culture vs. Grad School Leftism**

The hosts describe the book as structured like a spy novel — a long cast of characters, country to country — and Crabapple's closest American analog for the Bund is the Black Panther Party. Both are Marxist groups from a racialized "other," insisting on internationalist solidarity while fighting specifically for their own group's safety and dignity. Both ran free breakfast programs and eviction defense, built DIY schools, championed their culture's beauty in countries that wanted them dead, and had literal militias — "a bunch of like young men and women looking really sexy and cool in berets that are put at a slant."

In interwar Poland, the Bund had labor unions, children's movements modeled on socialist boy and girl scouts, summer camps, a sanatorium for tubercular slum kids, the most popular sports club in the country, theatrical troupes, women's movements for free childcare, and clubhouses where music, dancing, pamphlet-pounding, and strike-organizing happened simultaneously in different rooms. "When you read the memoirs and they talk about the energy in their clubhouses... you just fall in love with it."

Crabapple's critique of the contemporary American left — including her own DSA, to which she has belonged for 10 years — is that it is too "grad school pilled." Her boyfriend looked at some DSA material and asked: "Isn't summer school a punishment?" The Bund shows a model that appealed to "all sorts of very, very different types of people." She argues the left wins when it stops being so graduate student-coded and builds the kind of thick, irresistible culture that helps people raise their families and have fun, not just theorize — pointing to Zohran Mamdani's mayoral campaign as a recent example of the left doing this right.

**Identity, Solidarity, and the Path Beyond Woke 1.0**

Both hosts engage at length with the Bund's model of identity politics. The Bund's demand, as Crabapple frames it, was: "We want to live as ourselves." Not to change their names to Ivan, not to pretend to be Russian, but also not to practice a politics based on "endless self-denunciations" directed at the Polish or Russian majority. They just thought "different cultures are cool." Crabapple contrasts this with what she calls the inauthenticity of Woke 1.0 — citing BIJOC (Black and Indigenous Jews of Color, Sephardi and Mizrahi) as an example of an invented acronym with no community behind it, versus the Syrian Jewish community in Midwood, who have specific stores, specific holidays, a real social infrastructure.

Jay observes that one lesson from the book is giving a pathway out of the identity politics debate — that some of the most powerful organizing is always going to come from identity, especially when the violence is itself identity-based. When ICE is rounding up every Latino person at an airport, refusing to name racism out of fear of "sounding woke" is a kind of "constipation of action." Crabapple agrees: the left's hyper-theoretical language has made it impossible for many people to say obvious things.

**The Warsaw Ghetto and the Origins of Zionism**

The book's most morally demanding section, which the hosts press Crabapple to explain, is her treatment of how the Bund's faith in solidarity was tested and shattered during the Nazi occupation — and what that failure explains about Zionism.

Pre-war Poland, Crabapple notes, already had a military government whose main social project was trying to deport 3.3 million Jewish citizens — to Madagascar, to Palestine — before the Nazis invaded. During the occupation, the Polish Home Army (the largest resistance group in occupied Europe) received desperate requests from the Bund and allied groups for weapons to defend the Warsaw Ghetto. They gave 50 guns but refused to allow the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising to spread to the rest of the city — because the Soviets were still at Stalingrad and the Americans hadn't landed in North Africa, and an uprising would mean the whole city would be destroyed without allies close enough to help. The strategic logic was not evil, Crabapple says, but the result was that the ghetto burned while people watched from the street, just "as close as one New York building is to another."

"The Bund's whole philosophy depends on this faith in human solidarity... And that solidarity failed. It fucking shattered and failed during the Nazi occupation." She argues this is how Zionism — a "philosophy of profound cynicism" that divides the world into the strong and the weak — wins hegemony. Not because people are evil, but because it took traumatized refugees whose solidarity networks had collapsed and "promised them redemption through violence," in the same way the Taliban took traumatized refugees in camps and did the same. Her moral conclusion: "If you don't want psychopathic, violent, supremacist ideologies to take over, the only thing that is the defense against that is human solidarity across difference."

**AI and the Theft of Artists' Work**

In 2022, Crabapple began seeing images that looked like bad versions of her own work appearing when people prompted Midjourney to generate "Syrian street in the style of Molly Crabapple." She wrote her first piece against generative AI for the LA Times that year. She argues illustrators were the canary in the coal mine: a small, close-knit, precarious field with no insurance, where workers "put out" their style publicly to build careers, only to have megacorporations "take all of this work... and use it as training data for these disgusting slot machines whose goal was so clearly to replace and impoverish us."

The Carla Ortiz lawsuit — brought by Ortiz and two other illustrators — is still working through the courts. Crabapple notes the absurdity that you can still prompt AI to generate work "in the style of Molly Crabapple" and it produces recognizably similar output: "If you show that to any jury, they'd be like, oh yeah, obvious." The corporate strategy, she says, is "crimes too big to fail" — the implicit argument being that if they can't steal, they can't make money, so society must accept the theft to access the technology.

**Shaming Companies, Tech Journalism's Credulity, and the Data Center Backlash**

Crabapple argues that shaming companies is the most effective near-term tool creative workers have: "The only way that we're going to have any fucking segment of the market for us to work in as creative people is because companies were shamed out of using AI." She points to a journalism festival in early 2023 where paid AI company representatives told journalists they'd be "like a horse and buggy maker" if they didn't adopt the products — and many reporters accepted this framing uncritically. She describes tech journalism's deference to AI CEOs as a failure: "The man selling the magic box tells you the magic box is gonna do magic things, and then you're just gonna report that as though he's a neutral arbiter of fact."

Crabapple identifies a consistent Silicon Valley playbook: "This is going to facilitate human connection" — Facebook, smartphones, and now AI — followed in each case by broken promises, data theft, and damage to the social fabric. She cites bipartisan opposition to data centers as evidence that regular people aren't buying it: data centers are being built in rural areas where both Republican and Democratic residents object to electricity bills tripling, noise, and loss of farmland. "People do not want to give up their farm so people can make memes of Ted Cruz with boobs."

On loneliness and atomization, Crabapple argues that AI products that reduce human contact — AI therapists, AI customer service, AI voices posing as dermatologist receptionists — make the underlying problem worse, not better. Jay describes having Claude do his fantasy baseball draft for him and then feeling that "sucked" because the social element was the whole point.

**Political Realignment: Pro-Human vs. Anti-Human**

Tyler raises Matt Walsh's prediction that AI will cause a political reshuffling into "pro-human versus anti-human" coalitions, cutting across existing left-right lines. Crabapple agrees with the broad framing but notes a critical implication: any effective anti-AI pushback must be anti-corporate, because "AI is being shoved down our throats by bajillion dollar megacorporations." The right talks economic populism but doesn't deliver it. A real coalition might include conservative Catholics listening to the Pope's critique of AI as contrary to human dignity alongside "cool anarcho-communists," but it would have to be willing to challenge large corporations — which narrows the field.

Crabapple's ultimate critique of the major AI figures (Sam Altman, who she notes started with a geomapping Yelp app and is not an AI researcher; Elon Musk) is that they are "so bizarre, freakish, and socially inept that they have no ability to filter themselves at all" — trapped in a bubble of fellow machine-god believers, unable to understand how they sound to ordinary people. She sees that as the movement's saving grace.

# The New Yorker Radio Hour — "Is Cuba Trump's Next Target?"

**Host:** David Remnick. **Guests:** Jon Lee Anderson (New Yorker staff writer; author of a biography of Che Guevara; has traveled to Cuba extensively and recently visited in the wake of the Venezuela invasion); Ada Ferrer (professor of history, Princeton University; Pulitzer Prize winner for *Cuba: An American History*; left Cuba as a child in 1963; her memoir *Keeper of My Kin* is due out in May).

## TL;DR

- **Trump has openly mused about "taking Cuba"** — saying "I think I can do anything I want with it" and noting Cuba is "a very weakened nation right now," with the U.S. having effectively cut off its oil supply and caused a near-total grid blackout.
- **Removing President Díaz-Canel would accomplish nothing** — Anderson says Díaz-Canel is a "party apparatchik" with no real power; Cuba is still controlled from behind the scenes by the elderly Raúl Castro and his family. Díaz-Canel's re-election slogan was "continuity," a word Anderson notes "doesn't appeal to people under 30."
- **Up to 20% of Cuba's population has emigrated since 2021** — primarily young people and skilled workers (doctors driving Uber in Miami, cardiac specialists pushing the elderly in care homes). Anderson describes walking through Havana and three interior towns and finding them "just empty."
- **Cuba has had no fuel for three months** — the U.S. blocked Venezuelan oil after capturing Maduro on January 3rd; the national grid has collapsed repeatedly, sometimes for nearly a week. Cuba has only a "few days of oil supplies left."
- **Marco Rubio's strategy is economic dependency, not outright regime change** — Anderson describes the U.S. package as: open to private investment and enterprise (the sweetener), then receive fuel — but at a price that converts Cuba into a "neo-dependency" of the United States, as Venezuela now is. The Cuban government is very unlikely to accept the political succession component, since accepting it would mean "negotiating themselves out of existence."
- **Cuba's strategic leverage: the Iran quagmire and the threat of chaos** — Anderson argues Cuba's negotiators are watching the U.S. get bogged down in the Iran war and see it as an advantage; they plan to "spin out the negotiations." The regime can also credibly threaten the U.S. with instability: chaos 90 miles away means a potential re-immigration flood.
- **Ferrer: there is no such thing as a "friendly takeover" of Cuba** — the full history of U.S.-Cuba relations is one of repeated intervention: a military occupation after 1898, the Platt Amendment giving the U.S. the right of intervention, the CIA-backed Bay of Pigs invasion in 1961. She notes that in 1961, Kennedy White House officials were "slapping themselves on the back saying Bobby Kennedy was going to be the next mayor of Havana" — a parallel to Trump's current posturing.
- **The Obama opening was the most hopeful moment in modern Cuban history** — Ferrer was in Havana during the visit and describes an electricity of hope she had never previously seen; one older woman told her she watched Obama on television and referred to him as "my president." Trump has reversed Cuba's removal from the state sponsors of terrorism list and is deporting Cubans under Biden's humanitarian parole program.

**Who Actually Runs Cuba, and What Ousting Díaz-Canel Would Mean**

Anderson describes Díaz-Canel as a creditable but uninspiring "party apparatchik" and "front man" who defers almost entirely to the elderly Raúl Castro, who continues to control the regime from behind the scenes — Anderson draws an analogy to the way Iranian presidents defer to the supreme leader. Díaz-Canel won re-election three years ago and has two years left in office; he is due to leave by April 2028. Anderson's assessment of removing him: "zip," because he has no real power. For Trump, Anderson says, the logic is the same simplistic brand as Venezuela: get rid of the visible top guy, declare victory.

**Cuba's Collapse and the Scale of Emigration**

Anderson lived in Cuba in the early 1990s when the Soviet Union collapsed and Cuba lost its primary economic patron. He describes that period as terrible — Cubans went from driving cars to bicycles, from tractors to oxen, and the average Cuban lost significant body weight, with a spike in suicides. Venezuela stepped in, providing oil in exchange for Cuban expertise in medicine, education, sports instruction, and intelligence services — an arrangement that lasted until Maduro's capture in January. The current crisis, Anderson says, closely parallels that early-90s period: long blackouts (the national grid collapsed for nearly a week in October), no gasoline on the market, no fuel for three months. Since 2021, anywhere up to 20% of the population has left — primarily young people and skilled workers. Anderson visited Havana and three interior cities and was "struck by the emptiness."

Ferrer adds texture to the emigration picture, describing a complete privatization of basic survival: a cousin in Cuba in December 2024 needed hip surgery and the family had to source the prosthesis on the underground market themselves, bring their own medicine, and provide their own sheets because the hospital could not. A poet friend who told Ferrer for years he would never leave is now living in Madrid.

**Rubio's Strategy: Dependency, Not Liberation**

Anderson describes the U.S. package being negotiated as a two-part structure: a sweetener (economic opening to private investment, allowing the regime to save face) plus the requirement that Cuba become a dependency of the United States — receiving Venezuelan oil, but now paying for it rather than getting it free. Trump's framing gives away the underlying dynamic, Anderson notes: when Trump spoke about Cuba, "he didn't say we, the United States. He said I. It's all about him." Rubio's approach is more sophisticated than Anderson expected, but it still carries what he calls an "imperialist veneer" — the same logic used with Venezuela, now extended to Cuba. Anderson is skeptical it will hold beyond Trump. "This arrogance, a hubris, a kind of denialism about history and human nature, and a lack of knowledge about other countries and their past" has been on full display in the Iran intervention.

**Cuba's Negotiating Position**

Despite the fuel crisis, Anderson argues Cuba has more leverage than it might appear. The regime has spent nearly 70 years developing strategies to survive American containment, hostility, and negotiations, and "usually bested them." The Iran quagmire benefits Cuba: the longer the U.S. is bogged down there, the better Cuba's negotiating position. The regime can "spin out the negotiations," make economic concessions while holding firm on political succession, and wait. Their ultimate leverage is the chaos threat: internal unrest and economic collapse 90 miles from Florida could produce a flood of migrants to the U.S. that Washington has no interest in managing.

**The History of U.S. Intervention and Why "Liberation" Is a Hollow Frame**

Ferrer offers a rapid tour of the history: in the 1820s the U.S. specifically sought to prevent Cuban independence; in the 1850s, American actors invaded Cuba to liberate it from Spain and attach it to the U.S. as slave states; in 1898, the U.S. intervened in Cuba's 30-year independence struggle, occupied the island for nearly four years, and left only after Cuba accepted the Platt Amendment granting the U.S. the right of intervention; the CIA-backed Bay of Pigs invasion followed the 1959 revolution. The phrase "free Cuba" has always had a particular American meaning that diverges sharply from Cuban self-determination.

**Cuban-Americans and the Shifting Politics of the Diaspora**

Ferrer challenges the monolithic "conservative Cuban-American" stereotype. Most Cuban-born people in Miami arrived recently — they grew up under the Communist system, recited poems about Che Guevara in school, and experienced the accelerating crisis of the past five or six years firsthand. This means the support for Trump-Rubio action in Cuba is not primarily from older exiles who lost property in 1959; it is from recent arrivals who are desperate for any change. At the same time, Ferrer notes that Trump's deportation of Cubans under Biden's humanitarian parole program — including her own cousin, who is detained in what she calls "Alligator Alcatraz" — was generating real backlash before the Venezuela invasion. She heard from Miami political figures that Trump was "going too far." That sentiment quieted after Venezuela.

On the Obama opening: Ferrer describes unprecedented excitement on the streets of Havana. An older Cuban woman told her she watched Obama's speech and referred to him as "my president" — a phrase that captures how starved Cubans were for any signal that the 67-year hostility might end. "Now seems the polar opposite," she says, and the anxiety in Cuba today is layered — not just about Trump and Rubio, but about 36-hour blackouts, hospitals sending patients home because they can't perform surgeries, and a complete absence of any confidence that the current government can solve anything.

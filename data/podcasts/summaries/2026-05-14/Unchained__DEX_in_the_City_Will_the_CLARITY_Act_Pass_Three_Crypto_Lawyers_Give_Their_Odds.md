# Unchained — "DEX in the City: Will the CLARITY Act Pass? Three Crypto Lawyers Give Their Odds"

**Host:** Katherine KK (TradFi background, Starkware). **Guests:** Josh Riesman (Chief Legal and Strategy Officer, GSR; former Deutsche Bank and Soctgen; Circle regulatory lead); V (legal/crypto strategy background, VEDA).

## TL;DR

- **New 300+ page CLARITY Act draft released this morning** — the crypto market structure bill with bipartisan Senate support moved to committee markup this week; the goal is Senate passage before Memorial Day recess, then House reconciliation.
- **Developer protections strengthened with "specific intent" language** — the revised Blockchain Regulatory Certainty Act (BRCA) now requires prosecutors to prove developers acted with specific intent to help others launder funds, not just knew bad actors might abuse the tool.
- **Three lawyers give passage odds: Josh 45%, V 90%, KK 35%** — higher than a month ago (Josh was 30%, V was 60%, KK was 25%), citing bipartisan support and regulatory alignment; main blockers are Democratic ethics concerns and bank lobbying on yield provisions.
- **TradFi institutions are aggressively hiring and integrating crypto infrastructure** — Morgan Stanley, JP Morgan, and BlackRock are moving billions into digital assets; the New Morgan Stanley Bitcoin ETF saw hundreds of millions in immediate demand; this is not about disruption but about new revenue channels and using blockchain rails to improve settlement speed.
- **Chair Atkins' speech signals SEC alignment with Congress on modernizing regulation** — the SEC is proposing new rules for decentralized infrastructure (vaults, software-based clearing) rather than forcing old intermediary definitions onto blockchain systems; this represents 180-degree shift from enforcement-first approach.
- **"Crypto Spring" is maturing institutional adoption, not retail casino** — M&A season active (MoonPay/Deflow, Bullish/Equinti, Kraken/Reap), massive capital raises (A16Z, Han Ventures, Circle's $222M, ARK presale $3B), and public companies like Circle issuing tokens—confidence is high that clarity will pass and infrastructure will follow.
- **Regulators face the hard problem of technology-neutral protection without disadvantaging legacy players** — CME and legacy exchanges worry blockchain platforms will be regulated less expensively and draw their volume; regulators must ensure customer protection and market integrity across both intermediated and decentralized systems while respecting capability differences.

**The CLARITY Act and Developer Protections**

Katherine introduces the newly released draft of the CLARITY Act, the comprehensive crypto market structure legislation that has been holding bipartisan support but faces hurdles from Democrats on ethics grounds and from larger banks on yield provisions. The next step is committee markup this week, where members will vote on amendments, followed by full Senate debate. For the bill to move forward, it must advance before the Memorial Day recess so the Senate can vote and then reconcile with the House version.

Josh Riesman notes that despite the bank lobby's previous intransigence on stablecoin yield—a provision already addressed in previous versions—the current draft represents meaningful progress. There is "serious bipartisan engagement" in Congress around crypto market structure, and at a high level, Riesman assesses passage odds as "better than 50% still," though he acknowledges "we still got a lot of work to do." Democrats remain a wildcard because they will not gift Trump an easy win on crypto policy; they will push for an ethics provision to address conflicts of interest within the executive and judicial branches.

A key technical improvement comes in the revised BRCA language on developer liability. Katherine highlights a change that requires prosecutors to prove "specific intent"—meaning the developer must have intended for bad actors to use the tool for money laundering, not merely known they could. This is a higher bar than general intent. V explains the distinction: intent is the mental state to perform an act; specific intent is the mental state to bring about a particular result. Creating a privacy tool knowing it might be misused is not specific intent; specific intent requires that enabling abuse was the purpose. V notes this distinction is critical because it shifts the burden from mere knowledge to affirmative desire, matching how prosecutors approached the Tornado Cash case (where they alleged Roman Storm and others *created* the tool specifically to facilitate money laundering). Riesman observes that this protection has moved the developer advocacy community closer to supporting the bill, whereas months ago it was not there.

**Polling on Passage Odds**

The three hosts offer their latest bets on passage. Katherine (KK) puts herself at 35% passage probability, up from 25% a month prior but still cautious; she notes that even if the bill is good policy, many things must align perfectly for momentum to hold. V is significantly more bullish at 90%, up from 60%, citing real improvements in the draft and a sense that "all the pieces are falling into place." Josh is at 45%, up from 30%, motivated by evidence of bipartisan support and major institutional capital ready to deploy. Josh's main concern is political: Democrats will face pressure not to hand Trump a legislative victory on his signature tech policy, even if the bill is sound. The two main blockers Josh identifies are continued bank lobby resistance and the ethics provision that Democrats will demand. KK emphasizes that she desperately wants the bill to pass—in three years, if it fails, she'll be forced back to law firm work defending sued crypto companies for $10 million a year—but realistic political dynamics suggest a lower odds baseline.

**TradFi's Embrace of Digital Assets**

Josh addresses a common misconception: that banks oppose crypto because of competition. The reality is the opposite. At Consensus 2026 (the industry's flagship conference in Miami), the landscape has shifted dramatically. Where the main floor once featured DeFi protocols like Uniswap, it now features JP Morgan, Morgan Stanley, and BlackRock with some of the largest booths. These institutions are not staying away from crypto—they are "licking their lips" with excitement.

Josh points to two drivers. First, new revenue streams: the Morgan Stanley Bitcoin ETF attracted hundreds of millions of dollars immediately out of the gate because Morgan Stanley has a distribution pipeline to wealthy clients. Second, and more important in TradFi minds, blockchain rails improve traditional finance infrastructure itself. Settlement speed is a major operational cost and risk; blockchain enables near-instant settlement. Trading velocity increases; asset creation becomes more flexible. Smaller banks and institutions will struggle to compete in this new environment, which is why some have lobbied against the bill. But major global financial institutions are already pivoting to digital assets as a strategic growth area.

Katherine observes that Bitcoin has always had the attributes of a successful currency except one: legal and regulatory clarity. With CLARITY Act passage, that final puzzle piece would unlock broader TradFi adoption. KK and Josh both attended Consensus and noted the presence of "suit jackets" and "vests" everywhere—the visual signal of traditional finance's arrival in the crypto conference space.

---

**GSR's Two Core Tests for the Bill**

Josh outlines GSR's philosophy on what a good CLARITY Act should accomplish. First, the bill must position the United States as a center for innovation in the blockchain industry. Second, it must create conditions for deep, robust crypto markets onshore, drawing liquidity that is currently happening offshore. The fact that most spot and derivatives volume in crypto happens outside the US, despite the US being the largest capital market globally, is abnormal and represents a missed opportunity.

To achieve these goals, the bill must clarify the line between securities and commodities so market participants know what they can and cannot do without accidentally running afoul of the law. Initial drafts introduced the concept of an "ancillary asset"—the idea that when a crypto asset is issued, the asset itself can be separated from the offer and sale. This is crucial because secondary market trading (people buying and selling after issuance) must have clear rules. Does a token that was a security at launch remain one in secondary trading, or can it become a commodity? Josh notes that GSR spent millions on Howey analysis of tokens under the previous regulatory regime and still had no clarity—the analysis was highly subjective and based only on public information. Any regulatory outcome that requires third parties to perform subjective analysis is not scalable. The industry needs to know upfront, ideally from a rule or statutory statement, what the status is. Josh gives the example of the commodity futures market, where certain index products can switch between commodity and security status depending on market conditions, but this switching is defined in the rules and predictable. In crypto, such switching happens unpredictably across the board, creating "tremendous vegetation" (vegetative growth of regulatory uncertainty).

**SEC Chair Atkins' Recent Speech and Regulatory Alignment**

V highlights Chair Atkins' recent remarks at the SEC's AI Plus Expo, which, despite the title, focused heavily on digital assets and blockchain infrastructure. The big theme for V is a conceptual shift: the SEC is moving toward modernizing securities regulation for on-chain and software-based markets through actual rulemaking, not enforcement. This is a 180-degree turn from the previous regime that tried to stretch old definitions of exchange, broker, and clearing agency to fit blockchain systems.

Atkins specifically talked about revisiting how categories like "exchange" and "broker" apply—or don't apply—to decentralized systems. For years, the industry has been frustrated that these categories were designed for intermediary-based markets, and the SEC tried to force blockchain into them anyway. Now, there is a growing recognition that on-chain infrastructure can achieve the same goals (investor protection, market integrity, fairness, market conduct) through code instead of through intermediaries. V notes that this approach is consistent with the latest CLARITY draft.

Particularly exciting for V is that the SEC and Congress recognized vaults for the first time publicly—a decentralized custody system. This shows forward-looking, cutting-edge thinking about what decentralized, programmable, on-chain infrastructure can do. V is bullish because it feels like the SEC, the House Ag committee, the CFTC, and the White House are all "in lockstep" on the goal of creating smart regulatory frameworks for new technology.

**The Need for Congressional Education**

Josh emphasizes that most members of Congress are still at the Bitcoin-level of knowledge about crypto and blockchain. They don't know what vaults are, what VEDA Labs is, or what Starkware does. They still assume crypto is a casino or just another investment asset. It is incumbent on the industry to educate: Congress needs to understand that crypto is a parallel financial system being built mainly by US entrepreneurs, and the US should want to keep that innovation onshore rather than push it offshore.

Josh points to thought leadership from figures like Elli (Starkware CEO), who published a book written in plain English explaining cryptocurrency to non-experts—something Joshua says he could hand to a legislator to build understanding. Education efforts, in-person meetings in congressional offices, and sustained presence are critical to keeping the bill moving and preventing reversion to protectionist gridlock.

**Institutional Adoption and Crypto Spring**

Katherine shifts to a theme running through May 2026: "Crypto Spring," the sense that after winter, the ecosystem is maturing at an institutional level. Evidence includes a wave of M&A (MoonPay acquiring Deflow, Bullish acquiring Equinti, Payward/Kraken acquiring Reap), massive new fundraising rounds (A16Z and Han Ventures both announcing significant crypto funds, not AI), and a landmark event: Circle, a public company, is raising $222 million and launching an ARK token in a presale valued at $3 billion.

Josh contextualizes this as the "noisiest bear market in history." GSR has received more inquiry about real-world assets (RWA) in the past three months than in its entire history. Institutions want to know how to get on-chain. Years ago, the question was "should we build infrastructure?" Now it is "how do we bring the world on-chain?" This represents a fundamental shift from a nascent tech phase to a scale phase.

Katherine notes that while altcoin prices have been anemic, token-based fundraising is proven to be "one of the fastest and most accretive ways to raise capital." Projects that structure tokens correctly (combining utility with sustainable revenue models) will continue to use tokens as a cornerstone of funding. She contrasts the earlier crash of crypto-native companies that relied on token revenue alone with a new model where tokens are complementary to real business revenue.

---

**Regulatory Trade-Offs and Legacy Player Concerns**

Josh raises a hard problem regulators and Congress must solve: how to create new rules for new entrants (decentralized systems, blockchain-based infrastructure) without unfairly disadvantaging legacy players (CME, traditional brokers). If a decentralized platform faces less regulatory burden than a centralized futures exchange, volume will migrate, and the incumbents will feel the regulatory regime is unfair.

Josh stresses that regulators must ensure customer protection, market integrity, fairness, and market conduct are uniform across both intermediated and decentralized systems—that the customer receives "the same level of protection"—while acknowledging that code-based protections can achieve this differently from intermediary-based protections. The regulatory framework must be "technology neutral." V counters that "the incumbents are always free to innovate themselves"—regulation is not meant to protect business models, only customers and market integrity. If CME wants to compete with decentralized derivatives platforms, it should build blockchain-native clearing infrastructure, not lobby for protection.

**Crypto Good News Segment — AI for Pancreatic Cancer Detection**

In a lighter segment, Katherine deviates from crypto to spotlight an AI development: RedMod, a system that can detect pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (the most common and deadliest form of pancreatic cancer) by identifying faint tissue changes invisible on standard scans. Pancreatic cancer is notoriously hard to catch early, and this AI capability could dramatically improve survival rates. Katherine notes (humorously) that in crypto, "no one is dying" from policy errors, but emerging technologies like AI can literally save lives. She hopes one day to feature a crypto use case with similar life-saving potential.

# Code Switch — "Freedom from fatness: Why GLP-1s go after Black bodies"

**Hosts:** B.A. Parker and Gene Demby. **Guest:** Sabrina Strings (author of *Fearing the Black Body: The Racial Origins of Fat Phobia*; sociologist and researcher on the history of fat discrimination).

## TL;DR

- **GLP-1 marketing overwhelmingly targets Black women** — Ro's campaign features Serena Williams; billboards in Times Square depict older Black women; the pharmaceutical industry knows how algorithms sort users by body positivity content, then serves them weight loss ads.
- **BMI, the metric that defines "obesity," was invented by an astronomer with no medical training** — Adolphe Quetelet created it as a statistical distribution tool in the 1800s; eugenicist Charles Davenport weaponized it to identify "racial obesity"; fat-phobic researcher Ancel Keys renamed it "Body Mass Index" in the 1970s, adding false medical authority.
- **Fatness was originally sinned during the Protestant Reformation as a marker of corruption** — Fat monks became the visual archetype of religious hypocrisy; this Protestant imagery saturated print culture via the Gutenberg press and shaped anti-fat sentiment for 500 years.
- **Weight loss drugs are being sold as reparations for Black medical neglect** — Strings argues that pharmaceutical companies are saying: "You have limited healthcare access, so we'll give you access to *this* drug," conflating weight loss with systemic remedy and ignoring that Black people need actual healthcare, not just medications.
- **The moral contradiction is built in: fatness is shameful, but using a weight loss drug is also shameful (the "easy way out")** — Society insists overweight people should be naturally thin or work hard to become thin; when GLP-1s offer pharmacological solutions, the same moral framework punishes them for taking the shortcut.
- **Class determines who loses weight on GLP-1s** — With insurance, the drug costs ~$25/month; without it, ~$960/month; weight decline is faster among high-income women than other groups, not because they are biologically different but because they can afford the medication and have easier doctor access.
- **Black people's delay in seeking healthcare due to weight stigma creates worse health outcomes** — Doctors focus on weight loss so intensely that they miss diagnoses (cancers, organ failure); fat people avoid doctors or receive care-delaying lectures about weight before treatment of actual illness.
- **Lizzo and Serena Williams' weight loss felt like betrayal because they were symbols of body liberation, but individual choice does not erase the structural system enforcing their compliance** — Strings argues we must hold both bodily autonomy and structural critique: individuals can choose weight loss; the multi-billion-dollar industry can't simultaneously claim empathy while reshaping cultural messaging at scale.

**How GLP-1 Marketing Found a Target**

The episode opens with B.A. Parker describing her social media algorithm, which delivers an overwhelming volume of GLP-1 advertisements alongside her body positivity and fat liberation content. She observed that nearly every GLP-1 ad she saw featured Black women—Ro's Serena Williams campaign, AI-generated imagery of Black women receiving deliveries, a Times Square billboard stating "Obesity medication is now covered by Medicare" next to an image of an older, larger Black woman. Strings explains that this is not coincidence: the pharmaceutical industry studies algorithmic profiles. If someone follows body positivity creators, they likely read about fat liberation—and that data signal marks them as a profitable target for weight loss drugs. The industry is essentially saying: "We know you're trying to love yourself; here's a product to stop that."

**The Astro-Historical Origins of BMI and Fat Phobia**

Strings traces fatness back to the Protestant Reformation of the 16th century. During the shift from Catholicism to Protestantism in places like Germany, religious reformers (including Martin Luther) used the image of the fat monk as a visual symbol of corruption and hypocrisy. A fat monk represented someone who had accepted alms meant for the poor and kept them for himself. This archetypal image spread through printed materials—the Gutenberg press amplified it—and embedded fat-phobic sentiment into Western culture for five centuries.

The modern "obesity" metric has an equally surprising origin. BMI was invented by Adolphe Quetelet, a Belgian astronomer and statistician in the 1800s. Quetelet created the body mass index as a statistical tool to measure the *distribution* of height and weight across a population. He was not a physician; he had no interest in health outcomes. In the early 20th century, American eugenicist Charles Davenport took up Quetelet's index and rebranded it as a tool to identify "racial obesity." Davenport believed certain races were inherently obese and used the metric to support eugenic policies. The tool was renamed "Body Mass Index" by Ancel Keys, a fat-phobic researcher, in the 1970s—a name change that gave it medical authority it never had.

**The Contradictory Moral Framework Around Fatness**

Strings identifies a fundamental contradiction in how Western culture treats body weight. Fatness is treated as a moral failing—a sign of weakness, lack of discipline, or sin. Yet when individuals use pharmaceutical solutions to lose weight, they are also morally condemned for taking the "easy way out." The moral hierarchy, Strings explains, is: first, be naturally thin; second, work hard to become thin; third, at minimum, take medication rather than stay fat. Any outcome other than thinness is failure. When GLP-1s became widely available, the moral framework bent but didn't break—now people must take the drug, but using it is still implicitly shameful because it's perceived as sidestepping personal responsibility.

The advertising, however, hides this contradiction behind health language. Strings points out that GLP-1 ads always include the phrase "you deserve to feel great in your body," which assumes that a fat person does not feel great, cannot be healthy, and requires transformation. The ads then layer additional promises onto the supposed health benefit: confidence, sexiness, mobility. "How is it that a company selling you a weight loss drug is telling us about our self-esteem and confidence?" Strings asks. These things do not come from shrinking the body. Yet the industry has convinced people that there is a consumer solution to every problem: pay money, change your body, gain self-worth.

**Weight Loss Pharmaceuticals as False Reparations**

Strings argues that GLP-1 marketing to Black Americans is particularly insidious because it frames weight loss medication as a solution to structural inequality. Black people have historically had limited access to healthcare, been exploited in medical experiments, and continue to experience weight stigma and racism in clinical settings. The pharmaceutical industry's pitch is: "You don't have access to good healthcare, but we have *this* for you." Strings emphasizes: "Weight loss is not reparations. What we need is compassion and care and the understanding that people can be healthy at any size."

Black Americans also have disproportionately high rates of diabetes, creating a secondary vulnerability. Because nearly 50% of Black Americans are classified as obese and diabetes is prevalent in Black communities, there is a narrative that Black people urgently need weight loss. Strings notes that this creates a compounding stigma: Black people are targeted both for their size and for their choice to use medications marketed to address that size. The promise of the drug—that weight loss will cure diabetes and other illnesses—is scientifically overstated. Even if every American became thin, Strings argues, chronic illness would remain extraordinarily high. Thinness is the goal, not health.

**Healthcare Avoidance and Diagnostic Failure**

One of the most dangerous consequences of weight stigma is how it shapes clinical care. Strings describes that Black people routinely report experiencing weight stigma at the doctor's office. Doctors who are invested in a "fat-centric view of health" will instruct patients to lose weight before addressing their actual symptoms. This approach delays care; fat people sometimes stop going to doctors entirely because they know they will receive weight-loss lectures instead of treatment. There are documented cases of fat patients visiting a doctor with a serious illness—cancer, for example—only to be told to lose weight, and the cancer diagnosis is missed until the disease has advanced. When weight is at the forefront of medical analysis, fat people receive worse health outcomes, not better ones.

**The Paradox of Icons and Body Autonomy**

Gene Demby raises the case of Lizzo and Serena Williams, both of whom were celebrated as symbols of body liberation before both lost weight in recent years. Strings distinguishes between their cases. Lizzo's brand was explicitly about fat Black female confidence and sexuality; when she began losing weight and launched a shapewear brand, it felt like a reversal of her prior message. Many fans felt betrayed, not because Lizzo lost weight (that is her individual choice), but because her commercial strategy now contradicts her former stance.

Serena Williams' case is different. Williams has been subjected to public fat-shaming and physical scrutiny throughout her career, particularly racist and sexist commentary about her body. When she chose to use weight loss drugs, Strings reads it as an escape from that public shaming—finally complying with the thing people told her she should do. Both women are symbols, and their choices have ripple effects, but Strings emphasizes that they are also individuals who have endured relentless degradation. She offers compassion: "Even if you love yourself, even if you're happy with your size, even if you have a wonderful community, it still hurts to have to see that [shaming] in public all the time."

Strings also reflects on Oprah, who spent much of her public life pursuing weight loss and profiting from that narrative. Oprah's language has been one of repentance and redemption—"I know it's wrong and I'm going to do better." By publicizing her weight loss journey and the amount of fat she had shed, Oprah was essentially saying she had been morally deficient and was now redeemed. To hold a cultural position as a fat person, Strings suggests, you historically had to either be thin or be visibly trying to become thin.

**The Class Dimension of Weight Loss**

When GLP-1s are available with insurance, they cost approximately $25 per month. Without insurance, the same medication costs roughly $960 per month. Strings notes that low-income people face barriers at the stage of *getting a prescription* in the first place; without regular doctor access, they cannot obtain insurance coverage for the drug. Early data shows that weight loss is declining faster in high-income populations, particularly among women of a certain class status. This is not because they are biologically different; it is because they can afford the medication and have easier access to healthcare providers who prescribe it. Weight, Strings argues, is becoming a class marker—a visible sign of wealth or poverty.

**Fat Phobia as Structural Discrimination**

In the episode's closing discussion, Strings emphasizes that fat phobia is the "last bastion of discrimination you can do well and not get too much hate for." Airlines, restaurants, and workplaces openly shame fat people in ways that would be immediately condemned if directed at other marginalized groups. Importantly, fat phobia intersects with racism and misogyny. Strings notes it is a "threefer": you can be misogynistic and racist while appearing to care about "health" when you are really engaging in fat-phobia. The solution, she argues, cannot be individual—people cannot diet their way out of a structural problem. Congress must pass legislation including size discrimination in civil rights protections.

# Throughline — "The SAT's 100-year grip on higher ed"

**Host:** Rund Abdelfatah. **Guests:** Nicholas Lemann (author of "The Big Test" and "Higher Admissions"; expert on SAT history and higher education policy).

## TL;DR

- **The SAT was first administered June 23, 1926** — Harvard president James Conant commissioned it to identify merit-based scholarship students, rejecting the "preppy aristocracy" of East Coast elite schools in favor of talent from across America.
- **Carl Brigham, a eugenics-connected psychologist, created the original test** — Brigham held a seat on the American Eugenics Society's advisory council but later renounced his own creation in an unpublished 1934 manuscript, declaring the test measured "a sort of mix" of background, family wealth, and English fluency, not native intelligence.
- **WWII proved the SAT could scale nationally** — On April 2, 1943, the military administered the test to roughly 300,000 people simultaneously across the country, a logistical feat that convinced the College Board to abandon essay-based tests and standardize on the SAT.
- **Administrations exploded from ~10,000 pre-WWII to over 1 million in the 1950s** — The press coverage compared the SAT to the polio vaccine in terms of scientific breakthrough; by the 1950s–60s, nearly all four-year colleges required SAT scores as college demand surged.
- **The test became a multi-billion-dollar industry with persistent equity gaps** — By the 1990s, the SAT rebranded as "assessment" rather than "aptitude," but performance consistently varied by wealth, race, gender, and family income, triggering decades of bias critiques and policy debates.
- **The pandemic disrupted 80 years of continuity** — For the first time since WWII, Ivy League colleges went test-optional in 2020–2021, citing pressure to increase diversity and remove barriers to applicants of color.
- **Ivy Leagues are returning to mandatory testing for 2027–2028** — All eight Ivies require the SAT again, and recent Harvard research shows correlation between strong SAT scores and strong academic performance, complicating the equity debate.
- **Nicholas Lemann's assessment: "The SAT replicates existing class structures more than it doesn't, but there's still a few diamonds in the rough."**

**James Conant's Meritocracy Dream**

In 1933, Harvard president James Conant arrived from a modest middle-class background—the son of a small business owner—and was alarmed by the homogeneity of Harvard's student body. The university was dominated by White Anglo-Saxon Protestant "preppies" from East Coast cities, typically from families with generational wealth and private school pedigrees. Conant believed this "preppy aristocracy" was not competent to lead, and that the Depression crisis demanded better leaders. He was obsessed with Thomas Jefferson's concept of "natural aristocracy"—the idea that virtue and talent, not birth, should determine who governs. Conant worried that extraordinary talent existed in "far-flung corners of America"—people "walking behind a mule and a plow" instead of making scientific breakthroughs—and that he had no way to identify and recruit them. He tasked his assistant dean, Henry Chauncey, to find a test that was "background free and curriculum free."

**Carl Brigham and the Origins of the SAT**

Chauncey discovered Carl Brigham, a Princeton psychology professor who had been researching IQ testing. In the early 20th century, American psychologists and the U.S. military were deeply interested in measuring lifelong intellectual potential. During World War I, the military administered IQ tests to military inductees to sort recruits by aptitude—who could become an officer, who could handle technical tasks, and who should remain a regular soldier. Brigham was one of the founders and administrators of these military tests, and he analyzed the raw data with enthusiasm. He then turned his attention to schools, hooking up with the College Board—a group of mostly elite East Coast universities in charge of standardizing college admissions—and was hired to develop a test for high school students.

**Eugenics, IQ Testing, and Brigham's Secret Doubts**

The intellectual context of the 1920s and 1930s was deeply shaped by eugenics—the belief that intelligence, morality, and social worth were fixed at birth and that society could be engineered by encouraging certain people (particularly white, able-bodied people) to pass on their genes. The standard position among the liberal establishment and prominent academics at Ivy universities was to be sympathetic to the eugenics movement. Brigham was no exception—he held a seat on the advisory council of the American Eugenics Society. However, in 1934, after the SAT had already begun to gain traction, Brigham wrote in an unpublished manuscript that "the test movement came to this country accompanied by one of the most glorious fallacies in the history of science, namely that the tests measured native intelligence purely and simply, without regard to training or schooling." He wanted people to understand that the test measured "a sort of mix"—where people went to school, who their family was, how well they spoke English. Brigham's conversion was too late; his creation had already been unleashed.

**WWII and the Path to National Standardization**

In late 1933, Conant's team conducted an experiment: they offered 10 scholarship spots at Harvard to students identified via Brigham's test administered to high schools across America. Eight of the ten selected students were elected into Harvard's honor society, Phi Beta Kappa, proving the concept worked. By 1937, Princeton, Columbia, and Yale were using the exam for scholarship identification. The crucial turning point came on December 7, 1941, when Japan attacked Pearl Harbor. College Board officials were discussing how to eliminate essay-based entrance exams in favor of standardized multiple-choice testing when the telephone rang with news of the attack. The military again needed a rapid way to sort recruits. On April 2, 1943, the College Board figured out how to administer the SAT to approximately 300,000 people simultaneously across the country and score the tests securely. This was a monumental organizational achievement. The College Board saw the potential and abandoned its old essay-based tests, standardizing on the SAT. Press coverage was breathless—the SAT was compared to the polio vaccine as an "inarguable scientific breakthrough," covered in an admiring, "gee-whiz" tone.

**Expansion into a National Standard and Multi-Billion-Dollar Industry**

Administrations of the SAT exploded from roughly 10,000 before the war to over 1 million by the 1950s. By the 1950s and 1960s, college demand surged and nearly all four-year institutions required some form of standardized test scores. Test prep became a cottage industry and then a major one, as students realized that a good score meant admission to a top school, which meant a good job, money, success, and prestige. In the 1990s, the College Board rebranded the SAT: it dropped "aptitude" and added "assessment"—a subtle but meaningful shift. The test evolved into a multi-billion-dollar industry, tweaked and reformed in response to waves of criticism about racial bias, scoring methodology, and its underlying validity.

**Persistent Questions of Equity and Fairness**

Almost since the SAT's inception, performance has varied by wealth, race, gender, and family income. For decades, critics raised concerns about the test's fairness and its role in either opening or closing doors for students of color. Lemann reflects that the SAT replicates existing class structures; a student who can afford test prep classes has an advantage that is unavailable to those without resources.

**The Pandemic and Test-Optional Disruption**

When the pandemic hit in 2020, testing was paused for logistical reasons—students could not take the test in person. But the absence of testing soon became a longer-term policy shift. Colleges waived testing requirements and made it optional, citing a push to increase diversity and reduce barriers for applicants of color. This was the first time in roughly 80 years that the Ivy League schools went without requiring the SAT—a remarkable break in continuity that intensified public curiosity about why the test existed in the first place and what it actually measured.

**The Return of Requirements and Ongoing Debate**

For the 2027–2028 admission cycle, a majority of colleges will remain test-optional, but all eight Ivy League universities are returning to mandatory SAT requirements. Recent research from Harvard shows correlations between strong SAT scores and strong academic performance in college, complicating the case against testing. Federal data also indicates American teenagers are falling behind in skills and readiness for first-year college courses, which some argue makes standardized testing more necessary as a signal. The question remains unresolved: given the test's 100-year history, its roots in a eugenics-influenced intellectual movement, and its persistent correlation with socioeconomic advantage, how should higher education balance standardization, fairness, and access to opportunity?

# Embedded — "Where is Austin Tice?: The driver"

**Host:** Josh Baker (BBC Radio 4 / NPR, senior reporter). **Guests:** Sara Obeidat (investigative journalist and producer for the series).

## TL;DR

- **Austin Tice disappeared in Syria on August 5, 2012** — a Washington Post freelancer who had published bold stories from rebel-held Damascus, offering military advice to fighters despite security risks.
- **He reached Damascus disguised as a woman in an abaya** — people smugglers helped him pass through a Syrian army checkpoint; he was nearly shot but escaped back into rebel territory.
- **His last contact was from a farm in Daraya, May 28, 2012** — Austin celebrated his 31st birthday there with Free Syrian Army fighters, tweeting "best birthday ever" after swimming and drinking whiskey.
- **The driver was Hamid Abu Abade, a Palestinian refugee from Homs** — identified through months of investigation, yearning for a passport and citizenship that the regime promised in exchange for Austin's delivery.
- **The NDF militia had been monitoring Austin for a month before his abduction** — under commander Bassam al-Hassan, they watched him offer military training to rebels, suspecting him of being intelligence personnel.
- **Abu Ali, a former NDF commander, claims Assad himself approved the abduction** — Bassam al-Hassan called the president after learning about Austin from the driver; Assad allegedly said "bring him to us," making this the first public disclosure of presidential authorization.
- **Hamid disappeared after the regime fell in December 2024** — his family says someone threatened to expose him to the US embassy; he vanished and has not been seen since.

**Austin's Idealism and Why He Went to Syria**

Josh Baker opens with a conversation with Lindsay Hamilton, who knew Austin since college debate competitions and became close to him over the years. She recalls Austin's strong idealism—at Georgetown Law, he once stood with his back to a speaker he disagreed with. This idealism drove him to Syria in 2012.

Austin wrote a Facebook manifesto defending his decision. He rejected the safety concerns of friends, writing "I don't have a death wish, I have a life wish" and claiming Syria was "the greatest thing I've ever done, and it's the greatest feeling of my life" because "every single day people here lay down their own for the sake of others." Hamilton, reading the post years later, acknowledges Austin's desire to change the world but also observes he had "developed comfort in war zones that, frankly, nobody should have to develop." Others who knew him describe Austin as smart, charming, and physically capable—but also overconfident, prone to misjudging risk for himself and others. One friend worried he carried mental trauma from Iraq and Afghanistan.

**Breaking Into Damascus**

By August 2012, Austin had established himself as a prolific war correspondent, writing front-page stories and giving high-profile interviews after just a couple of months in Syria. Media coverage suggested rebel fighters might take Damascus within 36 hours. Austin wanted to be there for what seemed like an imminent government collapse.

David Enders, a journalist who reported alongside Austin for about a month, explains how Austin finally entered Damascus: people smugglers put an abaya and niqab on him—a massive six-foot-plus man dressed as a woman—and drove him through a Syrian army checkpoint. The soldiers figured it out quickly. Enders describes Austin being shot at as he "legged it" back out of the checkpoint into rebel-held territory. Austin tweeted about it afterward with characteristic bravado: "better luck next time, you clowns."

However, Enders expresses concern about the security implications. Austin then published a detailed story about the checkpoint escape in McClatchy News, publicly informing the Syrian government that an American journalist was now inside Damascus. Enders thought this was reckless: "I thought people who Austin was working for and reporting to should have been encouraging him to leave."

**The Farm in Daraya: Austin's Last Known Location**

After reaching Damascus, Austin moved to a rural village called Daraya on the city's outskirts—rebel-held territory with tight alleyways and high walls ideal for hiding. Josh and Sara locate the exact farm where Austin spent his final days. A former rebel commander, who asks to remain anonymous due to fears of regime reprisals, meets them there.

The commander shows them the property: a sanctuary with a swimming pool where Austin had his 31st birthday. Deborah, Austin's mother, had been sent photos from the farm, which helped Josh and Sara find the location. Austin's last tweet came from that day: "Spent the day at a Free Syrian Army pool party with music by Taylor Swift. They even brought me whiskey. Hands down, best birthday ever."

The commander describes how Austin integrated with the fighters: "He just became one of us. He took all our cigarettes and never even cared about how much he was consuming. We didn't really feel he was a stranger." The commander shows photos of Austin with eleven fighters—only two of whom are still alive. Austin spent 12 to 15 days at the farm before asking for a ride. He climbed into a red taxi with a driver he seemed to know, waving goodbye to the fighters. Then he vanished.

**Finding the Driver: Hamid Abu Abade**

The investigation into Austin's disappearance required identifying the driver. Intelligence files found in Syria—discovered by Josh and Sara in December 2024—mentioned the driver but gave no name. However, the files referenced a Syrian named George Chawi who may have been involved in Austin's abduction.

George Chawi, who owns bars in Damascus and is well-connected, initially denies direct involvement but reveals he was contacted by Austin's driver, who brought him Austin's backpack containing a locked laptop. George claims he called government contacts saying an American reporter had entered illegally and needed to be detained. Three days later, George tells Josh, "The bird is in the cage"—implying Austin had been captured.

Nusha Kabawat, a human rights activist who returned to Damascus after Assad's fall, knows George and describes him as charismatic and social. She also reveals George was part of the Syrian Electronic Army—a regime operation that monitored Syrians online and reported them to security services. When Josh confronts George about being named in intelligence files as potentially connected to Austin's abduction, George deflects, claiming the information was likely false and that he was just opening a laptop.

After months of investigation interviewing more than 20 people, a protected source provides a recording of a senior regime commander describing the driver. The commander mentions two names—Abu Ahmed and Abu Adham—describing him as "short, dark, bearded," from Homs, and "looks like a vegetable seller." Using this information and searching social media, Josh and Sara identify Hamid Abu Abade, an inactive Facebook profile matching the description.

Through Hamid's brother Samer, they confirm: Hamid picked Austin up in a red car. However, Hamid has vanished since the regime fell in December 2024. His wife, Gada, meets Josh and Sara at their apartment in Homs. She initially tells a vague story about Hamid helping transport injured people and giving Austin a ride to Homs. But after Josh gently lays out the full investigation—Hamid's links to the NDF militia, all the interviews, years of research—Samer reveals the truth.

**The Incentive to Betray**

Hamid and his family are Palestinian refugees in Syria, lacking full citizenship and facing restricted rights. The NDF promised him life-changing rewards for delivering Austin: a passport, a house, and citizenship for himself and all his siblings. In the end, "the only thing he got out of it was a gun." These promises would have been transformative for a stateless Palestinian family.

After the regime fell, someone called Hamid threatening to expose him to the US embassy. Hamid left home saying he would buy things for the house and be back in half an hour. He never returned. Days later, armed men—about 11 cars of them, some in civilian clothes, some in military-style garments—arrived at his home and stole his laptop and electronics. Gada does not know who they were. Hamid's phone has been off since he disappeared.

**The Surveillance Operation and Abduction Plan**

Josh and Sara track down Abu Ali, who worked under NDF commander Bassam al-Hassan and claims to have been present during Austin's detention. Abu Ali reveals the operation's scope and sophistication.

According to Abu Ali, Hamid Abu Abade had been in contact with Saqqar Rustum, a nephew of Bassam al-Hassan. Hamid informed Rustum about the American journalist, and Rustum called his uncle Bassam, who then called President Assad. Bassam told Assad: "There's an American journalist here. I'm going to have to tell the president about it." Assad replied: "Bring him to us." This is the first public disclosure suggesting Assad personally authorized Austin's abduction.

The surveillance operation was extensive. After learning of Austin from Hamid, the NDF monitored him for about a month. Hamid proposed: "Why don't we continue to monitor him? I'm not going to lose sight of him. He trusts me." The regime wanted to understand what Austin was doing and who he was meeting, suspecting he might be working for intelligence services.

Austin's offer of military training to rebel fighters heightened these suspicions. Abu Ali explains: Austin "would be present for their military training and would sometimes correct them or tell them, 'oh, that's not the way you use this kind of weapon. You have to use it differently.' It was almost like this person was a military or intelligence kind of person." The regime collected information about everyone Austin met and the people who helped him, though it remains unclear whether anyone was exposed or imprisoned because of that surveillance.

**The Moment of Betrayal**

The abduction occurred after Austin's birthday. Around 7 PM, the red taxi arrived in Daraya. Hamid picked up Austin and drove him toward Tahuni, an area where Bassam al-Hassan had his office. At that location, Hamid pulled out a gun and said "game over." Austin initially thought it was a joke and laughed, but when he realized it was real, he pulled out his checkbook and pleaded: "Here, I'll sign my name and just put whatever price you want. Please don't do this."

Soldiers then seized Austin, pulled something over his eyes, and took him into custody. Bassam al-Hassan immediately called President Assad to report success. "They were very pleased with themselves," Abu Ali recalls.

For years, there had been a sense that Austin pushed too far—that his boldness in going deep into Syria was the risk that finally caught up with him. But this investigation reveals a more deliberate scheme: before Austin ever reached Damascus, a plan to abduct him was already in motion, initiated by his own trusted driver, coordinated by a pro-Assad militia, and allegedly sanctioned by the president himself.

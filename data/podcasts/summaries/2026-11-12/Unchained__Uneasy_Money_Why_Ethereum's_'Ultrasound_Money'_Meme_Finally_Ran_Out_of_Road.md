# Unchained — "Uneasy Money: Why Ethereum's 'Ultrasound Money' Meme Finally Ran Out of Road"

**Host:** Kain Warwick (founder of Infinex; former Synthetix founder). **Co-host:** Austin Griffith (Ethereum Foundation, application layer development). **Guest:** Greg Marku (CEO of Sprinter; former Ethereum core dev; former web3.js maintainer).

**The EF's "Seppuku Clause" and What It Actually Means**

The Ethereum Foundation fired a board member who simultaneously had cancer and was pushing to remove the foundation's "seppuku" clause — a metaphorical honor-pledge stating that if you dishonor what you signed up for, you should kill yourself. Kain is unambiguous: the clause is a samurai-culture reference, not a trivialization of suicide, and anyone who makes removing it their primary agenda at a foundation is "negative EV" to have around. Austin agrees the metaphorical framing is fine now; a more serious org structure failed to materialize, but talented people did depart to form three credible organizations, which may be the better outcome. The cancer timing is genuinely unfortunate, but Kain and Austin both argue that illness doesn't immunize someone from performance or conduct-based exit if those are the actual causes. The broader incentive point: Vitalik cares deeply about precedent, and tolerating disruptive behavior for optics would invite more of it. No details on severance are confirmed.

**EIP-1559, the Burn, and the "Ultrasound Money" Meme**

Kain frames EIP-1559 as a classic case of unintended consequences. The stated goal was reducing fee market variance and improving wallet UX (pre-1559, transactions failed constantly; post-1559, "you hit go and it goes"). The ETH burn was added as a mechanism design afterthought — better than distributing fee revenue to a governance pool that would attract extractors, and simpler than the Zcash-style developer grant model Greg once proposed at Edcon Sydney, which was met with EIP999-style pitchforks. Once ETH started burning, "ultrasound money" became an effective meme — Kain admits he had the bat speaker emoji on his account for a while. The problem: the meme became the thesis, spawning burn trackers and community pressure to maximize burns, which was never the point. At DevCon, Vitalik came out to explicitly distance himself from the meme and argue that ETH's value should come from its intrinsic properties. Greg notes that with Wall Street now doing actual price impact analysis on ETH via ETFs, the community needs more careful framing than what shitposting on Twitter can provide.

**The Missing ETH-as-Asset Pitch**

The hosts identify a gap that has never been adequately filled: who is writing research-quality material explaining the investment thesis for ETH as an asset to a traditional finance audience? Brian Mosov at Ether Capital was the best at articulating the mechanism-design and economic case, but has largely stepped back — "there's only so much pain you can endure," Kain says. Vitalik isn't the right person; he's chief scientist, not PR. Greg argues the founder still needs a coherent thesis, but Kain counters that Bill Gates doesn't pitch Microsoft anymore and that Ethereum needs its Steve Ballmer ("Developers, developers, developers"). The community hasn't produced a credible successor to do that translation work for institutional capital.

**Core Devs vs. App Layer: Gas Repricing and Testing Complexity**

A proposed repricing of SSTORE (storage write) operations — part of ongoing efforts to make Ethereum scale more efficiently — went forward without adequately auditing implications for live deployed contracts. Kyber's Loy surfaced that the change breaks AMMs. Core devs initially acknowledged they hadn't reviewed the implications, and have since gone back to the drawing board. Greg defends them: Ethereum is a "monolithic beast," and finding invariants across years of deployed contracts is genuinely hard. He broke web3.js himself post-1559 because of an undocumented invariant with MetaMask he didn't know existed. The EIP intake is now "multiple times a day" with AI-assisted submissions, compared to roughly one per week historically, which compounds the review burden. Greg also notes a cultural dynamic: core devs build up a "no faster" reflex as a defense mechanism against perpetually relitigated proposals (e.g., EOF), which reads to outsiders as an ivory tower but is a rational response to the volume.

**Kain's AGI Benchmark and the Complexity of Chain State**

Kain proposes a practical AGI test: ask an AI "What was my PnL on August 20, 2020 on this address?" for a DeFi-active Ethereum address. The chain state on a single day in mid-2020 — across yield farming, early AMMs, liquidity protocols with rotating oracle pointers, proxy contracts — is so complex that only a truly intelligent system could reconstruct it. His own agent, running against his cain.eth address (900 transactions, roughly 2,000–3,000 events over the year), still has 58 unknowns after extensive processing. Fable 5.2 resolved two of those by identifying a Blur oracle disconnection and pointer change via a proxy contract, requiring backward traversal through blocks to find when the oracle changed. Greg generalizes the point: doing a Foundry fork test against live Ethereum mainnet state is nearly impossible because of the density of off-chain dependencies (oracle update schedules, protocol-specific invariants) that have to be reconstructed to get valid results.

**Ledger's Signing SDK Is Phoning Home**

Kain spent time digging into Ledger's signing SDK and found it makes a POST API call to Ledger's servers after every signing interaction — including failed ones — containing transaction metadata that can correlate a user to their address. The official CTO explanation is that this is necessary for clear signing (reading contract bytecode to tell users what they're signing), since device memory is too limited to do it locally. Kain's rebuttal: if that's true, why does the call fire after the transaction is already signed? The timing makes no sense for the stated purpose and suggests the telemetry is a byproduct, not a feature. Greg confirms the v2 API stripped some metadata but correlation is still possible. Clear signing (EIP-7730) has been technically specified for years but adoption is incomplete; dynamic contracts — proxies, diamond patterns, ENS addresses that change underneath — fundamentally can't be handled by preloaded firmware and require trusted server calls regardless, which is an unsolved problem. Both Greg and Kain agree hardware wallets are the wrong form factor for end users: they should function like YubiKeys plugged into a secure device, not standalone signing surfaces. Grid Plus had the right idea but failed on UX (cards bound to specific devices, no multi-card support). Today, serious holders are traveling with two or three dedicated signing laptops.

**The Future of Crypto UX: Native Apps, Passkeys, and Multi-Sig**

An iOS sandbox break the previous night allowed cross-app signing theft in crypto wallets before Apple issued a hot patch — illustrating that the security layer keeps collapsing regardless of hardware wallet choice. The practical direction the hosts converge on: passkey-based native mobile apps where the passkey is a signer on a smart contract, which is itself a signer on a multi-sig with daily spend limits, social recovery, and a "dead man's switch" for recovery resets. This requires no "wallet" abstraction users need to understand; the app just does the thing. Austin notes that embedded wallet providers (Privy, Turnkey) are the critical dependency layer. The cross-chain problem complicates everything: as soon as users want to interact with Solana (pump.fun, Polymarket's Binance-day hack, etc.), the security model drags down to the least-secure chain, making a database of dollars the pragmatically dominant outcome. Regulatory overhang from the Gensler era scared DeFi teams away from native apps for years — Synthetix never released a mobile app because the App Store requirement for a corporate entity created legal nexus they didn't want.

**MegaETH and On-Chain AI Inference Markets**

Kain relays a conversation with MegaETH's Namek: the project is building a permissionless two-sided marketplace for AI inference on-chain. The core arbitrage: API rack rates vs. monthly subscription plan pricing is a massive and structurally unsustainable gap — users who don't exhaust their plans subsidize heavy users, and tokens (inference credits) are so fungible that people will find every crack to exploit them (Kain describes a startup that banned all of Indonesia after stolen credit cards flooded a free-trial DevOps inference system). Venice.ai is already running this playbook: stake VVV tokens and receive daily inference credits at rates well below API pricing. Greg notes the blockchain removes counterparty trust requirements in a two-sided inference market — supply and demand can transact without identity verification. The limitation: blockchains can't see GPUs; it's a marketplace layer over APIs, not streaming inference over chain. Kain argues the arbitrage is a trillion-dollar opportunity that "nature finds a way" to close, mirroring the yield farming and gas token arb dynamics of early DeFi (Greg remembers gas tokens causing so much SSTORE repricing pressure that it uncovered the "dodging a bullet" Osaka bug — an infinite VM loop via incorrect gas accounting, undisclosed for two years).

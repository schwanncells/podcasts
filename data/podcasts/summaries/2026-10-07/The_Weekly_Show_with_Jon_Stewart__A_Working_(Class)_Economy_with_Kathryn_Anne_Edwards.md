# The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart — "A Working (Class) Economy with Kathryn Anne Edwards"

**Host:** Jon Stewart. **Guest:** Kathryn Anne Edwards (PhD labor economist; public policy consultant; writer; host of Optimist Economy podcast).

## TL;DR

- **Minimum wage frozen at $7.25 for decades despite 50 years of policy favoring capital over labor** — in the 1970s, 15% of the economy worked for minimum wage; today less than 1%. Government now subsidizes low-wage employers through Medicaid, food stamps, and earned income tax credits rather than requiring higher wages.
- **Nine million full-time workers rely on government benefits because wages are too low** — companies pay below-subsistence wages while the government fills the gap via tax credits and social safety net, privatizing profits while socializing losses.
- **The last five years have been the highest corporate profit years in U.S. history, yet workers have lost power and income share** — productivity per hour has tripled since the 1980s while wages have stayed flat; labor's share of the economy dropped from 52% in the 1970s to substantially lower today.
- **Employer concentration (Big Eight accounting firms now Big Four; studios consolidated similarly) means minimum wage hikes don't cause job loss** — in concentrated markets, workers are already paid less than their productivity warrants; raising the floor just cuts into monopoly profits rather than eliminating jobs.
- **Proposed labor reforms: raise minimum wage, guarantee sick days and vacation, require predictable schedules (no "clopening"), allow part-time/remote requests, delink health insurance from employment, and expand family benefits** — these aren't entitlements but investments that grow the economy by increasing labor force participation.
- **Government has become a stand-in for unions, providing via tax code what workers can't negotiate** — health insurance subsidies, child care credits, tips credits, and earned income tax credits amount to $2 trillion-plus in annual tax expenditures supporting low-wage work.
- **COVID recovery proved direct worker support outperforms corporate bailouts** — the 2008 financial crisis bailout cost Social Security 8 years of solvency; the COVID pandemic response of direct payments to people cost Social Security zero years, yet generated faster economic recovery and saved homes.
- **AI consolidation among seven companies poses risk of exacerbating inequality unless labor policy rebalances now** — with corporate power at historic highs and workers at historic lows, the moment to pass protective labor regulations is now, when concentrated employers can absorb them without job loss.

---

**Fifty Years of Declining Worker Power**

Edwards argues that the American economy has been out of balance for 50 years, with a systematic shift from protecting workers to privileging capital. Worker power comes from individual skills and credentials, but also from overall economic health (unemployment rates, job availability) and structural protections. The past five decades have consistently de-emphasized worker importance in favor of business interests, eroding protections and mobility. Edwards cites the minimum wage as the clearest illustration: it has risen only about four dollars in 50 years. Fifteen percent of the economy earned minimum wage in prior decades; today it is less than 1%. Rather than interpret this as the minimum wage becoming less relevant, Edwards reads it as a statement of priorities—that those workers simply do not matter to policymakers.

**Subsidizing Low-Wage Employers Through Government Benefits**

The affordability crisis is not a surprise but the inevitable result of intentional policy choices that favor capital over labor. Stewart emphasizes that even at $10 or $12 per hour, people cannot live comfortably, yet companies are allowed to pay those wages. The public then subsidizes these corporations through Medicaid, food stamps, and earned income tax credits. Nine million full-time workers are on Medicaid or food stamps despite working full-time. Edwards points out that when the government uses the tax code to subsidize wages—via tips credits, child care credits, and other tax expenditures—the burden falls not on employers but on other taxpayers. For example, a tax credit for tips means other workers with the same income who don't receive tips end up paying higher tax bills to offset that credit. Government has become an employer subsidy mechanism, using benefits and tax policy to keep workers at bare subsistence while companies capture the difference.

**The Tax Code as Substitute for Real Labor Policy**

Over the past 25 years, tax policy has been the most active policy tool in Congress, used to deliver benefits that should be mandated directly. Rather than raising the minimum wage, Congress issues earned income tax credits. Instead of guaranteeing paid family leave or universal child care, Congress creates tax-favored accounts and dependent care credits. The corporate tax system operates similarly: the statutory rate is one level (35%, then lowered to 21%), but the effective rate is much lower because of corporate tax expenditures (bonuses deductions, research and development credits, corporate jet write-offs). Edwards describes this tax-code-as-policy approach as less effective because it creates loopholes and complexity, whereas simple wage or benefit mandates are harder for employers to game. When Congress cut the corporate tax rate from 35% to 21%, the actual rate paid by corporations dropped to approximately 10%, showing that companies found additional loopholes after the statutory cut.

**Employer Concentration Breaks Economic Theory on Minimum Wage**

Classic economic theory predicts that raising the minimum wage above a worker's marginal contribution causes job loss. Yet real-world evidence after decades of state and local minimum wage increases shows minimal or no job loss. Edwards attributes this to employer concentration: the Big Eight accounting firms are now the Big Four; entertainment studios have consolidated similarly. In concentrated labor markets, employers already pay workers less than they produce because workers have fewer options. Raising the minimum wage does not eliminate jobs—it simply removes excess profits that concentrated employers capture by underpaying. In competitive markets, a wage floor might disrupt hiring; in concentrated markets, workers are already below their productivity, so the wage floor comes out of unearned surplus. This is the ideal time to pass labor regulations because concentrated employers have the market power to absorb them without layoffs.

**Core Labor Reforms to Rebalance the Economy**

Edwards proposes eradicating the low-wage job market through several concrete policies. First, update the Fair Labor Standards Act to guarantee paid sick days and paid vacation for every worker, making it illegal to fire someone for calling in sick. Second, raise the minimum wage. Third, pass scheduling laws requiring shifts to be announced at least two weeks in advance and paid if canceled; prohibit "clopening" (closing late, opening early the next morning) without overtime. Fourth, allow salaried workers to request part-time or remote work without losing their job—this retains people in the labor market during life disruptions (illness, divorce, childcare needs) rather than forcing them out. Fifth, delink health insurance from employment entirely; government already subsidizes employer health insurance to the tune of two-thirds of federal spending via tax preference, yet this subsidy is invisible, creates job lock, and prevents worker mobility between employers. Sixth, invest heavily in family benefits: paid family leave, universal child care, universal after-school and summer programming, and mental health support for teens. Seventh, ensure every worker has a retirement account with employer contributions. These are not charity but investments that increase labor force participation and grow the economy.

**Government as Proxy for Vanished Union Power**

The proposals essentially ask federal government to establish floors that unions once negotiated. Stewart observes that this means government must step in because workers no longer have sufficient power to organize collectively. Edwards agrees, framing these regulations as baseline standards for doing business in the U.S.: if you want to operate here, you must meet modern labor standards. The government is not being expansive by requiring these protections; it is being responsible by representing worker interests that capital has abandoned. This reframes the debate away from "business burden" toward "minimum standards for a developed economy."

**COVID Response Proves Direct Worker Support Outperforms Corporate Bailouts**

The contrast between the 2008 financial crisis and the COVID-19 pandemic responses illustrates the economic case for worker support. In 2008, the government bailed out toxic assets and financial institutions at the corporate level, making bondholders whole while millions lost homes. The damage was so severe it cost Social Security 8 years of solvency. In 2020, rather than bailing out corporations first, the government gave direct payments to people—stimulus checks, enhanced unemployment benefits, child tax credits. The economic bounce-back was rapid, people kept their homes, and Social Security paid no solvency cost. Edwards frames this as data proving that Keynesian stimulus to workers is more effective than trickle-down bailouts. When people have money, they spend it locally, supporting small businesses and employment. When corporations hoard profits, growth stalls and workers suffer.

**Unemployment Insurance and Long-Term Job Displacement**

Edwards advocates for a three-tier unemployment system matching reality. Most job loss is brief—people find new work quickly on their resume. A smaller share takes longer to find a comparable job. The smallest share involves workers whose skills are obsolete and require retraining. Yet the current system treats all three alike. Edwards proposes: for the first group, quick payments to cover the gap; for the second, support while they search; for the third, funding to transition to new work. Additionally, she advocates wage insurance—if globalization or technology disrupts an industry, workers should not take a permanent pay cut; government should insure against wage loss, allowing workers to move to new fields without losing living standards. This policy is especially relevant given AI fears about job displacement.

**Challenging the Deficit Argument and Economic Indicators**

When Edwards proposes robust labor policies, critics respond that government lacks money, citing budget constraints. Edwards pushes back: Republicans have normalized "tax cut and spend" deficits unseen globally while claiming progressive policies are unaffordable. The real issue is priority, not scarcity. She also challenges economic indicators: stock market tickers appear on news constantly because they change minute-by-minute, while unemployment updates monthly and poverty annually. News media treats market prices as health indicators despite workers' lived experience showing economic indicators disconnected from their reality. The task is not to add worker-share-of-productivity metrics (though these matter) but to listen when people say "I am hurting and you are not helping" and act on their needs rather than dismissing them with inflation warnings.

**Optimism Out of Spite and the AI Moment**

Edwards describes herself as a "cynical optimist," willing to believe change is possible because giving up grants victory to those hoarding wealth and power. She hosted the Optimist Economy podcast to maintain hope despite structural barriers. Regarding AI, Edwards warns that corporate consolidation around seven major companies mirrors the 1980s tech boom that led to decades of inequality. Unlike the 1980s, when workers had not yet experienced neoliberalism's failures, today people carry 50 years of evidence: declining real wages, housing unaffordability, foreclosures, worse health benefits year after year. This collective experience creates the opening to reverse course. If Democrats gain Congress, Edwards wants a full government effort to rebalance labor versus capital through policies like those she outlined—not performative hearings on Trump's corruption, which will go nowhere, but active legislation to raise the labor floor. She frames investments in workers not as entitlements but as human capital investments that grow the economy, enabling more people to work, earn, and spend, making the economy larger.

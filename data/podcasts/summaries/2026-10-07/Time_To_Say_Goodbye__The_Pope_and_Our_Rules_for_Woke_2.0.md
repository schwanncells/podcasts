# Time To Say Goodbye — "The Pope and Our Rules for Woke 2.0"

**Host:** Jay. **Guests:** Tyler (writer and commentator on politics, culture, and technology; co-host).

## TL;DR

- **The Pope is mounting the only credible institutional resistance to AI** — he published an encyclical on AI in September and has been lobbying global religious leaders to weigh in on whether AI might have a soul; unlike dysfunctional political parties, the Catholic Church has moral gravitas, global reach, and institutional heft to actually challenge tech oligarchy.
- **Woke 1.0 will return, but it needs six explicit rules for 2.0** — no violence metaphors (conflating words with physical harm delegitimized real violence discourse); establish a weighting system for oppression (trans people face real persecution; neurodivergent people do not); class-first intersectionality that acknowledges but subordinates identity; ban HR-led training and bureaucratic "wokeness"; enforce uniform rules for everyone (no separate standards by demographic); and strip out therapeutic psychobabble language that depoliticizes institutional racism.
- **Woke 1.0 failed when it stopped targeting institutions and started targeting individuals** — when the focus was the police, it was galvanizing; when it became "fire this random person for a 2010 tweet," the public recoiled, including a 25-year-old Portland Trailblazers announcer fired the day his hiring was announced for tweets he made at age 15.
- **The movement must identify a clear enemy, and that enemy is AI and data centers** — a combustible coalition is forming: young people disgusted by forced AI use in school, homeowners watching property values collapse near data centers, recently graduated workers displaced by AI, and professionals being proletarianized; "get the AI out of my life" populism can unite across class and race lines where identity politics fragmented the left.
- **Anthropic's push for AI consciousness is driven by genuine belief, not cynicism** — these are earnest people who have been circulating consciousness and soul questions since the 1950s (Asimov's *I, Robot*); they maintain a "soul doc" internally and truly believe they are creating something divine; they are not engaged in 4D chess with the public.
- **The Pope frames AI not as a neutral technology but as freighted with the political economy that created it** — directly arguing the technology inherits the values and power structures of oligarchic tech firms; this Marxist critique escapes the tech left's fantasy that AI can be repurposed for socialist aims.
- **Cultural and artistic resistance to AI is just beginning** — most artists and musicians remain unaware of the scale of work theft embedded in LLM training; once that awareness spreads and artists organize (as they did against Napster, though incompletely), cultural shaming and professional resistance could limit AI adoption.
- **The conversation between pundits differs drastically from public consensus** — media figures debate AI's merits across multiple positions; ordinary people (conservatives fishing, progressives concerned about cops, homeowners worried about data centers) have already decided AI is bad; the chattering class is three years behind actual public opinion.

**The Pope as the Only Credible Institutional Resistance**

Jay and Tyler frame the Pope as uniquely positioned to oppose AI oligarchy. Unlike the Democratic and Republican parties, both aligned with tech oligarchs and merely triangulating their messaging, the Catholic Church has global institutional reach, moral authority independent of electoral cycles, and economic resources. Tyler notes that secular critiques of AI reduce to material harms (environmental damage, labor exploitation) and leave untouched the deeper "bedrock moral disquiet" that people feel watching machines automate human cognition. The Pope's framework, grounded in anthropology—"what humans are for, what the soul is"—provides language that secular politics cannot. He published an encyclical in September and is now lobbying world religious leaders, particularly around Chris Ola's campaign to get religious figures to weigh in on AI consciousness and moral status.

**Woke 1.0's Mistakes and the Need for Structured Rules**

Jay and Tyler identify woke 1.0's core failures and propose explicit guardrails for 2.0. The first rule: no violence as metaphor. Throughout the Gaza conflict, progressive discourse flattened the language of harm, claiming "trans genocide" and "neurodivergence oppression" simultaneously with actual systematic bombing of civilians. This rhetorical collapse legitimated administrative overreach—if words equal violence, firing someone is justified prevention. Second: establish a weighting system for oppression. Trans people face material violence; someone with ADHD does not face the same oppression; gay white men are less oppressed now than they were 40 years ago, even in hostile regions. Acknowledging these weights prevents narcissists and the loudest voices from dominating conversations they shouldn't. Third: class-first intersectionality. The original Combahee River Collective was Marxist; they used their identity as Black women to participate in politics otherwise closed to them. Woke 1.0 inverted this, making identity the primary lens and subordinating class, leading to absurdities like "rich Black people are more marginalized than poor white people."

**The Destruction of Woke 1.0's Power Through Targeting Individuals**

Jay emphasizes that woke 1.0 had genuine power when directed at institutions—particularly police and systemic racism. When the Minneapolis police precinct burned in 2020, public approval hit 60%. The movement's reach and clarity were real. But then woke advocates began targeting individuals for personal redemption and shame. A Baltimore rock opera director was fired for wearing cornrows at Burning Man (a dust management choice). A 25-year-old Portland Trailblazers announcer was fired hours after his hiring announcement when a news organization resurfaced tweets he posted at 15 ("Asians are annoying"). The public, even progressives, recoiled from these hirings. Tyler argues this targeting won't stop unless woke 2.0 actively polices itself—and he is "somewhat optimistic" that progressive horror at Trump's return will generate the political will to avoid repeating woke 1.0's self-sabotaging excess.

**The Real Enemy: AI and Data Centers**

Jay and Tyler converge on AI and tech infrastructure as the unifying enemy for a renewed left movement. This shifts the focus from policing (woke 1.0's institutional target) to a technology and industry. Young people on campuses are "fucking livid" about being forced to use AI and actively work to avoid it. Homeowners near proposed data centers watch property values collapse and are becoming a powerful bipartisan political force—one Trump-voting family member told Jay that when $40,000 annual daycare costs are the norm, "I don't know what Republicans expect other than people embracing socialism." Professionals and recent college graduates are being displaced by AI automation. Combined with young people's countercultural rejection and older homeowners' financial interests, Jay identifies "a very combustible coalition." Tyler notes young people's hatred of the new AI video tool "Model" (which can generate near-indistinguishable synthetic human videos) is nearly universal; the public recognizes it as harmful with no legitimate use case except fraud.

**Why AI Leaders Push the Consciousness Narrative**

Tyler argues the push for AI consciousness is not cynical manipulation but genuine belief. These founders have been circulating soul and consciousness questions since Isaac Asimov's *I, Robot* in the 1950s. Anthropic maintains a "soul doc" internally. When these formerly marginalized researchers suddenly became billionaires, they did not abandon their founding intellectual obsession—they doubled down. Tyler compares it to religious recruitment: Hare Krishnas recruit because they believe it saves souls; AI leaders evangelize consciousness because they believe they have created something divine. This earnestness, combined with their wealth and explanatory power in a moment when the world is asking "what technology have we just invented?", makes them potent advocates. Jay accepts this analysis and pivots to a larger question: why is the AI industry failing to make its strongest argument to the public—medical breakthroughs, cancer research, solving Navier-Stokes equations, rockets to the moon—instead focusing on existential risk? Tyler responds that these are "earnest people" trapped in the same conversation loop they have maintained for 25 years, before money arrived. They are not optimizing for public messaging; they are trapped in their moral seriousness about the stakes.

**The Pope's Marxist Critique of Technology**

Tyler highlights a crucial insight from the Pope's position: AI is not neutral technology. It is "freighted with all the weight of the circumstances and the economy that built it and the oligarchy that built it." This directly refutes the tech left's fantasy that socialist movements can simply repurpose AI systems for emancipatory goals. The technology itself encodes capitalist logic. The Pope makes this argument in explicitly Marxist terms, something that secular left critiques often miss. Jay notes this opens space for renewed conversation about institutional racism and real systemic violence without collapsing it into individual moral purity projects.

**Emerging Cultural Resistance**

Jay describes the early stages of artistic resistance to AI. Most musicians, writers, and visual artists remain unaware of how thoroughly their work was stolen to train LLMs. Once awareness spreads—as it is starting to—a cultural movement could emerge analogous to anti-Napster campaigns. Such campaigns did not stop digital music, but they contributed to broader anti-tech sentiment and created pockets of meaningful resistance. Jay predicts artists and writers will eventually organize explicitly against AI training and use, which could shift norms. Tyler notes this is further complicated by the genuine utility of AI for mundane tasks; every creator must individually navigate their relationship with the technology before they can effectively resist it.

**The Pundits vs. the Public**

A central insight: media personalities are having an elaborate three-way debate (anti-AI, pro-AI, moderate) that looks polarized on Twitter, but ordinary people have already decided. Jay describes conversations with conservative fishermen angered by AI-generated fish images flooding fishing Instagram, displacing real content. Tyler notes the public is not fragmented on this issue—95% of people intuitively grasp that a comic book AI generates in seconds differs ontologically from three years of human labor. Yet pundits continue debating as if the question is open. Jay attributes this gap to the fact that journalists and commentators have personal professional stakes in AI (efficiency, threats to jobs) that shape their discourse. The actual public, having no professional investment, simply reacts to obvious harms: fake images, data collection, property value collapse, job displacement.

**The Fifth and Sixth Rules for Woke 2.0**

Jay adds a fourth rule: no separate rules for different demographic groups. During the pandemic, public health experts mandated lockdowns but exempted Black Lives Matter protests on racial justice grounds—a clear double standard that deranged large swaths of the population. Uniform rules for everyone, while acknowledging different vulnerabilities, prevents this perception of favoritism.

Tyler's fifth rule: eliminate therapeutic and therapeutic-adjacent language. Woke 1.0 psychologized institutional racism, turning it into an individual self-improvement project—"purify your soul of bias." This depoliticized the actual problem: institutional racism requires political and organizational solutions, not personal journey work. The therapeutic framing came from 1960s-1970s encounter group psychology, co-opted by HR in the 1980s, and embedded in woke culture by the 2010s. Stripping it out removes a major depoliticizing mechanism.

**The Enemy Must Be Named**

Jay's final rule: identify a clear enemy and align against it. Woke 1.0 had this with policing—it was galvanizing. Woke 2.0 must do the same with AI/oligarchy/tech. The enemy cannot be random individuals tweeting stupid things at 15. It must be large, institutional, and genuinely powerful. Tyler agrees and points to left populist figures like Bernie Sanders (though Tyler wishes Bernie "did some things wrong" to avoid sainthood) who focus on fighting oligarchy as the unifying frame. This framing "brings in everybody" because class-based opposition to concentrated wealth can accommodate the intersectional conversations Jay values. Once the big enemy is clear, space opens for people to discuss race, gender, and identity without those discussions fragmenting the movement itself.

**The Likely Enemy: Tech and AI**

Both hosts conclude the enemy for the next political cycle will be AI, data centers, and the tech industry. This unites young people's countercultural rejection, homeowners' financial interests, displaced workers, and intellectuals concerned with surveillance and control. Unlike individual shame campaigns, this has the scale and clarity to drive policy and grassroots organizing. The anti-data center movement is already bipartisan at the grassroots level, ignored by politicians at their peril.

The episode ends with discussion of whether young people will actually adopt Woke 2.0 or whether the political energy will flow somewhere else entirely. Tyler expresses optimism that people understand the excesses of 1.0 and will police themselves, but notes this will require constant vigilance: "a lot of those people have not left left spaces." Jay is also cautiously optimistic, particularly because Democratic horror at Trump's return will incentivize disciplining the worst excesses before they metastasize again into cultural sabotage.

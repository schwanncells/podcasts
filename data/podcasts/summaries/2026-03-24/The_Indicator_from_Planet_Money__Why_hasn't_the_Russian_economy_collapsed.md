# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Why hasn't the Russian economy collapsed?"

**Host:** Waylon Wong and Patti Hirsch. **Guests:** Alina Rybikova (economist and Russia specialist fellow, Peterson Institute for International Economics); Timothy Ash (Russian and Eurasian economics analyst, Chatham House, London); Vladislav Inozemtsev (Russian economist, co-founder of the Center for Analysis and Strategies in Europe).

## TL;DR

- **Russia is burning $500M–$1B per day on the war** — its debt has grown to $320 billion, and GDP growth slowed from ~4% in 2024 to under 1% in Q3, yet the economy has not collapsed.
- **Oil exports remain the primary lifeline** — Russia's energy, metals, and fertilizer exports are still in global demand, giving the country hard currency income that partially offsets war costs.
- **Sanctions failed to do the job** — analysts say they were "too slow," "too well telegraphed," and poorly enforced, with no one jailed or fined for helping Russia evade restrictions, including the U.S. itself purchasing Russian-refined oil.
- **China replaced Europe as Russia's largest trading partner** — buying oil, gas, agricultural goods, and chemicals while providing direct military support and consumer goods that free Russian industry to focus on military production.
- **Putin prepared for sanctions as far back as 2014** — technocrats stockpiled dollars, stopped capital flight, forced foreign companies to sell at fire-sale prices (estimated $170B+ in losses), and redistributed assets to build a loyal new business class.
- **"Smertonomika" (death economics) is stimulating the domestic economy** — soldier pay has risen 6x since 2022; signing bonuses now reach $20,000–$40,000; death benefits pay $130,000–$180,000 per casualty, more than a median Russian could earn in a lifetime.
- **The war economy is suppressing dissent** — wealthy Russians no longer fear conscription, so they don't protest; poor and desperate volunteers from deprived regions absorb the fighting and receive cash transfers that function as a form of Keynesian stimulus.
- **The reckoning is deferred, not avoided** — when the war ends, Russia will have to confront a decimated workforce, degraded economic sectors, and defense spending that has consumed up to half the government budget.

**Energy and Commodities: The First Pillar**

Rybikova identifies Russia's continued commodity exports as the single biggest reason the economy is still functioning. Oil is the dominant earner, but Russia also exports gas, metals, and agricultural inputs like fertilizers — all goods the global economy has not found it easy to forgo. The commodity revenues provide a base of hard currency that underwrites the war effort and cushions the impact of other pressures.

**Sanctions: Weaker Than Designed**

Timothy Ash is blunt in his assessment: "We haven't done enough. We've been too slow. We've been too well telegraphed. We've given the Russians lots of time to figure out ways to get around sanctions." Secondary sanctions — enforcement against third parties who help Russia evade restrictions — have been largely absent. No one has gone to jail or been meaningfully fined. Ash notes the U.S. itself has purchased Russian oil refined in third countries, undermining the moral and practical force of the sanctions regime.

**China as a Structural Enabler**

Rybikova identifies China as the third major pillar of Russia's war economy. China provides direct military support and supplies consumer goods that allow Russian industrial capacity to pivot toward weapons production. China is now Russia's largest trading partner, having replaced Europe entirely. Ash frames the relationship as asymmetric: Russia has become dependent on China, which has used the sanctions environment to deepen its economic leverage. Where China led in defying Western restrictions, other countries followed.

**Putin's Prepared Defense: Buffers, Technocrats, and Asset Transfers**

Ash argues that Putin began planning for Western sanctions response as far back as the 2014 Crimea annexation. When sanctions hit in 2022 and roughly $100 billion fled the country, Russia's macroeconomic managers were already in position. Skilled technocrats tightened fiscal and monetary policy, reduced external vulnerabilities, and built currency reserves.

Rybikova highlights a related maneuver: as foreign companies were forced to sell Russian operations — often at steep discounts — Putin transferred those assets to a new class of loyalists. The Kyiv School of Economics estimated that foreign businesses suffered over $170 billion in direct losses. This redistribution served a dual purpose: consolidating key assets into Russian hands while creating a fresh cohort of wealthy supporters who owe their positions to the president. As Rybikova puts it, "to create the new, even more loyal class of wealthy people, I think that was important to give them something. And in order to give something, you need to take something."

**The New Oligarchs and Support from Below**

The loyalty structure doesn't only operate at the elite level. Rybikova explains that Putin's inner business circle has rational incentives to close ranks: as long as the war and sanctions continue, they keep making money. Vladislav Inozemtsev adds that Putin has also consolidated support among ordinary Russians through direct cash payments tied to military service.

**Smertonomika: Death Economics as Stimulus**

Inozemtsev coined the term "smertonomika" — death economics — to describe the sixth and perhaps most striking factor sustaining Russia's economy. After the partial military mobilization of 2022 sparked real social discontent, Putin responded by dramatically increasing financial incentives for soldiers and their families. Monthly military pay is now six times what it was in 2022. Signing bonuses have risen from zero to $20,000–$40,000. Death benefits for fallen soldiers now range from $130,000 to $180,000 — more, Inozemtsev notes, than a median Russian worker aged 35 could expect to earn for the rest of his life.

Russia is losing an estimated 30,000 soldiers per month. In a dynamic Inozemtsev describes as "grotesque dynamic pricing," the benefits vary by the military's need for troops and by local governments competing to fill quotas and impress the Kremlin. Banks have also been ordered to forgive up to the equivalent of $120,000 in defaulted debt for recruits, at no cost to the government. Inozemtsev calls this a form of "helicopter money" notable for its efficiency: "No one steals it. There is no corruption. There is no mismanagement."

Because recruits come disproportionately from impoverished and peripheral regions, these payments function as macroeconomic stimulus in areas that would otherwise stagnate — what Inozemtsev calls "a kind of macabre Keynesianism."

**Social Pacification and the War Economy Trap**

The smertonomika system has sorted Russian society in a way that dampens opposition. Because poor and desperate volunteers from neglected regions are doing the fighting, wealthy urban Russians no longer face the threat of forced conscription. Without that personal stake, they have little incentive to protest. "Why should they care if they are not affected by the danger of mobilization?" Inozemtsev asks.

This political stability comes with an embedded trap. Putin needs the war to continue precisely because the war economy is what's keeping things running. When fighting stops, the military spending machine shuts down, and Russia will have to reckon with the full damage: a workforce decimated by casualties and emigration, degraded civilian economic sectors, and defense outlays that have consumed up to half of the federal budget. As the episode concludes: "Russia's been mortgaging its future to keep its economy rolling. But eventually, the debt will have to be paid."

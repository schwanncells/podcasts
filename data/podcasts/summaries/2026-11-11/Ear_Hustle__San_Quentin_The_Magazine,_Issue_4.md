# Ear Hustle — "San Quentin: The Magazine, Issue 4"

**Hosts:** Nigel Poor, Erlon Woods. **Inside producers and interns:** Tony Tafoya, Sadiq Davis, Tom Wynn, Jason Jenkins, Aaron Ramsey, Sammy Nichols.

- **$239 million Learning Center opened at San Quentin with new Ear Hustle studio** — features three office-park-style buildings, elevators (many incarcerated people's first in decades), and new production workspace for the podcast team.
- **Planned cafe with $16.90/hour minimum wage jobs fell through** — two incarcerated residents, Bert and Carrington, were hired to work in the Learning Center cafe at California's minimum wage (vs. 16–64 cents/hour standard prison pay), but the operating company pulled out before opening.

**The Learning Center: A New Landscape and Small Miracles**

San Quentin spent $239 million on a new Learning Center, a group of three office-park-style buildings that house the upgraded Ear Hustle studio and production workspace. Inside producer Tony Tafoya centered his story on the elevators—a detail that reveals how transformative infrastructure can be. For many incarcerated people, these are the first elevators they have accessed in years or decades. Tafoya rides with incarcerated residents who describe the experience with wonder: one man, after 45 years inside, recalls grabbing his mother's hand as a child on elevators and now marvels at the echo in the enclosed space. Another resident, speaking in the moving quiet of the elevator, describes it as the only place where the door closes and you are momentarily unreachable—a "reset" before returning to the yard. The elevator story is short but carries emotional weight precisely because it captures how a mundane feature of freedom takes on profound significance behind bars.

**The Cafe That Never Was**

Intern Jason Jenkins reported on a much larger promise: the Learning Center was to house a cafe staffed by incarcerated residents at California's minimum wage of $16.90 per hour—a staggering jump from the standard 16–64 cents an hour paid for prison labor. Jenkins interviewed two residents hired for the positions: Bert, who dressed in his "crispiest" blue button-up for the interview, and Carrington, who imagined sending money home to his son's mother and envisioned driving a black Nissan Altima with gold rims when released. Both were recommended by staff. The emotional stakes were high—Bert fantasized about ordering lattes and feeling "like a productive member of society." But the operating company pulled out before the cafe opened. Bert, who had been skeptical from the start, absorbed the disappointment more easily than Carrington, who had allowed himself to hope. At the time of Jenkins' reporting, rumors circulated that a new operator had been found, but residents had learned not to hold their breath.

**Tablets, Notifications, and the Cruelty of Almost**

Tom Wynn's story captures a technological promise that arrived, beckoned hope, and vanished. In May, San Quentin distributed new Securus tablets—upgraded from older models—with rumors of unprecedented capability: residents would be able to *receive* phone calls from family and friends outside. Until this moment, incarcerated people could only initiate calls. The prospect moved residents deeply. Michael reflected that "to know that people is thinking of you and wanting to reach out to you, I think that's a blessing." Sadiq imagined notifications proving his daughter wanted to talk to him—a proof of being loved.

The feature arrived in June. Residents saw notifications that loved ones were trying to call them. But when they pressed accept, nothing happened. The notification system was not backed by actual call acceptance. Two weeks later, the notification feature was disabled entirely. Wynn's narration captures the bitterness: "After 23 years in prison, you'd think I'd be used to the way promises we get from prison staff never pan out. But this one hurt." Despite the letdown, Sadiq expressed that even seeing a notification—proof that someone wanted to reach him—was better than nothing. The episode notes that Angie, another resident, had actually found the *idea* of being reachable unsettling, describing "communicative alienation" as comfortable after so long.

**Gaming as Connection and Healing**

Intern Aaron Ramsey's story offers a counterweight: video games at San Quentin create genuine fellowship. Skunk Works, a group of residents, organizes monthly gaming events featuring Madden, Mortal Kombat, and Red Dead Redemption. The games bring together people who would not normally associate. Jay described bonding with strangers over a single game of Madden and later discussing families and childhoods. For some residents, gaming echoes lost time with children outside: Jay remembered late-night fishing sessions with his son in Red Dead Redemption and said that being able to play inside and tell his son about it "creates that everlasting bond that we still have." The story captures how video games, often stereotyped as isolating, function here as a therapeutic and social bridge.

**The P-38 Problem: Cans, Cuts, and Unintended Weapons**

Sound designer Sadiq Davis reported on San Quentin's switch from pouch-packaged food (tuna, beef) to canned goods. The change requires a P-38 military can opener—a tiny, inch-long tool that looks like a door hinge. Opening a single can takes two to three minutes and leaves cuts and bruises on the index finger; opening three or four cans per day causes visible indentations. Davis speculated that the prison uses P-38s rather than standard can openers because the latter can be fashioned into weapons. But cans themselves present a threat: Jason shared that a cellmate once threatened him by putting a can of chili in a sock, leveraging it as a blunt weapon. The story balances multiple perspectives—some residents prefer cans because the food tastes fresher, others want the pouches back. The P-38 became the episode's illustration, signaling its unexpected centrality.

**Food Jackers and Earned Celebration**

Intern Sammy Nichols explored a prison phenomenon: food jackers. Events celebrating completion of self-help programs (anger management, etc.) feature outside catering—real chicken, chocolate chip cookies, Sprite. Incarcerated people who did not take the program sneak into these events purely for the meal. Richard Bullock, a long-term resident who has "never tasted a good pie" or "real fried chicken," justified food jacking as access to food unavailable through normal channels: "You can't find that. You can't buy that." But Raheem, one of the "bulldogs" (informal security) at events, pushed back with moral force. He had used the programs himself to process the deaths of his granddaughter, twin sister, and niece. The graduation celebrations, he said, were "safe spaces to release" and "comforting." Food jackers, he argued, steal joy from people who earned it: "Don't take that joy from somebody else because you don't want it. Especially not just over no piece of food." Nichols' narration ends with dry humor—"Those soft batch cookies are delicious"—capturing the real tension between empathy for both sides.

**The Internship and Creative Growth**

Throughout the magazine, the inside team and six interns (Jason, Aaron, Sammy, plus regulars Tom, Sadiq, Tony) share production feedback. Hosts Nigel Poor and Erlon Woods comment on each story, praising specificity, voice development, and emotional arc. Tom reflects on learning to trust collaborators after working as a solo musician; Aaron notes that the story challenges stereotypes about video games. The interns bring fresh energy and different story sensibilities, while the regular producers mentor them—a deliberate knowledge transfer that enriches the show.

The issue ends with a teaser for Ear Hustle Plus, where a recorded conversation with filmmaker Lynn Novick (director of *Crime and Punishment in America*, a Ken Burns-produced docuseries) explores the gap between American ideals and lived reality in the criminal justice system.

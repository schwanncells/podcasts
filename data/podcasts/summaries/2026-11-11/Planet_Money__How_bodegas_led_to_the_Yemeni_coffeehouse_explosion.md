# Planet Money — "How bodegas led to the Yemeni coffeehouse explosion"

**Host:** Amanda Aronchik, Jeff Kuo. **Guest:** Hakeem Soleimani (entrepreneur, founder of Yaffa Cafe in Brooklyn; son of Tarek Soleimani, Yemeni-American bodega owner since 1993); Bill Kerr (economist, Harvard Business School, researcher on immigration and labor clustering).

## TL;DR

- **Yemeni coffeehouses exploding across the US** — a couple hundred have opened in the past nine years, filling a gap for late-night third spaces serving Muslim and Arab American communities who wanted family-friendly alternatives to bars and clubs.
- **Yemen's historical coffee monopoly** — Yemen is one of the birthplaces of the coffee trade and has deep roots in coffee culture, giving Yemeni Americans a natural position to lead this trend.
- **Yemenis dominate US bodega business** — according to the Yemeni American Merchants Association, Yemenis run nearly half of all bodegas in New York City (~5,000 stores) and small grocery stores across Detroit, Buffalo, Fresno, Oakland, and thousands of stores nationwide, despite making up just one-fortieth of one percent of the US population.
- **Immigrant clustering driven by community networks** — economist Bill Kerr found that smaller immigrant populations with stronger internal social bonds (measured by in-group marriage rates) are more likely to concentrate in a single industry; Yemenis, Nepalis, Senegalese, and Zimbabweans rank highest in clustering.
- **Economic advantage to ethnic industry clustering** — immigrants earn higher incomes and wages by working in their community's specialized industry compared to self-employment in other sectors or wage work elsewhere.
- **Second-generation immigrants innovate on family playbooks** — Hakeem left his father's bodega to pursue photography, but saw the gap in specialty coffee shops and opened Yaffa Cafe, building on skills and community connections from the bodega but upgrading to specialty coffee ($6–$7 drinks vs. his father's cheaper drip coffee).
- **War and logistics barriers overcome with improvisation** — when Yemen's ports closed during the civil war, Hakeem smuggled coffee beans across the Saudi border and used Western Union to pay farmers after PayPal was shut down.

**The Rise of Yemeni Coffeehouses**

Hakeem Soleimani represents a new generation capitalizing on his community's entrepreneurial tradition. He opened Yaffa Cafe in Brooklyn in 2019, one of the first in what has become a rapid nationwide expansion. Yemeni coffeehouses now number in the hundreds, offering late-night service, pastries like honeycomb with cheese, and an atmosphere designed as a third space for Muslim and Arab American families—a place that felt family-friendly and welcoming where previous options like bars and clubs did not fit cultural preferences.

The success taps into Yemen's deep historical role in the global coffee trade, stretching back centuries. This cultural and historical connection gave Yemeni Americans a natural advantage in positioning themselves as leaders in the specialty coffee movement, particularly as younger generations moved beyond their parents' bodega model.

**The Bodega Foundation: Yemeni Dominance in Corner Stores**

Hakeem's father, Tarek, arrived in the US from Yemen in the mid-1990s during Yemen's civil war, following a family chain-migration pattern. His brother was already established in the bodega business and brought Tarek into it immediately. At the time, Tarek spoke no English and learned the job on site, working 12- to 15-hour days. Without a credit history, he couldn't secure bank loans, so he relied on interest-free loans from family and friends following Islamic tradition.

Today, according to the Yemeni American Merchants Association, Yemenis run nearly half of all bodegas in New York City—approximately 5,000 stores—and operate thousands of small grocery stores across major cities including Detroit, Buffalo, Fresno, and Oakland. This dominance is striking given that Yemenis comprise only one-fortieth of one percent of the US population.

**Why Immigrant Groups Cluster in Single Industries**

Economist Bill Kerr from Harvard Business School has studied why some immigrant groups concentrate in one industry while others disperse. He identified two main factors: smaller populations tend to cluster more, and populations with stronger internal social cohesion—measured by in-group marriage rates and shared community activities—cluster more intensively.

Kerr's analysis of 40 years of census data ranked 131 immigrant groups by clustering. At the top were people from Nepal, Senegal, Zimbabwe, and Yemen. At the bottom were Cubans, Canadians, Germans, and Poles. The clustering pattern emerges partly from circumstance (sometimes arbitrary, as with Vietnamese nail salons, which began when actress Tippi Hedren taught manicuring to refugees in the 1970s) and partly from deliberate community networking—shared language, weekend gatherings, and informal knowledge exchange at community centers.

Hakeem illustrated this dynamic through his own experience. When learning the coffee business, he read "The Monk of Mocha," a book about a Yemeni-American coffee importer, and called cousins back in Yemen to identify and inspect coffee farms. His family provided both the initial supply (his father hand-carried 50 pounds of beans in a suitcase) and the local knowledge needed to scale the business.

**The Economic Advantage of Clustering**

Kerr's research showed that immigrants earn higher incomes and wages by specializing in their community's industry compared to self-employment in other sectors or traditional wage work. This holds true even in industries with thin margins and grueling hours, like bodega work. The advantage stems from informal knowledge transfer—suppliers, regulatory navigation, operational best practices—that is available only within the network.

**The Second-Generation Shift: Building on, Not Abandoning, Family Foundations**

Unlike many second-generation immigrants who leave their parents' industries entirely (as often happens with Vietnamese nail-salon workers), Hakeem both left and then returned to his family's entrepreneurial model. As a teenager and college student, he worked shifts at his father's bodega but envisioned a different path—he pursued photography as a freelancer after graduation.

Frustration with finding a good workspace to do photo editing led him to a realization: his neighborhood lacked the kind of late-night coffee shop where he could work and study. He pitched the idea to his cousin, inspired by "The Monk of Mocha," and they began planning Yaffa Cafe. His father was skeptical—"Why would anyone spend more than $2 on a cup of coffee?"—and couldn't envision the market his son was targeting.

When Hakeem found a space three doors from his father's bodega (ironically offered by a landlord who respected Tarek's long tenure), he had solved multiple problems at once: high foot traffic near a subway, proximity to an established customer base, and family business connections that made the lease possible. His father now buys cappuccinos at Yaffa Cafe every day (free of charge), a symbolic shift from skepticism to acceptance.

**Overcoming Wartime Import Barriers**

The biggest operational challenge Hakeem faced was scaling coffee imports from Yemen. His initial approach—hand-carrying beans in suitcases—worked briefly but was unsustainable. When Yemen's civil war closed major ports and airports in 2019, legitimate import routes became impossible. To keep his supply chain alive, Hakeem hired smugglers to move beans across the northern border into Saudi Arabia, then arranged shipment to the US. The banking system's dysfunction meant PayPal transactions were blocked; he switched to Western Union to pay farmers.

These improvised solutions—relying on smuggling networks and remittance services—kept the coffee flowing despite geopolitical chaos. Hakeem has since expanded to two locations and is adding a full kitchen and dinner menu to the original Yaffa Cafe, cementing the shift from his father's bodega model to a new generation's specialty coffee vision.

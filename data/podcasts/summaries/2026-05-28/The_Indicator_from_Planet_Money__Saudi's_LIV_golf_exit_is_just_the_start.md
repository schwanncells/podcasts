# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Saudi's LIV golf exit is just the start"

**Host:** Wailin Wong, Darian Woods. **Guests:** Alan Shipnock (golf writer; author of *Live and Let Die*; son of an economist); Andrew Lieber (political scientist, Tulane University; non-resident fellow, Carnegie Endowment for International Peace).

**Vision 2030 and the Soft Power Gambit**

In 2016, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman announced Vision 2030, a diversification strategy to reduce Saudi Arabia's dependence on oil. The government would deploy its sovereign wealth fund—the Public Investment Fund—on both domestic and international ventures. Golf became part of this vision, driven in part by Yasser al-Rumayan, the fund's governor and chairman of the state-owned Saudi oil company, who personally loves the sport. But beyond individual preference, the Saudis recognized golf as a potent diplomatic tool. Shipnock notes that while economic forums and nighttime drinks offer limited access to power, "you can go out and spend five hours with the people who run the United States economy and run the United States government." The bonds formed on a golf course, he adds, are "profound."

**The 2019 Tournament and the Khashoggi Lesson**

Saudi Arabia's first international golf tournament in 2019 took place just months after Saudi agents assassinated Washington Post journalist Jamal Khashoggi; U.S. intelligence later determined that Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman approved the operation, though he has denied this. Despite international outcry, the tournament proceeded, and Saudi Arabia paid golfers $20 million in appearance fees—an unprecedented sum at the time. Shipnock emphasizes that the Saudis learned a crucial lesson: "you can buy the goodwill of professional golf, you can buy the services of the players, and they will follow the money."

**LIV Golf's Rise and Its Structural Weakness**

In 2021, the Public Investment Fund committed to backing LIV Golf Investments, with Hall of Famer Greg Norman as CEO. The league lured major players (Phil Mickelson, Dustin Johnson) with lavish signing bonuses, disrupting the PGA Tour's long-standing monopoly on professional golf in the United States. LIV also required venues, turning to President Trump, who owns a network of golf courses. Trump had long been excluded from elite, private clubs like Seminole, Augusta National, and Cypress Point—clubs that "don't like new money" and rejected him based on conduct and background. By hosting LIV tournaments, Trump found the prestige and access he could never buy into the traditional golf establishment. The arrangement created what Shipnock calls "very unprecedented dynamics where a foreign government was paying a former president and now a sitting president to host tournaments."

Yet despite billions in capital, world-class players, and Trump's backing, LIV struggled to generate audience engagement or sporting legitimacy. Shipnock observes that the league was consumed by its macro narrative—money, sportswashing, and Trump—rather than by the competition itself. When asked to name five great moments in LIV Golf history, even diehard fans "would stumble to get past one or two."

**The Saudi Pivot Away from International Soft Power**

After investing $5 billion into LIV Golf, Saudi Arabia soured on the venture. Last month, the Public Investment Fund announced it would not fund the league beyond the current season, leaving LIV scrambling for alternative capital. Some players who defected to the league are attempting to return to the PGA Tour's good graces, and LIV has postponed events, including a tournament scheduled for New Orleans.

Andrew Lieber, a political scientist at Tulane University, argues that Saudi Arabia's broader retreat from international cultural projects stems from a fundamental miscalculation about leverage. The Saudis believed that pledging investments and making "almost direct payments to the president's family" would "ensure significant influence over what the United States does in the Middle East region" and forestall major conflict. The 2025 Iran war proved this theory wrong. Lieber notes: "That theory has now gone out the window." Additionally, even before the war, Saudi officials were facing domestic pressure to justify massive spending on foreign initiatives. Lieber explains that citizens questioned "where our job's going to come from, but where our good job's going to come from that are going to enable Saudis to live lives similar to what they see in neighboring wealthier Gulf states." Saudi Arabia, despite being the largest economy in the Gulf, has lower per capita income than Qatar and the United Arab Emirates.

**The Domestic Alternative**

Rather than pursue international prestige projects, Saudi Arabia is shifting its sovereign wealth fund toward sectors it believes will benefit the domestic economy: data centers, AI development, and the Lucid electric vehicle company, which is opening Saudi Arabia's first-ever car factory. Interestingly, Vision 2030's cultural loosening—removing restrictions on public entertainment and gender mixing—has borne domestic fruit: a nascent film industry, a burgeoning comedy scene, and Arabic-language podcasts. These developments required no massive international spending. Lieber notes that such outcomes "did bear some fruit, just not on the international stage."

As for what comes next, Lieber has heard rumblings of a revised plan called Vision 2040. Given the mixed track record of marquee international investments and economic pressures from the Iran conflict, it is unlikely that Saudi Arabia will attempt another venture like LIV Golf.

# Science Friday — "Surveying wildlife along Lewis and Clark's route, 220 years later"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guests:** Dr. Roland Kays (head of the Biodiversity Lab, North Carolina Museum of Natural Sciences; leading a cross-country camera-trap expedition retracing Lewis and Clark's route); Dr. Ruth Oliver (ecologist, Bren School of Environmental Science and Management, UC Santa Barbara; lead author of a new *Science* study on human-wildlife interactions using cell phone and GPS collar data).

## TL;DR

- **220 years later, wildlife is broadly doing better than 100 years ago** — protected areas, bag limits, hunting seasons, and the Endangered Species Act reversed near-extinctions; Kays's team spotted 15 bald eagles in a single day on the Missouri River.
- **Near Pittsburgh, species absent in 1804 are now abundant** — sandhill cranes (not observed by Lewis at all), deer, and turkey are common at wildlife-friendly farms north of the city; passenger pigeons, which Lewis saw in flocks, went extinct.
- **Prairie dogs have lost over 90% of their historical range and are still being exterminated on public lands with government funding** — killed for competing with cattle, despite being native; Kays called the ongoing extermination "crazy."
- **Animals in less-developed areas are more disturbed by human presence than those in urban areas** — Oliver's study found cougars shrink their home range more sharply in response to humans in rural landscapes than in developed ones, a habituation effect.
- **Gray wolves expand their home range when humans are present, amplified in rural areas** — the long history of wolf persecution in North America may force them to cover more ground to avoid contact.
- **Cell phone device data paired with GPS collar tracking opened a new research window** — human location data exists commercially but is siloed behind ad-industry paywalls; Oliver's study is among the first to combine it with animal movement data at scale.

**A Modern Expedition Along Lewis and Clark's Route**

Dr. Roland Kays and his team are retracing the Lewis and Clark route by van and kayak, coordinating with over 100 scientists across the country running camera traps to document wildlife in a systematic way. The expedition, timed to the nation's 250th birthday, generates a region-wide snapshot of wildlife that can be compared to Lewis and Clark's journal entries. Kays acknowledges the comparison isn't statistically robust — the Corps of Discovery lacked camera traps — but the data is powerful for identifying broad patterns and telling stories about which species have recovered and which face new pressures.

The overarching finding, Kays says, is one of unexpected optimism. Threats that dominated the early 1900s — overhunting and unsustainable take with no regulatory framework — were largely addressed through protected areas, hunting regulations, and the Endangered Species Act. Bald eagles, once nearly extinct, now number in the dozens along a single day's paddle of the Missouri. Sandhill cranes, absent from Lewis's Pittsburgh-area journals, now appear daily at wildlife-friendly farms north of the city.

**What Went Right — and What's Still Wrong**

Near the American Prairie Reserve in Montana, bison recovery illustrates the new category of challenge: habitat compression. Less than a fraction of native prairie remains after conversion to farmland and ranchland. Kays distinguishes the problem clearly — 100 years ago the issue was overuse; today it's a crowded planet asking whether oil infrastructure, food production, and wildlife can share the same landscape.

The most urgent under-the-radar case Kays flagged came from South Dakota State University ecologist Amanda Cheeseman: prairie dogs have lost more than 90% of their range and are still being actively killed on public lands with government money because ranchers view them as competition for cattle. Kays was direct: "We've got plenty of cattle. Let's make room for these prairie dogs." The memorable field moment Kays described was a baby duckling fleeing a diving bald eagle that jumped into the team's kayak for protection — a vivid sign of a recovering raptor population.

**Big Data, Human Presence, and Wildlife Disturbance**

Dr. Ruth Oliver's *Science* paper, co-authored with Scott Yanko at the Smithsonian, takes a data-intensive approach to the same underlying question: how do humans affect wildlife behavior day to day? Her study paired GPS collar tracking on animals with anonymized, aggregated cell phone device counts — one of the first to do so at scale. Oliver noted that the human location data is abundant but largely inaccessible to science, locked behind commercial advertising contracts.

The central finding: animals responded more strongly to human presence in less-developed areas than in urbanized ones. Cougars shrink their home range when human density rises — but shrink it even more sharply when that density occurs in a rural rather than developed context. Oliver's interpretation is habituation: animals in frequently human-occupied spaces learn to tolerate people; those in wilder areas treat humans as a novel predator. Gray wolves showed the opposite movement pattern — expanding their range in response to human presence, again more dramatically in rural settings — possibly because the long history of persecution forces them to cover more ground to avoid contact.

The practical implication Oliver draws: conservation gains may be achievable through smarter temporal and spatial policies about when and where humans enter landscapes, not just through habitat protection alone.

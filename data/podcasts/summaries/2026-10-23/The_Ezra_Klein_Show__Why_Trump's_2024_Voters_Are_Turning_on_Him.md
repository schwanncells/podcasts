# The Ezra Klein Show — "Why Trump's 2024 Voters Are Turning on Him"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Sarah Longwell (political consultant; founder and publisher of The Bulwark; conducted hundreds of focus groups with thousands of voters since 2016; host of focus group podcast; author of *How to Eat an Elephant*).

## TL;DR

- **Trump's approval collapsed from 50%+ to 37.5% in less than two years** — caused by two specific policy choices voters identify directly with him: unilateral tariffs and the war with Iran, both of which raised prices and contradicted his core promises.
- **Prices are the dominant issue overriding everything else** — voters don't engage with policy rhetoric; they notice when ground beef costs $9 and eggs don't fall, and this simple fact drives evaluations of Trump more than corruption cases, legal fights, or geopolitical arguments.
- **Corruption broke through only because prices are high** — voters tolerated Trump's first-term corruption and crypto scams when they thought he could help them economically; now that they're suffering, brazen conflicts like Jared Kushner negotiating Russian oil sales to Trump family friends become salient.
- **Iran war promises kept failing — "I thought it was going to be like Venezuela"** — voters expected a quick regime change; instead it dragged on, gas prices spiked, costs rose across supply chains, and Trump kept saying it was over when it wasn't.
- **Data centers are now a dominant election issue** — voters in key Senate races see data centers as raising electricity costs, destroying communities, and displacing jobs for long-term automation, yet they vote against them locally and find they're "already a done deal" because decisions were made without public input.
- **John Ossoff, Josh Shapiro, and Pete Buttigieg are breaking through with Trump voters** — they do constituent services, show up in swing states, and level with voters about complicated problems without pretending to have all the answers, in contrast to Trump's dismissal of concerns as "hoaxes."
- **Trump voters still supporting him cite cultural grievances** — they emphasize his stance on biological sex in sports and opposition to "wokeness," and they're deeply tribalized; this core group is unlikely to budge, but their lack of enthusiasm may suppress turnout.
- **The midterm hinges on vibes and turnout more than traditional polling** — Democrats have motivation (opposing ICE, corruption, war, prices), while Republicans face dampened enthusiasm from Trump voters who are sad and annoyed; Longwell suspects a "wave" election against Republicans.

---

**The Price-Driven Collapse**

Longwell attributes Trump's historic 30-point approval drop to two concrete policy choices voters directly tie to him: unilateral tariffs and the Iran war. Both raised prices at the pump and in grocery stores, betraying his primary promise to lower costs. She emphasizes that voters do not engage with policy complexity or justifications; they notice lived experience. A woman in one of Longwell's focus groups captured this perfectly: "I grocery shop every week. So like, I know when prices are going down and up and, like, I'm like, he's lying. Like, nothing is. OK, maybe the eggs like have gone down, but like that's it. I've never paid nine dollars for ground beef before ever."

This gap between elite discourse and voter reality explains why policy arguments fall flat. Longwell notes that members of Congress and decision-makers don't walk into stores and recalculate their purchases when prices jump $2; they have no visceral sense of the squeeze ordinary Americans experience. Voters feel forgotten, and when promises to lower prices—Trump's core 2024 pitch—turn into price hikes driven by his own choices, they withdraw trust.

**The "America First" Promise and Perceived Self-Interest**

Voters who swung toward Trump in 2024 believed in "America First," interpreting it as a promise that the president would prioritize everyday Americans over international commitments and overseas spending. Longwell describes this as voters saying "we want to be taken care of. We want money spent on our schools." They saw Trump's businessman brand and his *Apprentice* persona as proof he could cut through bureaucracy and deliver results.

What they didn't account for was that Trump's personal interests diverged from theirs. While voters imagined him using magic businessman skills to lower grocery prices, he was actually focused on himself. The inflation promise failed not because of external forces but because Trump didn't prioritize it—and voters now recognize that disconnect.

**Corruption Broke Through Because of Economic Pain**

Corruption has surprisingly become the top issue in Fox News polling, outranking immigration, healthcare, and even prices in some measures. Longwell initially expected this to matter during Trump's first term—it didn't. Voters tolerated his golf spending, meetings at his hotels, and crypto scams from his family. The difference now is that voters are suffering economically, which makes Trump's visible enrichment—Jared and Ivanka's multi-billion-dollar Albanian resort funded by foreign money, Stephen Whitcoff co-founding Trump's World Liberty crypto business—feel like betrayal rather than background noise.

The corruption issue broke through not on its own merits but as an expression of frustration: Trump is not focused on them; he's focused on himself. And when voters are struggling, that self-focus feels unforgivable.

**The Epstein Files and the Death of "Anti-Establishment Trump"**

One specific promise Trump made but never kept was releasing the Epstein files. Longwell notes this matters to a specific strain of voters—particularly young men—who believed Trump would be a truth-teller, exposing elite pedophile networks and hidden conspiracies. His failure to declassify Epstein material broke trust with these voters. More broadly, the Epstein moment symbolizes the end of Trump's anti-establishment brand.

In his first term, Trump could claim to be president *and* anti-establishment, fighting the deep state. Now he *is* the establishment. MAGA controls the government. There's no hidden enemy preventing him from releasing files or ending wars—it's just him, making choices for his own benefit. That loss of the anti-establishment alibi is profound.

**The Iran War: Promises Unfulfilled**

Trump voters expected the Iran conflict to be swift, like the 2002 Venezuela analogy—get in, get out, declare victory. Instead, it became a grinding war. Trump repeatedly claimed it was over; it wasn't. Gas prices spiked, supply chains fractured, food and farming costs rose across the Midwest, and voters connected the dots: this war is why my costs are up.

Younger Trump voters, particularly under 35, blame Netanyahu and Israel for pulling Trump into the war, though Longwell notes this reasoning sometimes slides into antisemitic territory—the idea that "Israel controls President Trump." This framing, while politically potent, is dangerous and likely to outlast Trump's presidency.

**The Data Center Issue Emerges as Unexpected Powerhouse**

Data centers have become a dominant issue in midterm races—Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, Josh Tredick in Iowa, and others. Longwell was surprised by the issue's salience when it appeared organically in 2025 focus groups. Voters object for multiple overlapping reasons: rising electricity rates, visual and environmental blight, and fear that these facilities are being built to automate their jobs away.

Critically, voters turn out to local hearings and city councils to vote against data centers—and lose anyway. Deals were already struck. Farmers already sold their land. Voters realize that despite their voice, decisions were made without them. This compounds the sense of political powerlessness that Trump once capitalized on by promising to fight the rigged system. Instead, Trump sides with the "all-in" podcast billionaires, calling data center skepticism a "hoax" and claiming they're "the oil of the next 20, 25 years."

Trump's dismissal of voter concerns as lies is a stark departure from his 2016 brand. He's not explaining why data centers might benefit communities long-term; he's simply telling voters to stop caring. Democrats have an opportunity to walk voters through the complicated tradeoffs—acknowledging uncertainty, refusing to pick the opposite side reflexively, and demonstrating actual governance thinking. Longwell notes that neither party has fully figured out how to talk about AI and data centers because the issue is genuinely unsettled.

**The Immigration Paradox**

Immigration remains Trump's strongest issue, with him polling around 43% approval on it compared to low 20s on prices and war. Voters do credit him with closing the border, a broadly popular position. But his administration, under Stephen Miller, has pursued aggressive mass deportations that are creating a different kind of disorder than voters wanted.

Trump voters distinguishing between bad illegal immigrants and people paying taxes who've lived in the country for 25 years. They want the border closed but don't support masked ICE agents showing up at schools or dragging parents to the streets. Longwell emphasizes that most Trump voters aren't reflexively anti-immigrant; they're pro-fairness. They believe people should immigrate "the right way," though most Americans don't actually understand how difficult legal immigration is.

Longwell pushes back against the stereotype that all Trump voters are racist. She notes that voters make distinctions—criminals out, working undocumented immigrants alone—and this nuance gets lost in media coverage. What voters objected to was disorder and chaos; seeing masked federal agents create violence in the streets communicated that Trump isn't providing order either.

**Breaking Through to Trump Voters: Ossoff, Shapiro, Buttigieg**

John Ossoff has been extraordinarily effective at peeling off Trump voters in Georgia. His strategy differs from most Democrats: he doesn't overwhelming voters with a laundry list of Trump's crimes; instead, he walks them through a single story, slowly, step by step. He invites voters into a narrative about Trump's venality—how Jared and Steve Whitcoff are brokering Russian oil field sales to Trump family friends. This granular, methodical approach breaks through where sweeping corruption indictments become white noise.

Josh Shapiro in Pennsylvania and Pete Buttigieg, who's holding town halls in red states and refusing to accept "permanent red states," are similarly effective. Longwell notes that they do constituent services—voters call and get help. Shapiro won his governorship race by massive margins in the largest swing state. Buttigieg is holding conversations rather than delivering talking points.

Longwell is suspicious of California politicians running for national office because they lack gut-level instinct for swing-state voters. The politicians breaking through come from competitive places, built their careers there, and demonstrate real governance. Voters want to feel that a candidate cares about them. Trump conned them into believing he cared when he didn't; they're now desperately vulnerable to any candidate who appears to actually take them seriously.

**The Remnant of Trump's Base**

A core of Trump voters remains enthusiastically supportive. The top reason they cite isn't prices or tariffs or even the Iran war—it's cultural issues. They highlight Trump's stance on biological sex in women's sports, his opposition to "woke" corporate culture, and the cultural fights where they believe Democrats are strange or extreme. These voters are heavily polarized and tribal, so committed to Trump or opposition to Democrats that they won't acknowledge his failures.

Longwell worries about the Republican Party's future without Trump because his identity has become fused with these voters' sense of self. They're "dug in" and see supporting Trump as part of who they are. This deep identity fusion explains why facts about economic failure or corruption don't move them.

**Vibes, Turnout, and Wave Dynamics**

Longwell emphasizes that polling doesn't capture turnout dynamics or energy. Democrats have clear motivation to vote: opposition to ICE, the Iran war, prices, and corruption. Republicans face suppressed enthusiasm; Trump voters are "sad" and "bummed out." They don't like their candidates. In Georgia, hardcore Trump voters won't vote for John Ossoff, but they see everyone else voting for him and feel annoyed—and many stay home.

The masked ICE agents executing deportations created a signal that something had fundamentally shifted in the political regime. Voters who reacted with alarm will come out to vote. This energy imbalance, combined with Republican demoralization, suggests wave election territory for Democrats. This isn't about whether voters think Democrats can lower prices (they're skeptical); it's about which side has momentum and motivation.

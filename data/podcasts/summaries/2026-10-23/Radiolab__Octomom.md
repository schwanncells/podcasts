# Radiolab — "Octomom"

**Hosts:** Jad Abumrad, Robert Krulwich. **Guest:** Bruce Robeson (deep sea explorer, Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute; led the multi-year observation of brooding octopus).

## TL;DR

- **Octopus broods eggs for 4.5 years without eating** — a deep sea octopus discovered in April 2007 by Monterey Bay research team sat on a clutch of 160 eggs for 54 months, never leaving the rock, starving gradually until the eggs hatched.
- **Nothing was known about this species' brooding period** — scientists had no prior data on how long deep sea octopuses care for their eggs, making this observation unprecedented and unpredictable in real time.
- **She fended off predators while dying** — in year two, crabs circled the weakening octopus "like a witch at a stake"; when the research team returned a week later, they found crab parts scattered on the seafloor and all 160 eggs still intact.
- **Optic glands trigger starvation survival** — two rice-grain-sized glands in the octopus's brain release steroids and insulin during brooding, orchestrating a shutdown of most bodily functions while preserving focus on egg protection.
- **She died moments after the last egg hatched** — the team found tattered egg cases and multiple tiny hatchlings crawling on the rock, with no sign of Octomom; she had apparently stayed until the final baby left.

**The Discovery: A Mother in the Deep**

Bruce Robeson, a deep sea explorer at the Monterey Bay Aquarium Research Institute, was conducting routine surveys of the Monterey Canyon—an underwater chasm nearly as vast and deep as the Grand Canyon—in April 2007. Flying a remote submersible about a mile below the surface, he spotted an octopus moving across the seafloor. The animal, which he and his team nicknamed Octomom, had a mantle the size of a cantaloupe, arms roughly a foot and a half long, and visible scars: a crescent shape on one arm, a circular mark elsewhere.

A month later, when the team returned to the same location, they found Octomom perched on a vertical rock face, her body wrapped around a clutch of 160 jelly-bean-sized eggs. This discovery was significant because almost nothing was known about the brooding behavior of deep sea octopuses. The team could date the eggs to within a month of laying, and now faced an open question: how long would she sit there, unfed, slowly dying?

**Years of Vigil: The Unexpected Length of Brooding**

Whenever the research team had spare dive time during their regular ocean surveys, they would make detours to check on Octomom. What began as occasional check-ups stretched into months, then years. Across multiple visits, she never moved from the rock. The team watched as her body changed: she became pale, lost weight, and her eyes developed cloudiness resembling cataracts.

By May of the first year, she was still there. By months two, three, four—still there, cleaning her eggs with her arms, warding off small crabs and shrimp. As the clock ticked forward through 2007, 2008, 2009, and beyond (during which the iPhone was released, the economy crashed, Obama was elected, Michael Jackson died, same-sex marriage was legalized in New York), Octomom remained on her rock, completely motionless. By year two, she was visibly weaker. By year four, the researchers themselves had begun to wonder how much longer she could possibly endure.

**The Biology of Sacrificial Motherhood**

According to Yan Wong, an evolutionary neuroscientist who studied octopus reproduction, all octopuses follow a similar life trajectory: they grow and eat, reach reproductive maturity, find a mate, lay eggs, then die. The female octopus does not feed while brooding—none of them do. In shallow-water species, this fasting period lasts only about a month. For Octomom, the timescale would prove radically different.

Wong explains that the survival mechanism lies in the octopus's unique nervous system. Using an orchestra analogy, she describes how most of the octopus's brain—vision, motor control, heartbeat regulation, memory—operates normally during ordinary life. But once eggs are laid, a dramatic shift occurs: nearly every system begins to shut down, like an orchestra falling silent. The exception is the optic glands, two tiny structures the size of grains of rice located between the eyes. During brooding, these glands "solo," releasing a complex chemical cascade. They trigger the production of steroids and insulin, enabling the octopus to survive without food for months or years. Meanwhile, outwardly, she looks like a dying, withered creature; inwardly, she is intensely alive in a focused, centered way, devoted entirely to protecting her eggs.

**The Battle with the Crabs**

In year two, the research team arrived to find Octomom encircled by three or four crabs, moving as though staging a coordinated attack. The team considered breaking off crab legs to distract them, as they had attempted earlier, but doubted the gesture would help. They had other research to conduct, so they left. A week later, when they returned, they found the situation reversed. Scattered across the seafloor below Octomom were crab parts—torn to pieces. All 160 eggs remained intact. In her weakened state, Octomom had fought back and won.

**Four and a Half Years: Holding On**

By year four and a half, the total brooding period—over 54 months—set what was apparently the world record for longest egg-brooding period in any animal species. The team had visited month after month, sometimes multiple months apart, always finding her still there, still vigilant, still protecting her eggs as her body deteriorated further. They counted the eggs repeatedly: 160 every time, untouched.

Jad Abumrad observed the poignancy of the situation: just as a dying human mother might wait for a child to come home before letting go, Octomom had somehow timed her death to coincide with the moment her babies were ready to leave.

**The Outcome: A Successful Life Handed Off**

One day in year five, when the team descended toward Octomom's rock, she was gone. In her place lay tattered egg cases. The researchers frantically searched the vicinity for signs of hatchlings or catastrophe. Instead, they found tiny octopuses of her species crawling across the rock, already feeding and growing. They bore the same distinctive features—the crescent scar marking visible on some—confirming they were Octomom's offspring.

Octomom herself had vanished. Bruce believes she was consumed by a scavenger, likely in the moment after the last egg hatched. Her vigil had ended exactly when it needed to: not before, which would have doomed her eggs to predation, and not after, which would have been unnecessary. She had protected 160 babies through four and a half years of starvation and remained at her post until the baton of life could be handed off.

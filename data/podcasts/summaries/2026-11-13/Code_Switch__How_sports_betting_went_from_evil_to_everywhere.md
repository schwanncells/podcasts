# Code Switch — "How sports betting went from 'evil' to everywhere"

**Host:** Gene Demby. **Guests:** Jarrell Ezell (sociologist, University of Chicago; studies addictive behavior and public policy responses), Danny Funt (sports journalist; author of *Everybody Loses: The Tumultuous Rise of American Sports Gambling*).

## TL;DR

- **Sports betting legalized nationwide in 2018 after decades of leagues calling it "evil"** — the Supreme Court decision provided cover, but the real driver was $2 billion in projected annual revenue to leagues through partnerships and boosted viewership, as gambling keeps audiences watching to the end of blowouts.
- **Black and Latino men bet more than any other demographic** — historical lack of formal employment and wage-earning opportunities normalized gambling as a side income source in these communities, and underground sports betting still thrives alongside legal markets.
- **40% of American teenagers aged 13–17 placed a bet in the past year; 10% show high-risk gambling problem indicators** — a Rutgers study published weeks before this episode reveals youth gambling as a larger public health crisis than widely acknowledged, with invisible addiction (unlike alcohol or opioids) making it harder to detect.
- **Harassment of college athletes by angry bettors has become a "wildfire"** — players receive death threats and hate mail over prop bets; a Purdue bench player was harassed after hitting a meaningless three-pointer that covered the spread. An NFL security chief said "it's only a matter of time before someone tries to kill a player who costs them money."
- **Sportsbooks refuse to acknowledge addiction, citing it as "competitive disadvantage"** — the American Gaming Association head said he won't concede sports betting can be addictive. Industry insiders confirm that consumer protections beyond "lowest common denominator" reduce profit, making vulnerable customers the most valuable ones.
- **Intimate partner violence spikes 10% in towns after unexpected NFL losses, and higher still in states with legal gambling** — researchers at University of Oregon document correlation between legalization and violence; the human cost of widespread betting remains largely unaddressed by leagues.
- **Prediction markets (Kalshi, Polymarket) exploit regulatory loopholes by calling themselves "derivatives exchanges"** — they avoid gambling regulations and consumer protections, attract $40+ billion in investment, and enable betting on anything ("if two people could argue about it"), including election outcomes and geopolitical events.
- **Politicians and media have too much financial skin in the game to regulate effectively** — sportsbooks spent hundreds of millions on midterm races through PACs; TV networks profit from gambling-driven viewership. Overseas markets eventually imposed tighter guardrails (advertising limits, jersey sponsorship bans), but prediction markets remain a "whole other ball game" heading to the Supreme Court.

**From "Evil" to Inescapable: The 180-Degree Pivot**

For decades, sports leagues used language of moral condemnation toward gambling. Commissioners repeatedly called it "evil." Yet within a decade after the 2018 Supreme Court decision on PASPA (Professional and Amateur Sports Protection Act), gambling became central to how fans engage with sports—from betting lines peppered throughout broadcasts to apps on everyone's phones. The pivot wasn't primarily driven by legal precedent, Funt explains; the Supreme Court decision merely provided "cover." The real catalyst was financial: gambling industry research showed that bettors watch significantly more sports than average fans and watch through entire blowouts because props and live bets remain in play. Faced with cord-cutting trends and young people abandoning three-hour broadcasts, leagues were presented with a projection: legalize sports betting nationwide and earn over $2 billion annually through direct partnerships and indirect revenue from boosted viewership. For the NFL alone, even that figure justified a complete reversal of position.

**Racialized Targeting and Historical Context**

Ezell and Funt discuss how Black and Latino communities have higher rates of sports betting than white Americans, rooted in historical patterns of exclusion from formal labor markets and wage-earning opportunities. Gambling—particularly the numbers game, which Ezell notes was the biggest Black-owned business in Harlem during the 1920s—became normalized as a side income source when legitimate economic pathways were blocked by law and policy. That normalization persists. Funt observes the marketing strategies of sportsbooks deploy prominent Black actors and comedians—J.B. Smoove for Caesars, Jamie Foxx for MGM, Kevin Hart for DraftKings—to create perceived legitimacy and cultural connection. Funt pressed a Caesars marketing executive on this pattern; the white executive demurred, saying he knew the answer but didn't feel comfortable saying it aloud. Ezell adds that Black and Latino communities still support thriving underground sports betting markets, even as legalization was sold as a way to dismantle them. The industry never drove the black market out; an estimated $50 billion in illegal wagers annually still flows through street bookies and offshore websites, where customers can gamble on credit and avoid bank statements—options legal sportsbooks don't offer.

**Youth Addiction and Invisible Epidemic**

A Rutgers study published in the weeks before this episode found that 40–41% of Americans aged 13–17 had placed a bet in the past year. Among that cohort, 10% showed high-risk indicators of a gambling problem using standard diagnostic criteria. Notably, 40% of all teenage bettors exhibited at least one diagnostic indicator, suggesting a larger at-risk population than clinical addiction alone captures. Funt attributes the invisibility of gambling addiction to its stealth compared to other addictions: there's no smell on the breath, no physical marks. Spouses discover it by accident, if at all. The addiction operates on dopamine and adrenaline, but Ezell emphasizes that sports betting's appeal is uniquely complex because it layers in competition, gender, and racialized elements—making it "arguably one of the most complex addictions" to overcome. Yet the industry resists framing gambling as a mental health disorder. When Texas Tech quarterback Brendan Sorsby disclosed his gambling addiction before the season, many in sports media dismissed it as a cop-out. The American Gaming Association's Bill Miller explicitly told CNN he will not concede that sports betting is addictive—a position medical professionals have agreed on since the 1980s.

**Athlete Harassment and the Violence Pipeline**

Funt begins his book with what he calls "terrifying" examples of a "plague" of harassment targeting college athletes from angry bettors. A bench player on Purdue's men's basketball team, rarely playing but inserted for the final minute of a blowout, made a three-pointer. Unaware, he opened his DMs to a barrage of hate messages: the basket altered the point spread from 30 to 33 points, and bettors who wagered on Purdue to cover felt they'd been robbed. This pattern intensifies with player props—betting on individual performances rather than team outcomes—which Funt notes is now the sportsbooks' biggest profit driver. The microscope on individual players creates exposure to harassment, death threats, stalking at hotels and homes. An NFL former chief security officer told Funt, "It's only a matter of time before someone tries to kill a player who costs them money." When the NCAA addressed the issue, it proposed banning harassers caught through athlete reports—a solution that's reactive and entirely dependent on victims coming forward. At Vanderbilt, Funt found that most football and basketball players confirmed receiving hate mail but said they'd never report it, not wanting to show it was affecting them. The gaps in the safety response leave real vulnerability. Research from the University of Oregon adds another layer: intimate partner violence spikes 10% in towns following unexpected NFL losses, and the spike is even higher in states with legal gambling—data suggesting broader societal harm correlating with normalization of betting.

**The Industry's Addiction Denial**

Sportsbooks employ what Funt calls "window dressing" around responsible gambling while internally treating real consumer protections as a "competitive disadvantage." The American Gaming Association's refusal to acknowledge addiction echoes the playbook of tobacco companies, Funt argues. When pressed on intervention mechanisms, industry insiders confirmed that going beyond the "lowest common denominator" of consumer protections—the bare minimum competitors will tolerate—cuts into the bottom line. The paradox: vulnerable customers are the most valuable customers. If sportsbooks aggressively intervened on addiction signals, they'd lose revenue. Funt adds that this tension exists because gambling, like other addictions, thrives on a perception of control and insider knowledge. Bettors convince themselves they're not like roulette or craps players; they've watched sports intensively, so betting feels like deploying expertise rather than playing chance. It's a "very dangerous mentality," Funt says, because the odds are mathematically identical to casino games, yet the framing makes participation feel obligatory—as if leaving money on the table by not betting is irrational.

**Prediction Markets and Regulatory Arbitrage**

Prediction market platforms like Kalshi and Polymarket sidestep gambling regulations by claiming status as "derivatives exchanges" rather than gambling operations. This distinction carries real consequences: they avoid state-level consumer protections, face federal rather than state oversight, and attract massive investment ($40+ billion for Kalshi). Kalshi's CEO stated his vision plainly: "If there's anything under the sun that two people could argue about, we'd like them to be able to go find a market to trade on that argument"—including elections, wars, and weather. Funt expresses skepticism that every possible bet will draw equal interest (most money stays on sports), but the venture enthusiasm itself signals concern. The framing of prediction markets as investment rather than gambling carries its own irony: "That's actually an indictment of the stock market," Funt notes, highlighting how much Wall Street operates on speculation and risk identical to betting. Ezell adds that this shift mirrors a broader financialization and commodification of everything, from politics to personal outcomes. The potential Supreme Court showdown looms as multiple states have sued, arguing prediction markets are illegal bookmaking by another name and must be regulated at state level.

**Global Regulation and the Path Forward**

Funt points to overseas markets—Europe, Asia, South America—where legal online sports betting preceded the U.S. by years. Those jurisdictions eventually imposed tighter guardrails after things "got out of hand." The English Premier League eliminated gambling sponsors from team jerseys; European soccer leagues banned gambling ads during broadcasts to protect youth exposure. Advertising is Funt's "lowest hanging fruit" for U.S. re-regulation. Yet the financial headwinds are severe. Sportsbooks deployed hundreds of millions through PACs in the midterm elections. TV networks, sports leagues, and politicians all profit from the status quo. Funt remains cautiously optimistic that re-regulation is "likely in the U.S., even despite how much money the industry is willing to spend to fight that." But prediction markets present an unresolved frontier, and Funt's closing observation is sobering: the political will to constrain an industry that's woven into media, politics, and sports infrastructure is uncertain. Gene reflects on the broader cultural shift: betting legitimized a conspiracy-minded thinking pattern—every unexpected result now triggers "the fix is in" suspicion, mirroring political paranoia where losses automatically signal malfeasance. The normalization of betting has, paradoxically, normalized distrust.

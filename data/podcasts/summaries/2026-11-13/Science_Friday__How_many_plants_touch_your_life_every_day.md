# Science Friday — "How many plants touch your life every day?"

**Host:** Ira Flatow. **Guest:** Dr. Lucia Lohman (president and director, Missouri Botanical Garden; George Engelman Professor of Botany, Washington University in St. Louis; 2026 World Ecology Award winner).

## TL;DR

- **8 million dried plant specimens in the Missouri Botanical Garden's herbarium** — the collection also includes 1,500 species in a seed bank and 55,000 living plants, with 3.5 million specimens now digitized online.
- **Three plant species account for 50% of global calorie consumption** — despite 400,000 described plant species, humanity relies on an exceptionally narrow food base.
- **Only 10% of Amazon plant species are known** — expeditions consistently discover at least a dozen new species per trip, and the total undiscovered potential is vast; only 15% of described species have good geographic, genetic, and morphological data.
- **One out of four over-the-counter drugs is plant-derived, but less than 1% of plant species have been tested for medicinal use** — approximately 3,000 species are actively commercialized as medicines out of 400,000 total, representing enormous untapped potential.
- **The Amazon produces 20 trillion liters of water per day via evapotranspiration** — far more than the UK's annual water consumption; this "air conditioning of the world" is more critical than its oxygen production, since local respiration consumes most oxygen produced.
- **A daisy is actually a bouquet of hundreds of flowers** — the outer petals are individual sterile flowers, while the center contains fertile flowers producing seeds.
- **Watermelon flesh is a plant placenta** — the ovary becomes the fruit; the placenta is the tissue between seeds and fruit wall where ovules attach after fertilization.
- **Seedless watermelons and bananas have tiny vestigial seeds, not absent ones** — selective breeding reduced seed size from large, rock-like seeds in ancestral bananas to the barely-visible black spots in modern varieties.

**The Missouri Botanical Garden's Collections and Global Plant Diversity**

Lohman describes the scale of the Missouri Botanical Garden's plant collections: 8 million dried specimens in the herbarium, roughly 1,500 species preserved as seeds in a seed bank, and 55,000 living plants throughout the garden. The institution tracks all these collections using the Living Collections Management System (LCMS) for living plants and a digitization database called Tropicus for herbarium specimens. About 3.5 million specimens have been digitized and are publicly searchable online, with roughly half the total collection now digitized.

The garden acquires specimens through three main channels: field collection (roughly one-third), exchanges with other botanical gardens (another third), and acquisitions from local growers and nurseries (the final third). Lohman emphasizes that the goal is not simply to accumulate specimens but to build coherent collections that represent plant interactions and whole ecological systems.

**Global Plant Diversity and the Narrow Human Diet**

There are approximately 400,000 described plant species, with 350,000 being flowering plants and 50,000 comprising conifers, bryophytes, ferns, and other vascular plants. However, only three plant species account for 50% of the calories consumed globally—a striking concentration of dietary dependence.

**The Amazon Basin and Undiscovered Species**

Lohman has spent decades researching in the Amazon and notes that scientists estimate only 10% of Amazon plant species have been described. Every expedition she has led has returned with at least a dozen new species. Beyond raw numbers, knowledge gaps are severe: even for described plant species, good information on geography, genetics, and morphology exists for only about 15% of them. The diversity of the Amazon stems from multiple factors: the sheer land area, the region's stable tropical climate during ice ages (when other areas froze), the high number of species interactions, and especially the role of river systems in separating populations, driving speciation over time.

**The Amazon as Earth's Air Conditioner**

While commonly called the "lungs of the earth," the Amazon is more accurately described as the world's "air conditioning" system. Although plants produce oxygen, local respiration by animals, trees, fungi, and other organisms consumes most of that oxygen. The Amazon's primary global contribution is hydrological: trees release 20 trillion liters of water into the atmosphere each day through evapotranspiration—more than the United Kingdom consumes in a year. These "flying rivers" of atmospheric water have climate-regulating effects far beyond the forest itself.

**Plant-Derived Medicines and Untapped Potential**

One out of four over-the-counter drugs in pharmacies contains plant material. Yet less than 1% of plant species have been explored for medicinal applications. Approximately 3,000 plant species are actively commercialized as medicines, leaving roughly 397,000 species potentially untested for pharmaceutical properties. The potential for future drug discovery remains enormous.

**Flower Structure: The Daisy as a Bouquet**

What people typically think of as a single daisy flower is actually a dense inflorescence containing hundreds of individual flowers. The outer structures that appear to be petals are actually individual ray flowers, each sterile. The tiny structures at the center of the daisy are the fertile flowers that produce seeds. This structure is an evolutionary strategy: the outer sterile flowers serve to attract pollinators, while the inner fertile flowers handle reproduction.

**Fruit Structure: Watermelon and the Plant Placenta**

When eating a watermelon, one is consuming a plant placenta. After fertilization, the flower's ovary becomes the fruit (the watermelon flesh), the ovules become seeds, and the tissue connecting seeds to the fruit wall—where ovules attach—is the placenta. This anatomical parallel to animal reproduction surprised many in the audience.

**Seedless Watermelons and the Evolution of Seed Size**

Seedless watermelons do not entirely lack seeds; rather, seeds are present but reduced to tiny, barely-visible size through selective breeding. The same pattern applies to modern bananas. Ancestral bananas bore large, rock-like seeds that made them unpleasant to eat. Through multiple generations of genetic crossings and selection, seed size diminished dramatically, resulting in the tiny black dots visible in contemporary bananas.

**Career Advice and the Future of Botany**

Lohman encourages students to pursue botany, noting that curiosity is the essential trait for botanists. She argues that modern business needs botanists across sectors—banking (for carbon credits and biodiversity credits), architecture (for biomimicry and sustainable materials), and engineering (for plant-based innovations). Once one begins observing plants in detail, she notes, plants become visible in every meal and every walk, and appreciation for their ubiquity deepens.

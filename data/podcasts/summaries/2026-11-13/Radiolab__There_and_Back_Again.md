# Radiolab — "There and Back Again"

**Host:** Lulu Miller, Latif Nasser. **Guests:** Martin Wieckowski (managing director, Max Planck Institute for Ornithology; lifelong bird migration researcher); Lucy Cook (author, *The Truth About Animals*); David Wilcoff (evolutionary biologist, Princeton University); Ben Winger (evolutionary biologist, University of Michigan); Ken Lohman (biologist, University of North Carolina); Amanda Sobilewski (University of Florida professor; studies wildebeest migration).

## TL;DR

- **Cattle egrets in Bavaria sparked a 40-year mystery:** An 11-year-old Martin Wieckowski photographed unknown white birds with orange crests pecking at cows' eyes in 1965; no expert knew why African cattle egrets were in Germany, launching his lifelong migration research.
- **African spears proved migration, not hibernation:** In 1822, a German count shot a white stork with an African spear lodged in its neck; at least 25 such storks were found, finally convincing scientists that birds migrate rather than hibernate underwater or fly to the moon (Charles Morton's 1600s theory).
- **Arctic Terns transect the globe chasing sunshine:** Born in perpetual Arctic daylight, Arctic Terns must fly to the Antarctic 10,000+ miles away when the Arctic sun vanishes—one of Earth's longest animal journeys to find identical seasonal conditions.
- **Sea turtles swim 700 miles to return to their birth beach:** Turtles with feeding grounds within 10 miles of alternative nesting beaches instead swim hundreds of miles to nest on the exact beach where they hatched, despite the energy cost, because it's the only place guaranteed to have perfect conditions.
- **Migration tracking reveals individual innovation, not pure instinct:** GPS-tagged storks show highly variable routes; one stork named Hansi abandoned the entire migration to winter alone in a Turkish field, suggesting populations require individual risk-takers to adapt when environments change.
- **Modern tracking reveals migration as a global circulatory system:** MoveBank database animates thousands of tracked animals as colored veins flowing between continents with seasonal rhythms; wildebeest migrations support entire fish ecosystems via decomposing animal carcasses in river crossings.
- **Climate change and habitat loss are collapsing migrations:** Many animals now abandon migration entirely, settling at garbage dumps in Spain or other altered habitats instead of following ancestral routes.
- **A space-based satellite will soon track all migrating animals simultaneously:** Martin placed a data collection box in the space station to receive signals from tagged animals across species—creating a real-time view of Earth's entire migration ballet.

**Martin's Origin Question**

Martin Wieckowski was born in November 1965 in a tiny farming village in Bavaria, Germany. The village, dating back approximately 750 years, had a population of about 50 people, and his family had farmed there for centuries. As a child, Martin grew intimate with the farm animals and local wildlife—kestrels, barn swallows, badgers, and foxes—recognizing individuals and their behaviors as distinct personalities.

At age 11, while herding cows to pasture, Martin saw something entirely new: tall, skinny white birds with orange dinosaur-like crests and long sharp beaks, almost dancing alongside the cows and pecking at their eyes. He photographed them but found no answer among local experts. His biology teacher didn't know. A professor who had written 500-page volumes on Bavarian birds didn't know. Even scientists at the Max Planck Institute for Ornithology—the region's leading bird research center—couldn't explain why African cattle egrets were in Germany. Martin realized that "adults don't know anything about the world. It's just sort of a big enigma." This unanswered question set him on a 40-year journey to understand animal migration.

**Ancient Theories and the African Spear**

Lucy Cook traces how humans have puzzled over bird disappearance for millennia. Aristotle proposed three theories: migration, transmutation (robins changing into red starts), and hibernation. The hibernation theory persisted for centuries despite its illogic—scientists debated whether birds hibernated in trees, crevices, or underwater like fish. In the 1600s, physicist Charles Morton famously argued that swallows must migrate to the moon, which the newly invented telescope showed to have mountains and valleys—"a perfectly reasonable place to holiday if you're a bird."

The breakthrough came on May 1822 when a German count shot a white stork with an African spear lodged through its neck. The professor who examined the spear deduced it was "thrown from the hands of an African." Over the following years, approximately 25 such spear-struck storks were discovered, providing irrefutable evidence that birds migrated to Africa and returned. This inspired scientists to develop bird banding—attaching metal leg bands to track individual journeys. The practice evolved into modern migration science, tracking not just birds but also mammals, fish, whales, sharks, and manta rays across the globe.

**The Unimaginable Journeys**

Modern GPS tracking reveals migration routes of staggering ambition. The Arctic Tern, born in the perpetual daylight of Arctic summers, must chase an endless day by flying 10,000+ miles to the Antarctic when the Arctic sun vanishes, then returning again—essentially transecting the globe twice yearly. A monarch butterfly born in New York State navigates to central Mexico, a place never visited by its parents or grandparents but known only through genetic memory four generations removed. A dragonfly born in a pond is sucked upward by evaporating air, enters a monsoon cloud and jet stream, crosses the Indian Ocean, travels down the East African coast, reverses direction, and returns in a 10,000-mile circular journey, always sheltered within clouds.

Even more puzzling are the animals that refuse to leave home. Sea turtles with feeding grounds in Australia within 5–10 miles of usable nesting beaches instead swim 700 miles to return to the exact beach where they hatched. Alewife fish spend their lives in the ocean but return to the exact freshwater lake of their birth to spawn. From an evolutionary standpoint, this "home sweet home" compulsion makes sense: a female sea turtle lays roughly 2,000 eggs in her lifetime with only about two surviving. A nesting beach must have perfect conditions—correct slope, temperature, sand texture, vegetation, minimal predators, and few egg-eating ants. The only beach guaranteed to have everything exactly right is where she herself hatched. As Ben Winger notes, "The amazing thing about migration is not so much that these organisms accomplish this journey, but that they're doing it just to come back to this one place."

**Visualization and Global Circulation**

When scientists animated MoveBank's tracking database, the patterns resembled blood flow through a body. Colored veins of data points pulse from one region to another as seasons change—purple points flowing north from Africa into Scandinavia in spring, then reversing south in fall. Amanda Sobilewski describes this as "a kind of global circulation between different regions of the world, sometimes really vast regions." The migrations move not just animals but entire ecosystems: thousands of wildebeest cross rivers during African migrations, and up to half of the fish diet in those regions consists of wildebeest carcasses, meaning the animals literally circulate nutrients, feces, and decomposing biomass around the planet. This transforms migration from an isolated curiosity into a planetary circulatory system.

**Individual Variation and Adaptation**

As tracking technology improved, Martin and his team discovered that individual animals behave far more creatively than previously assumed. They don't follow uniform routes like robots. Instead, each stork takes a slightly different path, and some make radical choices. One particularly memorable stork, named Hansi, peeled away from the main migratory flock moving through Eastern Europe toward Africa. Instead, Hansi dropped into a remote field in southeast Turkey near the Syrian border—an area where no stork typically winters. Martin and his partner Ushi followed Hansi across three days, using GPS coordinates and radio signals to track him. When they finally found him at dawn, Hansi was alone in a field "feeding on frogs and snakes and whatnot," apparently thriving in his undiscovered location. Martin speculates that this might represent Darwinian selection: "When you get a population group and they all on average do one thing, nature kind of requires that someone on the edge do something else." These innovative individuals exploring novel routes become crucial for species survival when environments change unpredictably.

However, not all individual variation represents adaptive exploration. Many storks are abandoning migration altogether, settling instead at garbage dumps in Spain and quitting their ancestral journeys. These birds are responding to human-altered habitats rather than discovering new ecological niches.

**The Question of Will vs. Instinct**

The core tension animating migration science is whether animals are "puppets on the end of some kind of genetic code" or whether they possess will, play, and improvisation. David Wilcoff notes that the most pressing question is whether animals can adjust migratory behavior in response to climate change and habitat loss. If all migrations were purely instinctual, any dramatic environmental change would leave entire populations in the wrong place at the wrong season, resulting in mass death. But if every population contains individuals like Hansi—animals willing to deviate from ancestral routes—then the species retains flexibility. Storks learn socially and communicate with each other. If an innovator discovers a rewarding location, others might follow, potentially creating new migratory pathways.

Yet Radiolab's Lucy Cook observed one tagged stork that "basically never really left" its chosen dump, "didn't even bother going back north again in the spring. Just stayed by the dump the whole time." The question of whether modern animals possess genuine agency or are merely responding to environmental stimuli remains unresolved—and increasingly urgent.

**Climate Change and Disappearing Migrations**

Every scientist interviewed emphasized the same troubling reality: migrations are declining and in some cases disappearing entirely. Habitat loss, human barriers, and climate change are collapsing routes that animals have followed for millions of years. The future of animal migration remains uncertain, contingent on humanity's choices about land use and climate.

**Martin's Personal Journey**

Late in his career, Martin decided to experientially understand what migrating animals feel. He purchased a hang glider and flew into mountain thermals, ascending to 4,500 meters while shouting down at climbers on glaciers 50 meters below. He flew through rain and snow, feeling updrafts and downdrafts directly in his body. As he describes it: "In the hang glider you are the bird. You are the body of the bird and you have your wings that you fly left or right and you just fly with your hands, with your just moving your body around." These flights brought him as close as a human can get to experiencing migration's visceral reality.

**The Space-Based Future**

Martin recently placed a data collection box in the International Space Station. When activated, this box will receive signals from all tagged animals across all species simultaneously—birds, fish, mammals, insects—creating a real-time, species-spanning visualization of Earth's entire migration ballet. For millions of years, this "ballet has been part of human existence," but we may now witness either "a very sad show" as migrations collapse, or a celebration of animals like Hansi who innovate their way into survival.

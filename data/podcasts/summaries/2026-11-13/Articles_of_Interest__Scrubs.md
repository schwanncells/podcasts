# Articles of Interest — "Scrubs"

**Host:** Avery Trufelman. **Reporter:** Jasper Davidoff (reporter and games producer, Brooklyn).

## TL;DR

- **Fashion brands like Figs transformed scrubs into consumer products** — scrubs now appear in subway ads on billboards with chiseled models, sold as $80 fashion items alongside thong bodysuits, jumpsuits, and branded New Balance sneakers, reversing decades of institutional uniformity.
- **Two different scrub systems exist with opposite trajectories** — institutional OR scrubs (provided by hospitals, washed at 160°F minimum in medical-grade laundry facilities, never leave premises) remain shapeless and intentionally unfashionable, while personal scrubs (bought individually, washed at home) have become increasingly stylish and indistinguishable from regular clothing.
- **Infection risk from subway-worn scrubs is minimal for most roles** — Hannah Newman, Senior Director of Infection Prevention at Northwell Manhattan, notes that pathogens transmitted via clothing require an open wound to penetrate skin; normal daily exposure poses low risk except in sterile OR settings, where hospitals enforce institutional scrubs exclusively.
- **Scrubs evolved from medieval hazmat suits through 19th-century blood-stained frock coats to 1914 surgical innovation** — surgeon Harry Sherman introduced pastel green in 1914 because it's complementary to blood on the color wheel and soothing to eyes under bright OR lighting, establishing the modern surgical scrub.
- **Nurses pioneered personal scrubs as a symbol of professional authority and independence** — starting in the 1960s–70s, nurses adopted surgical scrubs worn in ORs and ICUs to signal competence and status; doctors adopted personal scrubs much more recently, especially post-COVID.
- **The proliferation of scrub styles has obscured traditional role signaling** — female doctors in scrubs are now routinely misidentified as nurses by patients; scrub fit (tight = nurse, loose = doctor), patterns (festive = nurse), and accessory placement (stethoscope in pocket = nurse) have become the primary way to distinguish roles within hospitals, but these codes vary by institution.
- **Scrubs have become workwear rather than special uniform** — the retail scrub industry's diversity and customization (including handmade options respecting religious modesty) demonstrates that scrubs are functionally just clothes, yet they retain symbolic power: wearing scrubs signals professional initiation and commands a modicum of respect in public spaces.

**The Rise of Fashionable Scrubs and the Figs Marketing Phenomenon**

Jasper Davidoff became curious about scrubs after noticing pervasive advertising from Figs, a company selling fashionable medical wear. Figs branded the U.S. Winter Olympic medical team and commissioned a Figs tuxedo worn by Noah Wyle. The company's product line has expanded far beyond basic scrubs to include thong bodysuits, jumpsuits, jogger pants styled like athleisure, and co-branded New Balance sneakers. This transformation from institutional uniform to fashion good raises the question: what makes something a scrub anymore? Trufelman and Davidoff observed people wearing scrubs on the subway, prompting a social media post about Memorial Sloan Kettering's "swaggy" cancer hospital scrubs. The response was divided—some commented that wearing scrubs in public is "disgusting," suggesting lingering cultural assumptions about hygiene and contamination. Yet scrubs are now openly worn as street clothing, blurring the line between professional medical wear and consumer fashion.

**Two Distinct Scrub Systems with Opposite Evolution Paths**

The episode reveals a critical distinction: institutional OR scrubs and personal scrubs operate under entirely different rules. Institutional scrubs (also called surgical or OR scrubs) are provided by hospitals, stored in vending machines accessible by badge, and never leave the premises. At the end of each day, staff change out of them and return them to the dispenser. These scrubs are laundered by contracted medical laundry services at a minimum temperature of 160°F—so hot it consumes 10–75% of a hospital's hot water and 10–15% of total energy use. The heat and repeated washing warp and degrade the fabric over years of use. They fit poorly, often too loose or too tight, with minimal elastic and unreliable drawstrings. Lauren Ferris Lee, a pediatric behaviorist, and Lindsay Johnson, a technical designer at Figs, describe institutional scrubs as "not cute" and "unflattering." Personal scrubs, by contrast, are purchased by individual healthcare workers (for $15–$80+), washed at home in regular laundry, and increasingly feature fashionable designs, pockets optimized for specific tools (pen pockets, scissor pockets, iPad pockets), and fits that are "so tight they look like joggers" or are styled as jumpsuits. The divergence is stark: as personal scrubs have become more fashionable and indistinguishable from street wear, institutional scrubs remain deliberately unfashionable and functionally isolated from public view.

**Infection Risk: Why Wearing Scrubs on the Subway Is Mostly Safe**

Hannah Newman, Senior Director of Infection Prevention at Northwell Manhattan (overseeing Lenox Hill Hospital, Meath, and Northwell Greenwich Village), addresses the concern head-on: wearing scrubs on the subway poses minimal infection risk for most healthcare roles. She explains that while clothes can be fomites (surfaces that harbor pathogens), the main transmission barrier is skin itself. For pathogens on fabric to infect someone, they must penetrate intact skin—which typically requires an open wound, active bleeding, or vomit directly on exposed tissue. For nurses and other staff working outside sterile operating room environments (dentists, veterinarians, most ward nurses), the risk is genuinely low. "Normal interactions day to day, if you're not going in an OR setting, if there's not going to be blood, it's really less of a risk," Newman notes. However, in operating rooms and other sterile, sensitive areas (ICUs, surgical suites), the rules are different. There, hospitals enforce institutional scrubs exclusively—never personal scrubs—and rigorous protocols ensure surgical-grade cleanliness.

**The Medieval Origins of Scrubs and the 1914 Color Revolution**

Scrubs did not always exist as a medical uniform. In medieval times, plague doctors wore elaborate hazmat-like getups: a long gown sealed with wax, gloves, boots, and the iconic beaked mask designed to create a safe cone of air around the nose and mouth. Ironically, this medieval costume was likely safer than what came 400 years later. By the 1700s and 1800s, when germ theory was unknown, doctors dressed like gentlemen—wearing black frock coats that concealed bloodstains. Alison Matthews David, an art and fashion historian at Toronto Metropolitan University, notes that in paintings like Thomas Eakins' *The Gross Clinic of Surgery* (1870s), surgeons wear dark formal wear. Being a doctor was barely differentiated from being a barber, butcher, or blacksmith; the main task was appearing professional enough to seem credible. Hospitals were filthy, patients wore their own (often unwashed) clothes, and surgeons wore clothes for decades without washing them—covered in blood was normal and even a badge of honor for a doctor's "hands-on" work. Four out of five deaths in 19th-century surgery departments resulted from infection. Everything changed when germ theory emerged around 1860–1910. Sanitation became imperative: surgeons washed hands between operations, sterilized instruments, and sealed clothes with beeswax (reviving a plague-doctor technique). Medicine needed a rebrand—it had to look modern, sleek, and clean. By the 1900s–1910s, everyone wore white, which signaled purity and modernity (and also didn't show dirt). But in the 1920s–30s, operating rooms became brightly electrified, and the glare of an all-white environment began to strain surgeons' eyes. In 1914, San Francisco surgeon Harry Sherman proposed a solution: pastel green scrubs. Green sits opposite red on the color wheel, providing visual balance to blood, and the calming tone reduces eye strain under bright OR lights. This innovation established the modern surgical scrub.

**Nurses Pioneer Personal Scrubs as a Status Symbol**

The path to personal scrubs is distinctly tied to nursing professionalization and autonomy. Before scrubs, nurses wore long black dresses with white bibs, aprons, and pleated caps—an outfit that was half nun, half maid, designed by Florence Nightingale in the early 1800s to professionalize and standardize the nursing workforce. Nursing schools (hospital-run vocational programs) rigidly enforced these uniforms for over a century. The outfit evolved with fashion (puff sleeves in the 1890s, shorter hemlines in the 1940s), but the core dress-apron-cap ensemble remained until the 1960s–70s. Maureen, a nurse who graduated from the Philadelphia General Hospital School of Nursing in 1969, recalls mandatory white stockings, white shoes, and inspections so strict that an instructor warned, "A nurse with dirty shoelaces probably has dirty underwear." Nurses in operating rooms and intensive care units, however, wore surgical scrubs—mark of prestige and clout, signaling advanced skills and authority. By the 1960s–70s, as gender liberation movements challenged dress codes and nurses gained more professional autonomy, other nurses began demanding the right to wear scrubs too. Christina Bates, a retired history curator from the Canadian Museum of History and author of *The Nurses' Uniform Cultural History*, explains: "Nurses really pioneer this idea of personal scrubs." They saw scrubs as a symbol of independence and professional fullness of self. Manufacturers responded by creating nurse uniforms sold directly to workers rather than controlled by hospitals. Nurses essentially liberated scrub-wearing from institutional gatekeeping—a shift that doctors would not adopt until much more recently.

**The Recent Doctor Adoption of Scrubs and the Post-COVID Acceleration**

Doctors' adoption of personal scrubs is shockingly recent. Luckett Lean, an emergency room and pediatric emergency physician in Ontario, recalls that in medical school during the 2010s, the rule for non-surgical rotations was formal: business attire and a white coat were required. By the time she began residency, doctors were starting to wear scrubs more often. Then came COVID, and "we began to be wearing scrubs all the time." This shift mirrors a broader workplace casualization (the "casual revolution"), where comfort and functionality have begun to outweigh formal hierarchy. Lean notes that research shows patients actually prefer physicians in scrubs over formal attire—scrubs reduce the power dynamic and don't reinforce expertise barriers as starkly as a tie. However, there's a significant downside for female doctors: they are routinely misidentified as nurses by patients. Lean, who wears a badge clearly reading "Dr. Lean" and has embroidery on her chest, has experienced entire examinations where patients still ask, "When do I get to see the doctor?" Her wife, who is Asian, is sometimes assumed to be housekeeping. Male doctors report no such confusion. This dynamic reveals that scrubs, while more comfortable and patient-friendly, simultaneously expose and amplify existing biases about gender and race in medicine.

**The Scrub Code: How Hospitals Now Signal Professional Hierarchy Through Clothing Details**

As scrubs became universal wear across all roles, the traditional visual distinction between doctors and nurses erased. Hospitals developed an intricate sartorial code to restore hierarchy. Luckett Lean has observed consistent patterns across institutions: festive patterns or colors signal nurse status, while plain or muted scrubs suggest doctor. Fit is crucial—tight scrubs read as nurse, loose as doctor. Stethoscope placement matters: nurses carry them in pockets; doctors wear them around the neck or in hip holsters. These codes are so specific and culturally variable that they work primarily within a given hospital's community. Yet the codes are not universal—religious and cultural modesty also shapes scrub choices. Asa Tu, a patient technician and Muslim woman, found standard hospital scrubs ill-fitting for her needs. She solved the problem herself: "These are literally just clothes." She buys fabric in bulk from West Africa, gives it to a tailor, and has them made longer and with longer sleeves. Her observation—"Yeah, no one really like notices or cares. So as long as the color is right, that they don't really care what you're wearing"—highlights how functional and flexible scrub-wearing has become.

**Scrubs as Workwear: Functional, Symbolic, and Personal**

By the episode's conclusion, Trufelman and Davidoff reframe scrubs not as special medical uniforms but as workwear—a category that encompasses functional, often personalized clothing worn in professional contexts. The retail scrub industry's diversity (multiple brands, styles, price points, customization options, handmade alternatives) demonstrates that scrubs have lost their monopoly on "special" status. Yet they retain cultural power. Wearing scrubs signals professional initiation; it affords "a modicum of respect" in public. On the subway, scrubs communicate something about identity: they may indicate someone coming from or going to a medical role, but the specificity of the scrub chosen—a $15 bargain-bin v-neck versus an $80 Figs fashion item with precision pockets—now reveals more about individual choices and identity than a standardized uniform ever could. In this way, the proliferation and diversification of scrubs, while obscuring the old binary of doctor versus nurse, opens space for healthcare workers to express who they are as people—their professionalism, their style, their values—while still inhabiting a shared uniform category that commands public recognition and respect.

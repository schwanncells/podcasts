# The Sporkful — "Is Sandwich Sexism Real? (Reheat)"

**Host:** Dan Pashman. **Guest:** Lauren Hale (associate professor, Stony Brook University; runs quantitative research studies).

## TL;DR

- **Sandwich sexism hypothesis:** A listener named Bill theorized that sandwich makers give smaller portions to women than to men for the same price, and asked Dan to investigate.
- **Four out of five sandwiches measured larger for women:** Dan and his friend Laura Anderson (Grist editor) ordered identical sandwiches at five New York shops (bodega, Italian deli, Mexican torta shop, Vietnamese banh mi, Jewish deli) and weighed them. Results: female turkey sandwich 10.9 oz vs. male 10.6 oz; female mozzarella 1 lb 9.1 oz vs. male 1 lb 6.2 oz; female pastrami 13.7 oz vs. male 11.7 oz. Only the torta showed the reverse (male 12 oz, female 10.8 oz).
- **Sandwich makers acknowledge gender-based portioning happens:** Jailavi, the woman who made the tortas, reported witnessing a male coworker make larger burritos for men, and described seeing portion differences at McDonald's, Starbucks, and in her own family growing up—"more food to my boys, the boys in my family, than the girls."
- **Small sample size limits statistical significance:** Lauren Hale confirmed that five sandwich pairs are far too few to publish findings; she estimated 30–40 pairs would be needed for meaningful statistical power. Inside Edition later ran a similar test at 17 locations and also found no evidence of gender-based sandwich sizing.
- **Unconscious bias may be at play:** While explicit discrimination is unlikely, sandwich makers acknowledged that they might unconsciously make smaller portions for women—a combination of assumptions about appetite, health consciousness, and social norms rather than deliberate policy.
- **Race and body-type bias emerged as additional factors:** Listeners reported different portions based on race and the appearance/weight of the server, suggesting portioning inconsistency is driven by multiple implicit biases rather than gender alone.

**The Investigation Framework**

Dan Pashman contacted Lauren Hale, an associate professor at Stony Brook University who runs quantitative research studies, to design the experiment scientifically. Hale explained that sample size depends on the magnitude of the suspected effect: narrower differences between male and female portions would require more sandwiches to detect statistically. Given time and budgetary constraints, Dan and his friend Laura Anderson—an editor at Grist who has written extensively about food—settled on purchasing five sandwich pairs (one ordered by each of them at the same location, same sandwich). For validity, the sandwich maker had to see the customer; pre-made or back-kitchen sandwiches were excluded. The pair planned to weigh each sandwich and interview the makers about their portioning practices.

**Bodega, Italian Deli, and Torta Shop Findings**

Their first stop was a typical New York bodega where they ordered turkey sandwiches on rolls; the counter worker was too busy to be interviewed. At Fico's, an old-school Italian place, Dan ordered a fresh mozzarella sandwich with broccoli rabe and artichokes. Counter worker Hoover Gutierrez insisted he maintains consistency and focuses on the sandwich, not the customer. He acknowledged that after nine years of making sandwiches, he eyeballs portions and occasionally adds extra for friendly customers, but tries to keep portions uniform. When asked if sandwich makers might unconsciously make smaller portions for women, Gutierrez replied, "I think that's definitely a possibility. And if I think someone was going to make a lighter sandwich, it would probably be for a female, just for their safety or for their better interest."

At Choza, a Mexican restaurant, Jailavi made their carnitas tortas. She emphasized consistency and argued with coworkers about maintaining equal portions. However, she also disclosed witnessing a male delivery driver make intentionally larger burritos for men and reported seeing portion disparities at McDonald's (fries), Starbucks (milk), and even in her own childhood household where "they gave more food to my boys, the boys in my family, than the girls."

**Banh Mi Skip and Vietnamese Coffee**

The banh mi shop was excluded because sandwiches were made in the back kitchen, meaning the cook would not know the customer's gender. Dan and Laura refueled with Vietnamese iced coffee instead.

**Jewish Deli and the Scale Test**

Dan and Laura brought all five sandwiches to the New York Public Radio mailroom, where Alethea John operated the scale. The results surprised both investigators:

- Turkey sandwiches (bodega): Male 10.6 oz, female 10.9 oz
- Fresh mozzarella (Fico's): Male 1 lb 6.2 oz, female 1 lb 9.1 oz
- Carnitas tortas (Choza): Male 12 oz, female 10.8 oz
- Pastrami on rye (2nd Avenue Deli): Male 11.7 oz, female 13.7 oz

Three of four sandwiches were larger for the female customer. Jeremy Liebowahl, the owner of 2nd Avenue Deli, had dismissed the sandwich sexism theory beforehand, noting that expensive ingredients like pastrami make portion consistency crucial for profitability.

**Interpretation and Statistical Reality**

Dan and Laura were surprised by the results, which contradicted the original hypothesis. However, Lauren Hale cautioned that five pairs lack statistical power; she proposed that listeners submit additional data—30 to 40 pairs from the same shop, same sandwich, same time of day, made by the same person—so she could rerun the analysis. After the episode aired, Inside Edition conducted a larger investigation at 17 locations and Chipotle burrito shops but also found no evidence of systematic gender-based portioning. Both investigations suggest that while sandwich makers may hold unconscious biases, the effect is neither consistent nor large enough to detect across multiple locations.

**Unconscious Bias and Broader Patterns**

Jailavi's comments were the most illuminating: she had witnessed portioning discrimination across multiple food venues and acknowledged that her own coworkers had acted on gender assumptions. Her experience suggests that unconscious bias—assumptions about appetite, health consciousness, and female customers' preferences for lighter meals—may drive occasional discrepancies, even in the absence of explicit policy. One listener, Melissa, reported that as a 29-year-old African American woman, she perceived receiving smaller portions than Caucasian customers and worried about being labeled "greedy" for requesting more food. Another listener noted that server size and appearance correlated with portion generosity, suggesting that multiple identity factors (race, gender, body size of the server) interact in complex ways to shape portioning decisions.

**Sandwich Making as Art Over Science**

Dan reflected that sandwich making, like bartending, is an art form where consistency is less important than craft. While he personally prefers that his sandwich feel uniquely made for him—rather than precisely measured—he emphasized not shortchanging female customers. The episode concluded that no one is as consistent as they think they are, and portioning disparities likely reflect unconscious biases and individual variation rather than deliberate discrimination.

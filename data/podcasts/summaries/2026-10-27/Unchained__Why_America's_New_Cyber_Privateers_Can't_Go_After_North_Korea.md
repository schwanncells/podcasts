# Unchained — "Why America's New Cyber Privateers Can't Go After North Korea"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guests:** Chris Giancarlo (former chairman of the CFTC; author of *The New Adventures of Crypto Dad*; co-proposed crypto privateering framework); Ari Redboard (global head of policy, TRM Labs; former federal prosecutor; former senior advisor, U.S. Treasury Department).

## TL;DR

- **North Korea has stolen $6+ billion from crypto in recent years; in 2025 alone, TRM documented 7,500 ransomware attacks**, but AI-enabled autonomous attacks in 2026 could scale to 75,000+ with zero human involvement required.
- **Letters of Mark and Reprisal are in the U.S. Constitution (Section 8) and were used historically to authorize privateers** — Giancarlo and Perkins proposed reviving them in a Cointelegraph op-ed; the White House issued an executive order in August 2026 authorizing government contractors to conduct offensive cyber operations.
- **The August order explicitly prohibits private contractors from targeting state-sponsored actors** — North Korea, Iran, and China remain off-limits; contractors can only attack transnational criminal organizations, cartels, ransomware gangs, and pig butchering networks.
- **Pig butchering networks operate scam compounds in Laos, Myanmar, Thailand, and Cambodia, stealing hundreds of billions annually** from individuals and small businesses—far exceeding North Korea's crypto theft in scale.
- **North Korea deploys deepfake videos in real-time hiring interviews** to infiltrate U.S. companies; the Drift Protocol hack in 2026 involved six months of social engineering and in-person conferences with fake actors.
- **State-sponsored actors require military/DOD response, not private contractors** — escalation risk and international law require elected officials and career diplomats to make targeting decisions, not the private sector.
- **Defense against North Korea focuses on sanctions, diplomatic pressure on China (the regime's primary trade partner), AI-enhanced cybersecurity for crypto platforms, and disrupting money laundering networks** — not offensive cyber by privateers.

**The Extraordinary Cyber Moment and AI Acceleration**

Redboard characterizes 2026 as "an extraordinary moment" in human history: bad actors are supercharging their operations with technology at an unprecedented scale. Over the past decade, North Korea has stolen billions from the crypto ecosystem, but this moment is different due to artificial intelligence. In 2025, TRM documented 7,500 ransomware attacks, all requiring human involvement to execute. In 2026, the threat landscape has shifted to end-to-end autonomous ransomware—attacks that run without human-in-the-loop oversight, executing at machine speed. This means the 7,500 attacks could become 75,000 or even 7 million attacks.

The danger is acute because hospital systems are the largest target for ransomware and cyber attacks, followed by water filtration and electrical grids. Redboard frames this as a "civilization-level" threat, not merely a law enforcement or national security issue. When North Korea steals billions from crypto, it is funding weapons proliferation and destabilizing activity. The challenge is that defending reactively is no longer sufficient; the United States and the private sector must "fight back just as fast and at the same scale."

**Letters of Mark and the Constitutional Framework**

Giancarlo explains that Letters of Mark and Reprisal are rooted in the U.S. Constitution, specifically Section 8, which grants Congress the power to issue such authorizations. Historically, nation-states issued letters to private actors to respond to attacks on shipping and commerce—existential threats at the time. Privateers like John Paul Jones during the American Revolution and others during the War of 1812 received government contracts to attack enemy ships. In a Cointelegraph op-ed with Chris Perkins of Franklin Templeton Crypto, Giancarlo and Perkins proposed modernizing this Constitutional power for the 21st century: unleashing the "awesome innovation capability" of the private sector to hack back against state-sponsored hackers like the Lazarus Group. For years, the U.S. has been on defense; Giancarlo and Perkins argued it was time to go on offense.

Following the op-ed's publication, Giancarlo attended a White House crypto summit in March 2025, where he presented the privateering idea to President Trump and his cabinet, including Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick. The proposal gained traction; Giancarlo and Perkins subsequently presented to the House Agricultural Committee and members of the U.S. Senate. The White House issued an executive order in March 2025 ordering federal agencies to prepare defenses and consider offensive action. More significantly, in August 2026, the White House issued a presidential memorandum formally authorizing government contracting agencies to enable the private sector to conduct offensive cyber operations against specified targets.

**How the August Order Differs from the Original Proposal**

Giancarlo acknowledges that the White House's approach is "directionally" aligned with his and Perkins's vision but differs in important ways. The original proposal sought to utilize the Constitution's Letters of Mark and Reprisal power; the White House instead invoked the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act as legal authority, sidestepping constitutional avenues. The order also focuses on government contractors rather than a broader gambit of privateers, making it narrower in scope. The presidential memorandum set an October 11, 2026 deadline for federal agencies (Department of Justice, others) to publish detailed rules, though as of the episode's recording date, those rules may not yet be public. Giancarlo characterizes this as "a first step" and "a good step" that sets the right direction, and expects further action as recent hacking incidents accelerate policy attention.

**The Critical Restriction: No State-Sponsored Targets**

One of the most important constraints embedded in the August order is that private contractors **cannot** target state-sponsored actors or rogue nation-states. They can only pursue black-hatted private criminal actors. This distinction is crucial and counterintuitive to many observers. Giancarlo explains the reasoning: privateers in history have attacked private bad actors, but going after nation-states or committing acts of war is "a distinct government action" and a matter of international law. The White House sought to avoid "backing ourselves into a war inadvertently" by imposing strict limits on what contractors can target.

Redboard elaborates: the March 2025 executive order addressed transnational criminal organizations stealing billions from Americans; the August order operationalizes that by allowing private-sector offensive cyber against criminal enterprises. But when the state itself is the criminal actor—Iran, North Korea, China—that becomes a different category requiring different channels and government authorization. The practical implication: privateers can target pig butchering scam networks, cartels, and ransomware gangs, but not Lazarus Group or other DPRK-backed units. Redboard notes this is likely temporary; as Lazarus Group continues attacking at unprecedented scale and speed, the policy may expand, but the current iteration is deliberately incremental.

Giancarlo notes that the controls written into the memorandum are substantial: each authorization must be signed off by the Department of Justice and Treasury Department; there are limits on what contractors can target; and contractors cannot engage in the historical privateering model of attacking any ship with booty. The controls are "quite a bit," and Giancarlo terms the White House approach "in the right direction, but incremental." He views this as prudent, allowing the government to observe how the first wave of operations performs before expansion.

**Targets and Attack Methods**

The types of criminal organizations privateers can target include pig butchering networks, drug cartels, and ransomware gangs. Redboard uses pig butchering as a concrete example: these networks operate through scam compounds in Laos, Myanmar, Thailand, Cambodia, and beyond, where human trafficking victims are forced to scam people 24 hours a day via text, cell tower infrastructure, and Starlink. Their "entire infrastructure is digital," making them vulnerable to infrastructure takedown, facility disruption, and fund recovery. Redbook references the PRINCE Group, a transnational criminal organization with connections to the Cambodian government and even Chinese intelligence services; through sanctions, criminal prosecution, and forfeiture actions, the U.S. recovered over 10 billion dollars—the largest forfeiture in world history.

Giancarlo describes the core of privateering as "authorizing the dirty trick squad to go after the people that are using dirty tricks on us," citing historical precedent from military cyber operations against Iran. Government agencies have seized dark net markets, operated them covertly for months to observe criminal flows, and then shut them down. The most famous example is Hansa Market, where law enforcement (FBI/Europol) continued operating the platform post-seizure. These strategies extend to targeting infrastructure—servers, networks—rather than individual cases, since the volume and speed of attacks make case-by-case prosecution impossible.

Redbook emphasizes that offensive cyber is one tool among many: "we have so many authorities at our disposal, and we need to be using all of them." Sanctions, the Patriot Act (which allows the U.S. to subpoena foreign banks and threaten them with correspondent banking cutoffs—a "death penalty" for global banks), and money-laundering disruption are equally vital. When Redbook's team served a Patriot Act subpoena on a Chinese bank involved in North Korea money laundering, the threat of cutting off correspondent banking forced compliance. These coordinated approaches—targeting the bad actors, their infrastructure, and their financial networks—are more effective than cyber alone.

**North Korea as an Exceptional Case**

A fundamental question arises: if privateers can help the U.S. fight back, why can't they go after North Korea? The answer lies in international law, statescraft, and escalation risk. North Korea is not a traditional criminal actor; it is a state whose government itself engages in theft and weapons proliferation to fund its regime. As Giancarlo phrases it: "who pulls the trigger"—in the case of state-sponsored actors, the government (military, DOD) must decide, not the private sector. Giancarlo emphasizes that this is "good civics": democracies have long delegated state-to-state conflicts to "proper military and officials" rather than private actors. The U.S. is "not disarming ourselves" by restricting privateers; it is "just deciding in the case of a state actor, you know, who gets to throw the punch." The federal government retains all capabilities to go after North Korea through national security agencies and DOD.

Redboard adds crucial context: while privateering is off the table for North Korea, the government should absolutely be using "every offensive capability" available. However, the path to disrupting North Korea requires a different toolkit: targeting its money-laundering networks (many of which involve Chinese organized crime), applying diplomatic pressure to China as North Korea's primary trade partner, maintaining sanctions, and hardening crypto infrastructure. Redboard traces the issue back to North Korea's role in historical money laundering (counterfeit $100 bills, cigarettes) and notes that crypto is simply the latest target because of the scale of theft possible.

On the specific question of whether the U.S. secretly deployed cyber operations against North Korea's internet—a DDoS attack occurred days before the episode was recorded—both Giancarlo and Redbook smiled but declined to speculate, though Giancarlo said he was "pretty happy to see that happen," implying approval of an unstated actor.

**North Korea's Evolving Attack Methods and U.S. Responses**

North Korea has pioneered a particularly sophisticated infiltration tactic: hiring its own skilled hackers as "IT workers" for foreign companies, including U.S. firms. In July 2026, government agencies across multiple countries warned that North Korea was deploying real-time deepfake videos in live hiring interviews. Candidates appear perfect on camera, present well, get hired remotely, receive laptop access, and immediately exfiltrate data. Some American employers have responded by requiring new hires to work at headquarters for at least a week for training and in-person verification—a practical counter-measure against deepfake infiltration.

The Drift Protocol hack of 2026 exemplifies North Korea's sophisticated social engineering: the attackers conducted a multi-month operation involving in-person meetings at conferences with fake actors posing as employees or business partners. This six-month campaign shows that state-sponsored actors are not simply using technology; they are investing in patient, human-centered deception that targets trust networks.

Redbook advocates for deploying AI as a counter-tool: every compliance team and government agency needs to use AI to identify deepfakes. He cites UC Berkeley professor Hani Farid's company, Get Real, which specializes in deepfake detection. The escalating arms race—bad actors augment capabilities with AI; defenders must do the same—requires that organizations "use AI to harden your systems against these types of attacks."

**The Broader Strategy Against North Korea**

Beyond cyber offense (which remains government-only), the strategy for countering North Korea's crypto theft involves multiple levers. Diplomatic pressure on China, North Korea's primary trade partner, is critical; the U.S. can incentivize or coerce China to crack down on North Korean money laundering through its financial system and halt purchases of North Korean coal and other exports. Continued and potentially escalated sanctions against Chinese banks that facilitate North Korean money laundering are another tool.

Within the crypto ecosystem itself, hardening cybersecurity practices is essential. Redbook noted that the DeFi Education Fund is working on best practices for crypto developers, aiming to raise the baseline of security across the space. Shin observed a "growth" in the development of security standards among crypto builders, which is "heartening." These defensive measures, combined with improved detection of deepfakes in hiring and enhanced monitoring of threat intelligence, create a broader deterrent.

Redbook frames the ultimate constraint: "We're never going to arrest anyone in North Korea or China or Iran or Cambodia." This reality forces policymakers to think in terms of infrastructure disruption (via offensive cyber, government-operated), financial strangulation (sanctions, money-laundering disruption), and alliance pressure (leveraging China), rather than relying on traditional law enforcement.

**Escalation Risk and the Limits of Privateering**

Laura Shin posed a crucial closing question: could privateering escalate tensions and make the situation worse? Giancarlo acknowledged this was "always the flaw, perhaps" in the original privateering proposal. Once the private sector is unleashed, can they be trusted to make "the judgments as to statecraft" that have historically been the province of elected representatives and "people of discretion and discernment"? These are life-and-death decisions for citizens. Giancarlo expressed relief that the presidential memorandum is "very prudent" in requiring DOJ and government oversight of authorization decisions, preventing the private sector from unilaterally deciding escalation.

Redbook concurred: the question of escalation vs. de-escalation must be decided by the Department of Justice and the federal government under the NSPM, not by individual contractors. The government makes the call; the private sector deploys approved activities. Both speakers agreed that statescraft—knowing when to escalate and when to show restraint—is too consequential to delegate to private corporations, no matter how capable.

**Infrastructure Hardening and AI Defense**

Beyond offensive cyber and diplomatic pressure, the episode emphasizes defensive hardening. Chris Giancarlo and Ari Redboard both stressed that AI must be deployed to identify vulnerabilities in critical infrastructure: smart contracts, operating systems, water filtration facilities, electrical grids, and hospital systems. The attacks of the previous weeks—PHP bugs in WordPress, Railway platform secrets, weak encryption keys from the Emudra certificate authority—exposed how deeply embedded vulnerabilities run in foundational internet infrastructure. While some infrastructure is protected by redundancy (the U.S. electrical grid has many non-networked isolated grids, and older nuclear plants are standalone), the trend toward networked systems creates new risks.

Giancarlo noted that testing of power grid facilities by adversaries has been ongoing for over a decade, but the AI era has supercharged the threat. He acknowledged that steps have been taken to harden infrastructure, but the pace of innovation in defense must match the pace of innovation in offense. Redboard added that the private sector's innovation in cybersecurity—combined with AI-powered vulnerability scanning—is essential. The "cops and robbers" dynamic will never end, but the U.S. goal is to ensure it is never "three or four steps" behind, only one step.

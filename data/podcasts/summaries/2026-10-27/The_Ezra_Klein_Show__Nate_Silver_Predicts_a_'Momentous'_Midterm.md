# The Ezra Klein Show — "Nate Silver Predicts a 'Momentous' Midterm"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Nate Silver (election forecaster, founder of FiveThirtyEight, editor of Silver Bulletin on Substack, co-host of Still Counting podcast, author of *The Art of Risking Everything*).

## TL;DR

- **Democrats favored to win Senate with 70+ percent probability** — a historic shift from a year ago when it seemed mathematically impossible. Democrats need to flip four seats in states Trump won by double digits, and polling shows them competitive or ahead in Kansas, North Carolina, Ohio, and Texas.
- **An 11-point midterm swing toward Democrats vs. the 2024 presidential result** — compared to the 7.5-point average midterm swing against the president's party, this is substantial enough to flip states like Ohio (Trump +11 in 2024) into competitive territory.

- **Democrats overperforming in red heartland states but underperforming in blue states** — this pattern is driven by gas/diesel prices hitting rural agricultural and trucking economies, possible "last in first out" reversal of Trump's 2024 coalition gains, and stronger Democratic governance in red states than in blue ones facing anti-incumbent backlash.

Pollsters are aware of and correcting for past misses, and early voting data shows low-propensity voters trending Democratic.
- **House Republicans unlikely to face 1994-style wipeout** — due to redistricting and fewer swing districts, even a Democratic wave would yield only 20-40 seat gains, not 60. A 90+ percent Democratic House win probability translates to only 22 seat gains on average.

If Democrats win significant Senate seats, the narrative will center on "momentum era" candidates and generational turnover, not ideological victory.

**The Historic Senate Flip Possibility**

A year ago, the idea that Democrats could win the Senate seemed nearly impossible. They would have needed to flip seats in states Trump won by double digits—Kansas, Ohio, Texas—alongside defending their own competitive seats and flipping purple states like Maine and North Carolina. But with a week to go, Democrats sit at a 70+ percent probability of Senate control in Silver's model, and some paths lead beyond a simple majority.

The math hinges on an 11-point midterm swing against Trump's party, larger than the 7.5-point historical average. Trump won Ohio by 11 points in 2024; an 11-point swing puts it at a toss-up. Similarly, in Texas (Trump +13), an 11-point Democratic swing yields a R+2 environment where candidates like Tallarico can potentially win. The shift is not random—it reflects both structural factors and the quality of candidate recruitment.

**Candidate Quality and Recruitment Strategies**

Democrats have followed a strategy of nominating strong, often moderate candidates in red states while taking more risk in purple territory. In Texas, Tallarico can leverage Ken Paxton's "many sins." Roy Cooper, a strong recruit from Schumer's team in North Carolina, carries executive credibility. In Iowa and Alaska, candidates like Sherrod Brown (a longtime populist) and Mary Peltola are outperforming expectations. Conversely, in bluer states, Democrats have sometimes nominated more progressive candidates—Abdul El-Sayed in Michigan, Troy Jackson in Maine—who face tougher partisan headwinds. Silver estimates candidate quality can swing outcomes by 5 points at most, but in an 11-point overall swing environment, that's often enough to flip races at the margin.

The DSCC's earlier failures, like losing the fight to make Janet Mills the Maine nominee (she eventually won the primary, but Troy Jackson emerged as the nominee anyway), masked a broader success. Over the full slate, Democratic candidate recruitment looks effective relative to the Republican field.

**Regional Overperformance and the Heartland Story**

Democrats are polling better in red heartland states—Kansas, Texas, Iowa—than in traditionally blue states like Minnesota, Michigan, or Oregon. Several factors explain this. First, gas and diesel prices hit hardest in agricultural and trucking-dependent regions of the Midwest, and Trump's Iran war spiked prices into the mid-$4s. Second, there's a possible "last in first out" dynamic: Trump's 2024 coalition gains among Hispanics and working-class whites may be partially reversing, or those voters may simply be less likely to turn out for lower-profile Republican candidates. Third, anti-incumbent sentiment is running high in blue states with Democratic trifectas—Minnesota (Tim Walz is 17 points underwater in approval), Oregon, and New York—where governance critiques have traction, whereas Republican-led red states don't carry the same baggage.

One hidden factor: The Economist reported that Trump voters living closer to data centers are more likely to flip Democratic in this election. Data centers concentrate in the Midwest for space and proximity to the grid, potentially amplifying the regional effect.

**Polling Methodology and the Seltzer Cautionary Tale**

When discussing polling resilience, Silver navigates the tension between methodological sophistication and episodic catastrophic error. The Ann Seltzer Iowa poll in 2024—from the highest-rated pollster, tied with the New York Times—showed Harris winning by 3 points when every other poll had Trump +9. The poll was off by 13-15 points. This opened speculation that everyone was "overcompensating" for past Trump underestimation.

However, Silver notes that 2018 and 2022 midterms saw historically accurate polls, and the problem may not be methodological but epistemic. Every pollster now knows they were wrong about Trump in 2016, 2020, and 2024. They employ various techniques to account for low-propensity voters and turnout modulation. Some ask directly about voting intention (which yields Democratic-skewed samples); others model turnout based on prior voting history (which produces more modest Democratic leads).

A key advantage for Democrats this cycle: early voting data shows low-propensity voters leaning Democratic. If Republicans make up that gap by election day, we're in line with polls. If they don't, Democrats could see a favorable polling error—the inverse of 2024.

**House Dynamics and Redistricting Constraints**

While the Senate gets narrative attention, the House outcome matters enormously for leverage and legislative capacity. Democrats have a 90+ percent probability of winning the House, but this translates to only 22 seat gains on average because of redistricting and voter self-sorting. The number of swing districts has collapsed. Republicans gained net seats from redistricting in Louisiana, Alabama, and Tennessee by eliminating majority-minority districts (roughly 3 net seats), while in Texas and Florida—where they drew optimistic maps—they face Democratic gains because Hispanic voter realignment (most aggressive demographic shift toward Democrats) undermines their assumptions.

If a wave emerges, it would overwhelm unprepared Republican candidates, but the structural reality means a 60-seat swing like 2010 is off the table. Democrats winning by 3 points could yield 40 seats; a 5-point wave might produce comparable gains to 2018's 41 seats.

**Trump's Declining Approval and the Gas Price–Governance Nexus**

Trump's approval has fallen steadily since taking office in January 2025. Statistically, the decline correlates strongly with gas prices. The Iran war drove gasoline and diesel into the mid-$4s, hitting agricultural, trucking, and heating-dependent regions. But beyond immediate price pain, the economic narrative has shifted: the stock market remains solid (Trump's key metric), yet voters now question whether good stock indices outweigh high interest rates, anemic wage growth, and growing debt.

Critically, Trump's first term benefited from a "halo effect"—when the economy felt good and unemployment was low, voters tolerated or ignored concerns about democracy and culture war. That license has expired. Additionally, Trump fatigue is real: he has been the dominant figure in American politics since mid-2015, and at 80 years old, he shows signs of wear.

**Trump, AI, and the Political Incentive Puzzle**

Silver identifies two rationales for Trump's pro-data-center stance despite it being an 80-20 issue against data centers (60% want AI to slow down). One is substantive: Trump genuinely believes the U.S. is in a military and geopolitical race with China, and "whoever wins AI wins." The other is self-preservation: the stock market is his one strong economic indicator. If the AI boom falters, that collapses, leaving him with high rates, weak growth, and no narrative.

However, this creates a puzzle about Trump's midterm strategy. He could have ended Ken Paxton's Texas primary and backed Cornyn, strengthening Republicans' Senate position. Instead, Trump has only won midterms when he himself is on the ballot. When he's not—2018, 2022, all the runoffs, ballot initiatives—Republicans lose decisively because Trump's unique ability to mobilize disengaged voters doesn't transfer. Roger Marshall and Ken Paxton don't inspire the same "crawl on glass" turnout.

**Polling's Final Test and the Margins That Matter**

Silver emphasizes the most important early-warning signal: if Democrats don't win the House, polling credibility collapses. A Democratic House win aligns with polls; a Republican hold would require a 3-4 point uniform error against Democrats across all races, a theoretically possible but implausible scenario given early-vote data and turnout incentives.

Conversely, if Democrats lose key Senate races (Ohio, Texas, Iowa), it doesn't necessarily mean polling failed—it could just mean partisanship held stronger than the 11-point swing could overcome. If Democrats only flip North Carolina and lose Maine and Michigan, that "would kind of defy everything that we think we know about how midterms work."

**The Democratic Generational Cohort and Narrative Implications**

Beyond the binary outcome, the midterm's sub-narratives will shape the Democratic Party. If progressive candidates like El-Sayed underperform moderates, that fuels a "moderation wins" story; if both factions do well, it signals a talent influx. Silver identifies an emerging "elder millennial" Democratic bench—influenced by Obama, including El-Sayed, Ossoff, Rob Sand, and James Tallarico—that is winning across ideological boundaries.

The Democratic Party's Gen X and late-boomer classes were "caught between political eras and communication eras," but this new cohort is redefining what Democratic leadership looks like. If these candidates win, the narrative is not "progressives defeated" or "moderates vindicated," but rather "generational turnover, new talent, and a Steph Curry effect where Obama's model of politics is finally bearing fruit in the next generation."

Whether an election is decided on election night also shapes narrative power; an ambiguous race like Alaska (with ranked-choice voting) won't carry the same weight as a clear call in Texas or Kansas.

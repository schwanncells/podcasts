# Code Switch — "Weaponizing IQ: Who gets to be smart?"

**Host:** B.A. Parker, Brittany Luce (crossover with It's Been a Minute). **Guests:** Quinn Slobodian (historian, author of *Hayek's Bastards: Race, Gold, IQ, and the Capitalism of the Far Right*); Angela Saini (journalist and science writer, author of *Superior: The Return of Race Science*; journalist and MIT lecturer on intelligence, race, and categorization).

## TL;DR

- **IQ talk resurgent in politics and tech** — President Trump uses "high IQ" to glorify himself and "low IQ" to denigrate rivals, while Silicon Valley figures like Curtis Yarvin propose IQ bars for voting or governance, treating intelligence as a sole metric for power and utility.
- **William Shockley, transistor inventor, proposed paying people with IQ below 100 one thousand dollars per IQ point to sterilize themselves** — Tech sector figures building on his idea suggested lowering the price to make the program more cost-effective, revealing eugenicist roots in Silicon Valley's founding DNA.
- ***The Bell Curve* (1995) by Hernstein and Murray explicitly weaponized IQ claims against the civil rights era** — The book argued that racial "biological hereditary inequality" made affirmative action and egalitarianism futile, sold hundreds of thousands of copies, and brought IQ racism back into mainstream American discourse.
- **Intelligence testing originated in the eugenics movement via Francis Galton** — Galton saw heredity as deterministic and designed IQ tests to identify and rank people on the bell curve; this entire framework fed into racial pseudoscience that persists today.
- **IQ tests are culturally biased and conflate knowledge with intelligence** — Early tests asked about Shakespeare and required specific cultural exposure; the BITCH (Black Intelligence Test of Cultural Homogeneity), designed by psychologist Dr. Robert Williams in 1972, flipped the script to expose this bias by using African-American cultural references.
- **Environment, not genetics, is the primary lever for developing intelligence** — No intelligence gene has been found; genetic potential requires supportive environments to manifest. Early IQ test score gaps between groups reflect access disparities, not innate capability differences.
- **AI and superintelligence discourse mirrors the IQ fetishism problem** — Sam Altman's claim that AI improves IQ by one standard deviation yearly, combined with influential writings (Nick Bostrom's *Superintelligence*) that framed human progress as intelligence selection, is now driving language shift from "AI" to "superintelligence."
- **Modern testing systems (SAT, admissions) remain imperfect but offer pathways for low-income and marginalized students** — While subjective, standardized tests give talented students from under-resourced environments a chance at recognition and higher education that teacher grades alone might not provide.

**IQ as a Political and Techno-Capitalist Tool**

Quinn Slobodian explains that IQ obsession connects the Trump wing of politics with the Silicon Valley ruling coalition. Trump uses "high IQ" and "low IQ" as personal epithets—glorifying himself while denigrating opponents like Maxine Waters in what Slobodian describes as obvious dog-whistling. In tech, IQ operates differently: as a ranking of human utility in a computerized economy. Curtis Yarvin, a prominent figure in tech circles, has proposed IQ bars for voting in places like South Africa, a proposal Slobodian notes translates clearly into racial restrictions on suffrage given the ideological linking of race and IQ.

**The Bell Curve and Eugenic Backlash Against Civil Rights**

Slobodian traces the resurgence of IQ racism to *The Bell Curve*, published in 1995 by Harvard psychologist Richard Hernstein and Charles Murray. The book was explicitly reactionary, arguing that civil rights demands, affirmative action, and calls for racial equality would "run up against the hard fact of biological hereditary inequality." Hernstein and Murray claimed the book proved "black-white intelligence differences." It sold hundreds of thousands of copies and 800 pages of statistically dense social science gave the argument scientific veneer. The stated goal was to convince the country to stop trying to achieve equality—what they called "living with inequality in America again."

**The Eugenics Origin of Intelligence Testing**

Angela Saini traces the history of intelligence testing back to the eugenics movement of the early 20th century. When Darwin's theory of evolution became prominent, statistician Francis Galton reasoned that if traits were hereditary, society should encourage "intelligent and beautiful" people to have more children and discourage "less intelligent" people. Measuring intelligence became central to this plan. Galton designed early IQ tests to place people on a bell curve—identifying who was at the top, bottom, and middle. Saini notes Galton was Charles Darwin's cousin and convinced himself he was brilliant because Darwin was brilliant, embedding assumptions about heredity into the very foundations of intelligence measurement. This framework directly fueled the conflation of intelligence with race.

**William Shockley and Silicon Valley's Sterilization Advocacy**

In the 1970s, William Shockley—one of the inventors of the transistor and a founding figure of Silicon Valley—pivoted hard into IQ fetishism and proposed paying people with an IQ below 100 one thousand dollars per IQ point to sterilize themselves. Slobodian notes this was shocking even at the time, but some Shockley sympathizers went further, arguing that the sterilization price should be lowered to make the program more cost-effective. These are not fringe figures—Shockley is "the closest you can come to tech royalty" and shaped the entire sector.

**The Illusion of Objectivity and the Masking of Bias**

Slobodian explains that numbers and graphs make these arguments feel scientific and therefore beyond reproach. A person might naturally feel uncomfortable around people of different races, but instead of questioning that discomfort or society's structure, they can be shown a chart and told: "No, that's because they're actually of lower intelligence. Here's the evidence." This mathematical veneer lets people avoid confronting their own biases or systemic inequality.

**Cultural Bias in Testing and the BITCH**

Saini describes how early IQ tests were loaded with culturally specific knowledge—questions about Shakespeare, references requiring particular upbringing or education. This meant immigrants and people from unfamiliar cultural backgrounds performed worse, not because they lacked intelligence, but because they lacked the cultural context. Dr. Robert Williams, a Black psychologist who also coined the term "ebonics," created the Black Intelligence Test of Cultural Homogeneity (BITCH) in 1972 to expose this bias. One sample question asks what "blood" means; the culturally savvy answer is "a brother of color," using African-American slang. The test's point was to show that knowledge is subjective and cultural, and that any single test will advantage groups whose background matches its assumptions.

**The Myth of Hereditary Determinism**

Saini emphasizes that heredity does not work as a simple one-to-one relationship: two smart parents do not necessarily have smart children. Environment is the crucial factor. She uses the metaphor of identical plants in two pots—if one gets water and the other doesn't, one grows and one doesn't, despite being genetically identical. The same applies to humans: no intelligence gene has been discovered despite decades of searching. However, genetic potential requires supportive environments to manifest. Saini stresses that the evidence overwhelmingly shows that the more support, nurturing, education, and care a child receives, the better they perform on testing and in educational terms. This is a universal rule across every country on the planet. When Silicon Valley or race-science advocates claim there's no point in investing in certain communities, they are running counter to all available evidence.

**The Fallacy of Biological Race and the Social Nature of Intelligence Gaps**

Saini clarifies that race is not a biological construct—it is social. There is no gene that exists in all members of one race that doesn't exist in another. Early IQ tests created the false conflation of race and intelligence by asking culturally loaded questions. Immigrants, people from different cultural backgrounds, and marginalized groups all did worse on these tests not because of innate differences but because they lacked the specific cultural knowledge and exposure the tests assumed. Yet because researchers compared groups, they concluded certain races were less intelligent—a circular, rigged argument.

**The Problem of Meritocratic Sorting and Its Limitations**

Saini acknowledges the tension: when countries have limited college places and selective schools, some sifting mechanism becomes necessary. The system that has emerged values certain narrow markers—problem-solving, engineering, mathematical skills—as intelligence. She reflects on her own family: her father was an engineering graduate with science smarts, her mother never went to college but had creative and practical intelligence. She never thought one was smarter than the other; they had different talents. But when institutions have to choose among millions of people, they resort to these quantified hierarchies. Saini notes that when affirmative action was struck down in colleges, there was an expectation that Black and Hispanic students would disappear from top universities, implying a belief that these students can never reach a standard. This assumption itself is dangerous, she argues, because it implies to those students that they'll never do better any other way.

**The Utility of Standardized Testing and Pathways for Marginalized Students**

Despite these problems, Saini argues there may be value in SAT testing as it currently stands. It allows talented students living in under-resourced environments or with neglectful teachers a chance to distinguish themselves and be recognized. She came from a low-income background and attributes her university attendance and trajectory to strong SAT scores; without them, she would have had to rely on potentially biased teacher assessments. Many low-income and middle-income students see standardized tests as a lifeline, not a burden. If such tests disappeared entirely, those students might lose their only mechanism to transcend their circumstances.

**Against Intelligence Hierarchies**

Saini and the hosts agree that it is not useful to think about intelligence in hierarchy at all. All measurements of intelligence are flawed and limited. Moreover, even within families, no one can rank relative intelligence across domains—one person might excel at math while another excels at emotional understanding, creativity, or practical problem-solving. Thinking about people in such reductive terms is a mistake.

**The AI Superintelligence Pivot and Continued IQ Fetishism**

Slobodian points out that the White House has decreed that artificial intelligence is no longer called "AI" but "superintelligence" (SI). This shift reflects ongoing influence from *Superintelligence*, a 2015 book by Swedish philosopher Nick Bostrom that heavily influenced Elon Musk and Sam Altman when they founded OpenAI. Bostrom's thesis held that human progress has always been determined by intelligence and that selecting for high-IQ people to reproduce more is one path; another is genetic modification. But he suggested AI might be even faster. Altman has clearly embraced this logic, claiming AI improves its IQ by one standard deviation every year. This reframes the problem: if intelligence is the crucial variable and humans grow too slowly, the solution is silicon-based superintelligence. It's a continuation of the same eugenicist logic, now applied to machines.

**Reframing Intelligence Beyond the Quantifiable**

Slobodian argues that if we get off the "IQ train" that both the right and left have ridden, we can start thinking about other forms of intelligence: emotional, physical, relational. Care work is difficult to replace with robots. If society could recenter often-devalued skills like care, emotional understanding, and community support, there might be hope in the AI moment. The core problem is not a specific test or number—it's the underlying commitment to inequality as permanent and the reduction of human value to a single metric.

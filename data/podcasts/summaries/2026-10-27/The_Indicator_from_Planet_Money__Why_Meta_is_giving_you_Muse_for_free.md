# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Why Meta is giving you Muse for free"

**Host:** Adrian Ma, Ricky Mulvey. **Guests:** Mark Zuckerberg (CEO, Meta); Rachel Troutman (photographer and actor, early Muse user); Alex Kantrowitz (host, Big Technology Podcast); Patrick Wardle (cybersecurity researcher, co-founder, Objective-C Foundation); David Singleton (VP of Engineering, Meta's Super Intelligence Labs).

## TL;DR

- **Muse is free and massively distributed:** Meta's AI agent offers 100 million tokens per week on its free tier and has surpassed 10 million downloads since launch in early September, making it one of the fastest-growing apps ever.
- **Three revenue streams baked into one:** Meta is building Muse to generate advertising (serving valuable ads near the point of purchase), referral fees (taking a cut when Muse-guided users make purchases), and subscription revenue (users paying for premium models).
- **This is existential for Meta's advertising dominance:** Advertisers may shift budgets from Instagram ads to AI agents if AI-directed purchases prove more effective, threatening Meta's core business model that has made it a big company.
- **Muse shatters the "best model wins" narrative:** Meta built Muse on standard, off-the-shelf AI models—not breakthrough technology—yet it's winning through superior product design, suggesting the AI race is shifting from competing on model capability to competing on applications.
- **Patrick Wardle found a critical Mac vulnerability allowing hackers full access:** He discovered a flaw that would have let attackers into anything a user granted Muse access to (email, finances, etc.), contradicting Meta's public claim that Muse was "designed with security and privacy in mind."
- **Users are trading away extensive data and access for convenience:** The more information users grant Muse (email, shopping history, location), the more useful and valuable it becomes as a targeting tool, creating a security and privacy tension that users often underestimate.
- **Muse Charm—a physical listening device—raises new privacy frontiers:** Meta is also releasing Muse as a keychain that listens to in-person conversations and makes recommendations, introducing a form of ambient surveillance that even enthusiastic early adopters find invasive.

**Muse and Meta's Pitch to Consumers**

Rachel Troutman is an early adopter of Muse who has already used the agent to search for jobs, shop for apartments, and even migrate her professional website to a new domain. Meta's CEO Mark Zuckerberg framed Muse at a product launch as a genie that "doesn't just answer questions. It gets things done for you"—in his example, organizing projects with his kids and ordering baking ingredients. Troutman understands that using Muse comes with trade-offs; she has concerns about energy consumption from AI inference but feels her identity is already so exposed online that granting Muse access doesn't materially worsen her digital footprint.

**Why Meta Is Giving Muse Away: The Advertising Play**

The fundamental business model behind Muse mirrors Meta's advertising strategy on Facebook. According to Foundry's data, Meta earns roughly $7 to $20 per thousand ads viewed on its platforms. Alex Kantrowitz, host of the Big Technology Podcast, explains that Muse is uniquely positioned in Meta's ecosystem because it takes users "very close to the point of purchase." An AI agent that finds the best pad Thai restaurant within 10 miles of you and then facilitates the booking represents high-value advertising real estate—advertisers are willing to pay premiums to be at the moment a consumer is about to spend money.

Beyond advertising, Kantrowitz identifies three revenue mechanisms stacked together: Meta can sell ads, claim a referral fee when Muse directs a user to make a purchase, and eventually charge subscription fees for access to superior models. The free tier is designed to hook users—it offers 100 million tokens per week, roughly equivalent to 750 novels' worth of information, with zero cost to the user.

**The Competitive Stakes: Muse as Existential Threat and Opportunity**

Kantrowitz frames Muse's success as existential for Meta because artificial intelligence will increasingly mediate user attention and purchase decisions. "Our time will be spent with AIs and our purchase intent will go to AIs," he notes. If advertisers discover that spending budget on AI companies that guide purchase intent is more effective than Instagram ads, they will shift their dollars accordingly. Muse has achieved more than 10 million downloads in roughly two months, making it one of the fastest-growing apps ever, and this matters acutely to Meta's investors, who are questioning the massive debt Meta has accumulated for data center buildout.

**From Model Dominance to Application Dominance**

A surprising twist in the AI arms race is that Muse was not built on a breakthrough, cutting-edge AI model. Instead, Kantrowitz notes, Meta "built the basic version of Muse off standard models, not the most breakthrough high-tech stuff." Yet Muse is winning in the market, suggesting a fundamental shift in how AI will be competed on: not by who can build the smartest model, but by who can build the most useful product. Kantrowitz predicts a transition "from an intelligence battle to an AI application battle" where tech companies will need to develop applications that people actually use in their daily lives, not just demonstrations of AI capability.

**Critical Security Flaws and Privacy-by-Design Failures**

Cybersecurity researcher Patrick Wardle discovered a vulnerability in Muse's Mac application early in its rollout. The flaw would have allowed a hacker to gain access to anything a user had granted Muse access to—email, finances, calendars, and other sensitive data. Wardle published the vulnerability on Twitter as a zero-day disclosure rather than reporting it privately to Meta's bug bounty program (which pays up to $300,000 for such findings). He made this choice because private disclosure would have silenced him from speaking publicly about the issue. Wardle was "definitely disappointed" that Meta released Muse with these kinds of flaws, particularly because Meta's public messaging claimed the product was "designed with security and privacy in mind." Wardle disputes this characterization, saying the moment he examined the app, he concluded "no, you didn't. That's a lie." David Singleton, VP of engineering at Meta's Super Intelligence Labs, acknowledged on X that the company quickly fixed the bug after Wardle's disclosure, which Wardle credits them for doing. However, Kantrowitz notes that security lapses reflect a broader pattern: tech companies are under intense pressure to ship AI products as quickly as possible, and insufficient diligence is done before release.

**User Trade-offs and Boundary-Setting**

Rachel Troutman, the early user featured at the episode's start, has set deliberate boundaries with Muse: she refuses to grant it access to her bank account information and has intentionally not given the agent a name or a cute mascot icon, instead using a crystal symbol to keep it at a distance as a tool rather than an artificial companion. Both Troutman and Kantrowitz emphasized the importance of carefully controlling what data and access users grant to AI agents like Muse, understanding that the more information an agent can access, the more useful it becomes—and the richer the raw material available for Meta to create targeted advertising, though Meta claims that Muse conversations will be isolated from its ad systems.

**The Muse Charm: Ambient Surveillance in Physical Form**

Meta has also unveiled Muse Charm, a physical keychain device that embodies a new frontier in AI integration: it listens to a user's in-person conversations and makes recommendations based on those spoken interactions. Adrian Ma expressed skepticism about this product, noting that it feels "a little invasive" and that bringing such a device to a social gathering would likely result in social friction—broadcasting that Meta is listening to conversations in real time. Ricky Mulvey echoed the concern, jokingly noting he would not want to be the first person in his social circle to carry a device constantly reporting on his environment.

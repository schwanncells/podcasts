# Masters of Scale — "Eric Schmidt scores US vs. China: who's winning the AI race?"

**Host:** Bob Safian. **Guest:** Eric Schmidt (CEO of Relativity Space; former CEO and executive chairman of Google; advisor to the U.S. government on AI and national security; involved in setting up China-U.S. discussions with former Secretary of State Henry Kissinger).

## TL;DR

- **AI gap narrowed from 1-2 years to 6 months** — China has achieved near-U.S. performance by developing algorithms that require one-tenth the energy, compensating for U.S. chip export restrictions that Schmidt helped implement but expected to last 10 years.
- **Frontier AI labs: 5-7 in U.S., 5-7 in China** — creates a "12-body problem"; China's centralized government can enforce coordination, while the U.S. requires voluntary agreement, making mutual slowdown unlikely.
- **U.S. leads in model performance and safety controls; China leads in cost, energy, deployment speed, and global influence** — Schmidt provided a scored comparison: U.S. won on performance, talent, safety, and military AI; China won on cost (3-5x cheaper tokens), energy (560+ gigawatts renewable production), deployment speed, and near-certain global dominance via free/cheap models in developing nations.
- **Open-weight Chinese models pose security risks** — guardrails can be removed via "obliteration" technique, exposing raw models to cyber attacks and potential terrorist misuse; Schmidt expects a cyber event within 12 months illustrating this vulnerability.
- **U.S. lacks energy and data center infrastructure** — needs 60-80 gigawatts of power by 2030-2031 (~$50 billion per gigawatt; one nuclear plant equivalent); Schmidt called for building dedicated power plants in rural America rather than waiting for interconnection queues.
- **"No-surprise rule" proposed for rational U.S.-China cooperation** — modeled on Cold War missile-launch notification protocols; mutual transparency could prevent misunderstandings while pursuing separate advances.
- **AI is the defining competition of the generation** — both nations race toward superhuman reasoning and intelligence systems; the outcome will affect children and grandchildren fundamentally and globally.

**The Narrowing Gap: How China Caught Up**

Schmidt begins by emphasizing that the cost of AI software has collapsed from very expensive to nearly free, meaning "an awful lot more" of it will be built. When Safian asks what changed most recently in the U.S.-China competition, Schmidt replies that the gap has narrowed dramatically. Two years ago, China appeared one or two years behind the U.S.; now it appears six months behind by multiple studies. Schmidt attributes this to China's algorithmic ingenuity: despite lacking access to the most powerful chips due to U.S. export controls (which Schmidt helped put in place and originally expected to hold for roughly 10 years), China has developed algorithms that deliver comparable performance using only one-tenth the energy consumption.

**The 12-Body Problem: Coordination and Competition**

Schmidt frames the strategic challenge as a "12-body problem." Roughly five to seven frontier AI labs exist in the U.S., and a similar number in China. On the Chinese side, the centralized government can theoretically order coordination. In America, achieving any slowdown would require voluntary agreement among competing companies. As Schmidt notes, "How are we going to convince the Chinese to slow down given they already think that they're behind? Not happening." This asymmetry shapes the competitive landscape fundamentally. Schmidt argues the competition is not simply about building faster systems but about ensuring that development remains safe while America maintains its lead.

**America's Structural Strengths, China's Operational Advantages**

China possesses a structural advantage in centralized strategic direction—the government can dictate AI as national policy and align resources behind it. America's advantage lies in diversity and decentralization, with world-class talent, deep funding markets (where, Schmidt observes, "$50 billion [can be] got on a text message"), and cutting-edge research. However, China has implemented industrial policy at scale. China produced 560+ gigawatts of renewable energy last year and faces no interconnection queue waits—bottlenecks that in the U.S. stretch seven years. China is building massive data centers while America lacks both the energy infrastructure and the political will to accelerate power generation. Schmidt calls for America to "build your own power systems" in rural areas, citing de Tocqueville's 250-year-old observation that "America is a great country because of the optimism of its people, the abundance of the land, and the absence of a king."

**Open vs. Closed: Strategy and Risk**

The U.S. and China pursue opposing approaches to model access. China's strategy is largely open-source and open-weights, releasing the trained parameters so others can build on them. America's strategy is closed-source and closed-weights, keeping both the code and parameters proprietary. Schmidt explains the rationale: frontier models cost between $100 million and $500 million to train, so companies naturally hesitate to release their work. However, China chose openness strategically—it was behind and needed rapid user adoption. Schmidt notes that when China invents something in open-source, "we copy it in America. Thank you, China." Examples of open-weight models include Reflection AI's 500-billion-parameter model, NVIDIA's Mnemotron, and Google's Gemma 4. The contest between these strategies is "the defining question" of the era, with each side convinced its approach will maintain dominance.

**Security Vulnerabilities in Open-Weight Models**

Schmidt identifies a critical unsolved problem: open-weight models include guardrails designed to prevent misuse, but these can be removed via a technique called "obliteration," exposing the raw underlying model. Once guardrails are stripped, these raw models become usable for cyber attacks. This concern is intensified by the precipitous drop in software cost; capabilities are expanding "so extraordinarily" that technical leaders in San Francisco and elsewhere are worried. Schmidt notes that many people hope to slow down AI development, but voluntary slowdown is "very difficult to do." China does not want these models misused against itself, so it is unlikely to support restrictions, though Schmidt acknowledges the contradiction in relying on China's self-interest for security. He predicts that "there will be some kind of a cyber event in the next 12 months that will illustrate this point."

**The Scorecard: Who's Winning?**

Schmidt provides a point-by-point assessment across eight factors:

Model performance goes to the U.S., which leads "by all the benchmarks." Labs indicate next-generation models will be "incredibly performant," and the industry remains in the scaling-law phase with "amazing things coming."

Model cost goes to China, with tokens three to five times cheaper. Schmidt attributes this forced efficiency to the absence of powerful chips, spurring Chinese labs to innovate in algorithms and supervised fine-tuning.

Energy and compute go to China. The 560+ gigawatts of renewable energy, combined with frictionless data center construction, far outpaces U.S. infrastructure.

Leadership and talent go to the U.S., though Schmidt criticizes America's immigration policies: "Taking the smartest people and arresting them and kicking them out of the country is like a bad strategy." He highlights a quantum researcher who was expelled from the U.S., only to lead China's quantum program—an adversary America now faces.

Military and defense AI goes slightly to the U.S., though the assessment is uncertain. Studies suggest Chinese models have been used by Russia, but "not in an extensive way." Should conflict erupt, China's dominance in consumer drones and rapid military deployment would be a challenge.

Safety controls clearly go to the U.S. Various labs have agreed to exchange safety practices. Schmidt observes that regulation is inevitable: "AI is so pervasive in society that there are going to be bad outcomes, and those bad outcomes will cause regulation."

Speed of deployment goes to China. While the U.S. invents frontier systems, China deploys them into everything "digital, mechanical, and so forth" at an astounding rate, with demos showing applications absent in America.

Global influence will go to China over 10 years. Schmidt explains the decisive factor: "What's the difference between a free product and an expensive product? More users for the free product." Developing nations lack the wealth to afford U.S. token costs; they will adopt Chinese models cheaply or freely, creating "huge geopolitical implications" that most geopolitical analysts have not yet grasped.

**Is This a Zero-Sum Game?**

Safian asks whether the U.S. and China can both win. Schmidt replies: "I don't think AI is a zero-sum game." Historical precedent shows competitors can create entirely new markets. He proposes a "no-surprise rule" modeled on Cold War protocols—when a nation launches a missile, it notifies others so they can verify and conduct verification training. No random launches. Similarly, Schmidt suggests rational conversation between U.S. and Chinese elites to establish mutually beneficial rules. These decisions were made "50 years ago" for missile launches; a comparable framework for AI could serve human flourishing.

**A Call to Action for Business Leaders**

Schmidt concludes by reiterating that AI is "the single most important thing that's going to happen for the rest of our lives," underpinning scientific progress that creates wealth and opportunity. He urges business leaders in the audience to actually use AI. He notes that many executives tell him they haven't started using it, yet when he encountered deep chain-of-thought reasoning and extended reasoning, he realized "it has arrived." This realization changed his thinking about his life, work, investments, and management of companies. He offers concrete examples: finance functions could be automated so the CFO focuses on interpretation rather than report generation; manufacturing, inventory, security, and cash-flow forecasting can be transformed once leaders embrace the mindset that AI can handle routine work. "That's why this is such a precious moment to be alive."

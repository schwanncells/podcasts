# Planet Money — "Where all those weird new drinks are coming from"

**Host:** Jeff Guo (with Kenny Malone as co-host). **Guests:** Tom Gibson (chief flavorist, Flavor Man — designs beverage flavor recipes for clients across the industry); Kristen Weber (CTO, Flavor Man — leads new beverage type and functional ingredient development); Dave Defoe (founder, Flavor Man — nearly 40 years in the beverage industry); Katie Clark (R&D director, Flavor Man).

## TL;DR

- **Three forces explain the beverage explosion:** advances in flavor science, the invention of new functional beverage categories, and a dramatic fall in manufacturing barriers to entry.
- **Flavor science now offers "exponentially more colors"** — scientists have isolated new aroma compounds, improved extraction techniques, and can now use bacteria to synthesize flavor chemicals without harvesting the fruit, creating entirely new fantasy flavors like dragon fruit.
- **Functional beverages are the current growth engine** — sodas marketed for gut health, sleep, or energy have expanded total beverage sales by inventing new reasons not to drink water; gum acacia (a prebiotic) is one of the most popular ingredients in brands like Olipop.
- **Manufacturing minimums have collapsed** — in the 1990s, entering the market required ~12,000 cases (~250,000+ cans minimum); today small test runs of a few thousand cans cost only a few thousand dollars total.
- **The microbrewery boom was the catalyst** — excess canning capacity at craft breweries in the 2010s opened contract manufacturing to independent drink brands at tiny-run prices.
- **Flavor Man has secretly launched tens of thousands of beverages** — including, publicly, Ocean Spray and Jones Soda; a juice box for aging cats (salmon flavored) is on the trophy wall.
- **The Planet Money house drink**: a yuzu elderberry sparkling coconut water with a jambu twist — jambu is a tropical herb traditionally used to numb the mouth for toothaches, producing a building tingly "battery-in-the-mouth" sensation.

**Why There Are So Many New Drinks Now**

Jeff Guo opens the episode at a supermarket with co-host Kenny Malone, cataloguing the bewildering variety of the beverage aisle: rainbow walls of flavored seltzers, canned lattes beside latte-flavored energy drinks, "functional sodas" promising gut health or better sleep, and limited-edition flavors like Dubai chocolate Pepsi and Cosmopolitan-flavored Spindrift. Guo traces this proliferation to a visit at Flavor Man, a beverage development laboratory in Louisville, Kentucky that has quietly helped create tens of thousands of drinks over 30 years. Most work is under NDA; publicly acknowledged clients include Ocean Spray and Jones Soda. Their trophy wall also includes a salmon-flavored juice box designed specifically for aging cats.

**Advances in Flavor Science**

Tom Gibson, Flavor Man's chief flavorist, explains that what people experience as "flavor" is largely aroma — hundreds of distinct chemical compounds that together produce, say, the smell of a fresh strawberry. His lab holds thousands of individual aroma chemicals in amber bottles, many of which appear across multiple fruits; a single background note can serve as a building block for berry, cherry, and Tutti Frutti flavors simultaneously. Gibson demonstrates one compound found in dairy, sweat, and vomit — also a trace note in ripe berries — to illustrate how dissonant elements enrich flavor. Without that note, he says, a berry flavor is "flat."

The key shift Gibson identifies: 10 to 20 years ago, many of today's aroma chemicals were simply not commercially available — "In the old days, this either wasn't found, or it was found and no one manufactured it." New scientific techniques and extraction methods have expanded what he calls "the palette of colors." Perhaps the most striking development is that bacteria can now be trained to synthesize specific flavor compounds, eliminating the need to harvest the source plant at all. This expanding supply of exotic building blocks has enabled a generation of exaggerated or entirely invented flavors — including dragon fruit, which Gibson likens to a Jackson Pollock of aroma chemistry — that have in turn shifted the American palate toward craving still more novelty.

**The Functional Beverage Boom**

Kristen Weber, Flavor Man's CTO, works in a lab stacked with Ziploc bags of white powders — pure caffeine, gum acacia (a prebiotic fiber ubiquitous in brands like Olipop), and other functional ingredients. She traces the category arc from the caffeinated energy drink craze of the 1990s and 2000s through the seltzer boom to the current vogue for functional beverages: drinks that claim to support gut health, promote relaxation, improve sleep, or deliver electrolytes. Weber notes that the human body doesn't actually require any of these products — the industry's strategy has been to invent new use occasions to displace plain water. The same logic that convinced gym-goers to reach for a Gatorade instead of the water fountain is now being applied to the bedside table with nighttime recovery sodas. Total beverage sales have grown over the past decade as a result.

During Guo's visit, Flavor Man was prototyping next-generation concepts: non-alcoholic versions of an Aperol spritz and a matcha liqueur, a non-alcoholic protein beer (described as tasting like pennies), and what Weber calls a "sports broth" — a pineapple-flavored beef bone broth drink tinted Gatorade yellow, delivering electrolytes, protein, and bone broth solids.

**The Manufacturing Barrier Collapse**

Dave Defoe, Flavor Man's founder, provides the third and most structural explanation. In the 1990s, beverage manufacturing was dominated by Coke and Pepsi, and contract bottling lines required enormous minimums — typically a full truckload, roughly 12,000 cases and more than a quarter million cans — a threshold prohibitive for any new entrant wanting to test a product at small scale.

Starting around 2010, the craft beer explosion changed the economics. Microbreweries proliferated across American cities, each running canning lines that were frequently underutilized. As alcohol consumption has since declined, these breweries have had even more excess capacity to rent to non-beer clients at small-run prices. Defoe estimates current all-in costs at roughly 20–30 cents per can in flavoring and sweeteners, about 20 cents per can in aluminum (up from 10 cents pre-tariff-and-inflation), and roughly 20 cents per can in manufacturing, targeting a total around 60 cents per can. Small test batches of a few thousand cans can now be arranged for just a few thousand dollars. "The barrier to entry has lowered significantly." Defoe notes most aspiring drink entrepreneurs don't even know it's possible. Guo draws a parallel to the music industry, where cheap recording software and streaming distribution similarly democratized access for independent artists. In both cases, falling barriers have produced a long tail of upstarts taking market share from established Goliaths — unusual in an era when incumbents typically dominate.

**The Planet Money Soda**

The episode closes with a tasting of a prototype Flavor Man created for Planet Money. Tom Gibson's team settled on a yuzu elderberry sparkling coconut water: yuzu is a Japanese citrus described as more complex and floral than orange, one that "explodes in a lot of different directions"; elderberry is an earthy blackberry-adjacent flavor popular in Europe; coconut water provides natural electrolytes including potassium, sodium, and magnesium. Guo describes the drink as refreshing, light, and "zingy" with an unfamiliar citrus note he couldn't place until told it was yuzu.

The team then produced a surprise second version containing jambu extract — a tropical herb traditionally used to numb the mouth for toothaches, comparable to Sichuan peppercorn — which creates a building tingling sensation "like someone had put a tiny little battery in my mouth." Weber notes jambu is occasionally used as a background note to amplify perceived effervescence; under U.S. labeling rules it would appear simply as "natural flavors." The final beverage is framed as "sophisticated yet approachable with a dose of surprise." Guo and Malone discuss producing a small listener batch for a live event.

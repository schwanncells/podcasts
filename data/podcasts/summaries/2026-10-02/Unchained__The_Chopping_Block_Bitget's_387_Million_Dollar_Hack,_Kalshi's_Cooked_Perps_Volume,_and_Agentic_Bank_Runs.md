# The Chopping Block — "The Chopping Block: Bitget's 387 Million Dollar Hack, Kalshi's Cooked Perps Volume, and Agentic Bank Runs"

**Hosts:** Haseeb Qureshi, Tarun Chitra (head of Gauntlet; crypto risk modeling and protocol design), Robert Leshner (founder, Superstate; DeFi infrastructure). **Other Participants:** Sieve (Dragonfly Capital; early-stage crypto investor).

## TL;DR

- **Bitget hacked for $387 million by Lazarus Group (North Korea)** — hot wallet breach; company's $460 million insurance fund covered all losses and has begun payouts, preventing contagion.
- **Stolen funds laundered via ThorChain and Nier** — attacker moved stablecoins and assets cross-chain to avoid freezes; ThorChain's permissive design facilitated this, while Nier's anomaly detection froze $500K and blocked $100M+ in additional movement.
- **Property rights on blockchains exist on a spectrum** — hosts debate whether protocols should be neutral infrastructure (like Ethereum/Uniswap, can't censor), regulated like exchanges (full censorship), or somewhere in between; Nier's middle-ground approach allows programmatic blacklisting of demonstrably hacked funds.
- **ConsenSys validator compromised, raising questions about "risk-free" staking** — attacker stole <1 ETH in unclaimed rewards but triggered forced exit of 5% of Lido's stake; foregone yields (~200 ETH opportunity cost) highlight that staking is not actually risk-free and may command a risk premium.
- **Kalshi's perp volumes were artificially inflated via market maker incentives** — analyst "Benny" exposed $5,500 recurring trades and wash-trading patterns; company was reportedly paying equity grants for volume; CFTC investigating; Kalshi ended the liquidity program.
- **AI agents will cause gradual "agentic bank runs"** — Muse/Claude-style agents will optimize deposits from low-yield checking to treasuries and other products, eroding banks' sticky deposit advantage; process is gradual (years) but similar to crypto's rapid capital flows; banks lack the API infrastructure to compete.

**Bitget's $387 Million Hack and the North Korea Connection**

Bitget, one of the top five crypto derivatives exchanges, suffered a $387 million hack that's "very likely attributable to the Lazarus Group, which is a part of North Korea." The breach affected hot wallets and warm wallets through an infrastructure-level compromise; the exact technical details remain unclear. Fortunately for Bitget's users, the company is "very profitable" and had $460 million set aside in an insurance fund specifically for user protection. The company immediately began payouts and has already started processing withdrawals, preventing any contagion or broader market instability. Leshner stresses that because Bitget's theft pales in comparison to last year's profits, "the industry is solid."

**Money Laundering via Cross-Chain Protocols**

The stolen funds' movement illuminates a broader debate about blockchain protocol design. The attacker first moved stablecoins off Bitget and attempted to convert holdings to non-freezable assets. They made strategic use of cross-chain trading protocols: ThorChain and Nier. ThorChain is a permissive, "censorship-resistant" spot-trading bridge; it processes any swap without evaluating the source of funds. Volume on ThorChain spiked during the hack. Nier Intents, by contrast, employs Shield—a validator-layer protocol that runs anomaly detection and checks against known hack reports. Nier froze approximately $500,000 in stolen assets and refused to route over $100 million in additional attempted movement, demonstrating active intervention capacity.

**The Philosophy of Property Rights: Neutral Infrastructure vs. Regulation**

This split response generated intense debate among the hosts about blockchain governance. Haseeb frames it as a spectrum: one extreme is complete neutrality (like Ethereum or Uniswap—no discretion, no censorship, full permissiveness), and the other is centralized exchange-style control with full KYC and withdrawal approval. Anything in the middle is philosophically incoherent or self-contradictory. Robert counters that the middle ground isn't just viable—it's necessary and desirable. He argues that "robust property rights" aren't merely passive state transitions; they include active enforcement like police determining whether a found purse was stolen. Nier's Shield can programmatically detect "this was in this protocol 10 minutes ago, and now it's all living on your balance" without invoking centralized whim.

Tarun notes that cross-chain protocols inherently introduce discretion because they must translate between two different systems of record and notions of property rights. He frames this as "laboratories of democracy" where chains can adopt different approaches, and users choose based on their preferences. Robert pushes back on whether markets actually price these differences; stablecoins (USDC vs. DAI) do not trade at premiums reflecting their respective freeze risk, though historical hacks (like DAI spiking to $1.25) suggest the market does price risk during acute stress. The conversation suggests that chains offering stronger property rights and rollback capabilities may attract users, similar to how Delaware's corporate law advantage eroded when it violated its own standards on Elon Musk issues.

**ConsenSys Validator Breach and the Illusion of Risk-Free Staking**

ConsenSys, which manages a significant slice of Lido's validators (roughly 5%), experienced a validator key compromise. The attacker accessed less than 1 ETH in unclaimed rewards. Critically, the compromised validator didn't control user funds—delegators pointed their stake at the validator, but retain ownership. ConsenSys exited all 5% of delegated stake and rotated keys; the exit window alone cost ~200 ETH in foregone rewards (opportunity cost across the full amount). The incident challenges the narrative of staking as a "risk-free rate." Tarun frames staking as actually the "best possible rate, not the worst possible rate"—volatility and unexpected risks are real. The validator surface area hasn't been fully stress-tested; Ethereum has had fewer validator incidents than other networks, but this is the first major one for a large, well-known provider. If validator compromise becomes recurring (even at a 5% annual probability), that risk should command a premium. US Treasury CDS currently trade at 35 basis points; the realized loss here is much smaller, but the principle stands. Leshner notes that adding even a small risk premium to staking—say 35 bps—would materially reduce staking participation and dramatically cut leverage usage by looping strategies.

**Kalshi's Artificially Inflated Perp Volumes**

Kalshi operates a derivatives exchange and launched a perpetuals product after CFTC approval. An analyst going by "Benny" (self-identified as an autistic Swiss quant on Twitter) publicly challenged crypto influencer "ICObeast's" claims about Kalshi's massive volume. Benny's thread demonstrated that Kalshi was running wash-trading schemes: recurring $5,500 trades visible in order books, patterns suggesting coordinated entry and exit by the same parties, and market maker incentive structures that explicitly rewarded high volume. While market maker rebates are standard on traditional exchanges (CME, equity markets), Kalshi's structure was egregious: 3 million in open interest but hundreds of millions in daily volume. Anyone inside the company "with any access to the data" should have noticed. Kalshi's defense—that trades were "genuine activity" because one side was losing money—was widely panned as circular reasoning. The drama escalated when ICObeast's apology appeared to be ghostwritten by Kalshi's legal team (abrupt tone shift from profanity to corporate language like "I may have misspoken"). The Wall Street Journal subsequently reported CFTC investigation into Kalshi's volume claims. Kalshi announced termination of its liquidity incentives program, which should reduce the wash-trading incentive. The hosts note this is not novel to crypto—DeFi yield farming and perp DEX incentive races have driven similar inflated volumes before—but Kalshi's public marketing of the inflated numbers as evidence of mainstream adoption crossed a line.

**Agentic Bank Runs and the Erosion of Deposit Stickiness**

Apollo published research suggesting that AI agents like Muse or Claude (Hermes-style agents) will trigger "agentic bank runs" in traditional banking. The mechanism is simple: consumers have historically kept cash in checking accounts earning zero basis points simply because it's inconvenient to move money. Banks have built their entire profitability on this "laziness" and the resulting sticky deposits. An AI agent can automate the optimization: monitor deposit rates across banks and products (treasuries, money market funds, etc.), and rebalance automatically. JP Morgan currently pays roughly 1 basis point on checking; treasuries yield substantially more. An agent can eliminate this friction.

The hosts split on the severity. Robert argues this will be gradual—it will take years for the average American to hand over banking and portfolio information to an agent. Tarun notes that wealth transfers from boomers to younger, more tech-savvy heirs will accelerate the shift incrementally. However, both agree the effect is real: banks' profitability is built on consumer inertia, and agents destroy inertia. One critical constraint: legacy banks like JP Morgan fiercely resist open APIs and agent integration. JP Morgan fought Plaid for a decade over even read-only access. Amazon blocked Muse integration specifically because Amazon's margin comes from ad placements, not product sales; an agent that optimizes for price would eliminate Amazon's pricing power. By contrast, newer fintechs like Column and Mercury are building MCP hooks and fine-grained agent permissioning from the ground up, putting them at a structural advantage.

The hosts draw a parallel to crypto. DeFi saw identical capital flight: yield farmers and loopers instantly abandon one protocol for another if rates improve by 10 basis points. Traditional finance has been insulated from this dynamic because consumers don't optimize; AI agents will bring crypto's efficient-market behavior to banking. The analogy is sharp: "Homo economicus is back, baby. Everyone's homo economicus." If agents become ubiquitous, even a small probability of SVB-style bank runs accelerates into a contagion, because agents make coordinated flight possible and automatic. Unlike humans who need to be tweeted at to panic-exit, agents can execute en masse in minutes.

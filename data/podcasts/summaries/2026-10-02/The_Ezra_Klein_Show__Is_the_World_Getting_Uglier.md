# The Ezra Klein Show — "Is the World Getting Uglier?"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Debbie Millman (graphic designer, brand designer, teaches at School of Visual Arts, hosted Design Matters podcast for 20+ years, revamped logos for Burger King, 7-Up, Haagen-Dazs).

## TL;DR

- **Ugliness vs. vulgarity — the world is getting more vulgar, not necessarily uglier.** Millman distinguishes: ugliness can be intentional and seen as glamorous; vulgar is accidental, lacking intention. Examples include the Cybertruck, bloviating housing that infringes on neighbors' space, and Gen Z fashion.
- **Proportion and mathematics underpin beauty.** Humans can assess when something is out of proportion without knowing the math. When something is out of proportion, it gives people a sense of discombobulation and changes the emotional atmosphere around them.
- **Two separate critiques of modern design:** The world is getting uglier (things actively make you feel bad) AND the world is getting drabber (things make you feel nothing—what an N+1 essay calls the "drab sublime"). Most of what surrounds us now is mute and inoffensive rather than beautiful or intentionally ugly.
- **Apple's minimalism has dominated tech for 25 years.** The iPod's white earbuds (introduced six weeks after 9/11) created the visual language that every tech company now copies to "seem like the very best," leading to a quarter-century of homogenized design.
- **Nearly a third of American homes in 2021 were investment vehicles rather than lived-in homes,** driving a "color recession"—homeowners painting gray because resale value depends on bland neutrality, not personal expression. Homeowners associations and rental logic prevent people from differentiating their spaces.
- **The attention economy rewards extremity, eroding proportion and purpose.** Designers are incentivized to be instantly visible, recognizable, and shareable. As algorithms serve everything, sameness also increases (coffee shops in New York, LA, Mexico City, and Buenos Aires look identical).
- **Convenience is displacing individuality.** People trade privacy and personal expression for frictionless digital services. Physical friction (vinyl records, hand-me-down furniture, liner notes) forces attention and creates objects with soul, whereas planned obsolescence and digital services make people feel like things are temporary visitors.
- **Public space is being consumed by market logic.** Advertising increasingly colonizes public spaces like the NYC subway system, which receives only 1% of operating revenue from ads. Poetry and art on public transit offer an alternative vision of the civic space.

**Vulgarity, Proportion, and the Foundations of Design**

Millman argues the world is getting "more vulgar" than uglier. Vulgarity is accidental, lacking intention; it's something nobody sets out to create. Ugliness, by contrast, can be intentional and even seen as glamorous or subversive—think Donald Trump's gilded Oval Office. Examples of modern vulgarity include the Cybertruck (unnecessarily imposing), bloviating housing developments that fill entire lots leaving six inches of grass between house and sidewalk, and certain Gen Z fashion trends.

The foundation of beauty, Millman explains, is proportion. Much of proportion is mathematical, and humans can assess disproportion without knowing the math. When something is out of proportion, it creates a sense of discombobulation and literally changes the DNA of the environment around us. Marie Kondo's insight wasn't just about tidying; it was teaching people to notice what their environment makes them feel. The complication is that much of what is now considered beautiful was originally out of proportion and received negative reviews—suggesting that taste evolves and people can't always trust their immediate feeling about a thing.

**The Two Critiques: Ugly vs. Drab**

Klein identifies two distinct lines of critique in the "is the world uglier" conversation. One holds that the world makes you feel something bad—it's actively ugly. The other is that the world is getting drabber: it makes you feel nothing. An N+1 essay from 2023 coined the term "drab sublime" for the homogenous, under-thought ocean of stuff—flat-pack furniture, municipal infrastructure, commercial graphics—so uniform it feels like a digital rendering.

Klein notes that while he doesn't find the Cybertruck beautiful, he appreciates that it makes him feel something, that someone's vision is behind it. Contrast that with how much apartment buildings and cars around us are mute—they're inoffensive but intentionless. Things without intention, built without respect for surroundings, are perhaps worse than either ugly or vulgar. There's been a flattening of the world around us—in fashion, apartment design, car design, place design—and now a reaction is occurring: people are moving toward maximalism, "looks-maxing," more attention-grabbing and vulgar styles.

**The Apple Minimalism Legacy**

Millman explains that six weeks after 9/11, Apple introduced the iPod with white earbuds—an extremely minimalist gesture into a moment of chaos. The earbuds signified early adoption and cultural status. The minimalist visual language took hold across all tech companies because, for 25 years, everyone wanted to seem like the very best. Now, roughly a quarter-century later, every tech company—until recently, Samsung and Apple notwithstanding—looks the same because the expectation of what excellence should look like has crystallized. As new designers come into leadership at Apple and Samsung, this may begin to shift.

**Design as Manufactured Meaning and Branding**

Millman defines a brand as "manufactured meaning." A crucifix, a religious symbol, the white iPhone earbuds—these are manufactured meanings that entire groups agree on. Design is what communicates differentiation through that manufactured meaning. The issue now is that many things are under-differentiated. While we can cite concrete reasons for sameness—fuel regulation standards, efficiency requirements, earthquake safety codes—these strictures push everything in the same direction aesthetically. The intentions we now have include many other things than just beauty: energy efficiency in buildings, aerodynamics in cars. We're optimizing on function, which forces sameness, and people are losing sight of the possibility that beauty and function can coexist.

**Convenience vs. Individuality and the Soul of Objects**

Klein notes that when life becomes frictionless, it loses something even if the product quality is higher. Physical friction—vinyl records requiring you to flip them, hand-me-down family furniture—forces attention and creates meaning. Record collections mean more to people than Spotify libraries partly because of the physical, tactile relationship; books mean more than Kindles partly because of the experience of holding and marking them.

Millman and Klein discuss the tension between convenience and individuality. We're giving up so much privacy and individuality for convenience. Millman points out that when you design things with care—like the Turkish ShowerCap brand making beautiful shower caps at slightly higher cost—you're signifying that someone thought the person deserved more respect in that experience. But increasingly, planned obsolescence and knowing something is temporary makes people feel it's a temporary visitor to their home rather than something that expresses who they are.

The loss of hand-me-down furniture illustrates this: when furniture was expensive and lasted, families had to attend to it; now, knowing a piece is a starter item that will be replaced in a few years, people don't invest emotionally. But Millman finds it magical to pass down objects that hold memory and continuity—her niece now has her great-grandmother's dining room table, where generations have gathered and lived.

**Market Logic Eating Other Spaces**

Klein argues that one of the core problems is market logic colonizing domains it shouldn't dominate. Markets demand frictionlessness, tradeability, and legibility across distances. They don't want specificity. Evidence of this includes the "color recession"—in 2021, nearly a third of American home sales went to people with no intention of living in them; they were investment vehicles or flip properties. When you view your home as something to resell, painting it yellow and green for idiosyncratic personal taste damages resale value. Homeowners associations enforce this logic preventively, controlling paint colors to preserve the tradability of neighboring properties.

Millman agrees this logic filters into ugly behavior: big bloviating houses that infringes on others' air and space, raising their own property to make more money. That's exactly what is being controlled when resale value is the priority—controlling the emotional space of others.

**Public Space and the Commercialization of the Subway**

Klein discusses his concern about the New York City subway—a remarkable, egalitarian public space where all strata of society mix. Yet it's become a canvas for relentless advertising: ambulance-chasing lawyers, Tinder, plastic surgery, horror movie grotesque imagery. The MTA gets about 1% of operating revenue from ads, so it's not financially critical. Yet the visual environment is dominated by market messages. Occasionally, the MTA uses ad space for poetry or art, which is beautiful and harks back to the progressive movement's goal of enriching the citizenry through public space.

Millman notes that if the ads were interesting and provocative—something that made you think or inspired you—it might be different. But they're boring and ugly. San Paolo, Brazil took down all its billboards as a test, and it changed the visual environment. The artist Ed Ruscha has a series exploring what the world looks like without advertising. Klein argues that maybe public spaces should not be commercialized in this way—that there should be oases from constant capture of attention and visual field by the market.

**Symbols, Tribes, and the Blurring of Private and Public**

Millman traces the history of symbols: humans have used symbols for 50,000 years to designate what they believe and belong to. Symbols—religious iconography, brand logos, fashion choices, plastic surgery decisions—telegraph tribal affiliation and values. We're socialized to experience the world through symbols and objects since prehistoric times.

The conversation turns to a shift in how symbols function. A gold chai worn as a religious symbol in childhood carried different weight than Nike shoes worn to fit in. A flag pin meant something specific post-9/11 that it wouldn't have meant in other eras. Yet now, symbols from non-commercialized spheres (religious symbols, national flags) increasingly sit on the same plane as brand logos. This represents a convergence of logics that perhaps should remain separate.

Klein describes how different spheres operate differently: markets demand frictionlessness, tradability, and legibility across distances, while private, personal, and spiritual domains operate differently. One thing that's happening is market logic is consuming more and more spaces, imposing its logic on them. If you know your home is an investment or you'll only stay a few years, you won't paint it your favorite colors.

**Ugly-But-Beloved Design: The Dr. Zimor Ads**

Millman recounts that Dr. Zimor, a dermatologist, ran advertising on New York City subway billboards for decades with deliberately garish, chartreuse and orange visuals, rainbows, terrible kerning, bad scaling, poor silhouettes. The ads were hideous, but over time, through repetition and consistency, they became part of the fabric of the subway system—like the "Crazy Eddie, his prices are insane" ads of the 1970s.

Even when something is ugly, if you see it enough times, it becomes familiar and ultimately beloved. People become attached to the consistency, even if the thing itself is not beautiful. When logos change, people don't necessarily miss the old logo because it was good—they miss it because it's familiar. In a time when everything feels tenuous, taking away that familiarity makes people feel insecure. The key is that there was clearly a person, a voice, a vision behind Dr. Zimor's ads—not a committee or sophisticated branding agency. That humanness mattered.

**The Tension Between Individuality and Social Judgment**

Ezra's friend dresses in loud, attention-grabbing ways intentionally to make people feel joy and whimsy. Ezra dresses to not be noticed. There's a spectrum: some people are willing to have others have opinions about them, to be polarizing; others want to be acceptable and have the gaze fall off them. Millman notes that even choosing not to be obvious is still a choice—it's still deliberate. Her father covered his Ralph Lauren polo logo with a flag, thinking he was avoiding logomania, but he'd just exchanged one symbol for another.

The broader question is whether society has become less comfortable with people taking stances that invite judgment. Are people choosing safety in blandness? Every choice—wearing black exclusively, painting your house gray, wearing no makeup—is deliberate self-expression, but it can also signal conformity. The question is how much of a society's apparent sameness comes from genuine preference for minimalism versus anxiety about being judged.

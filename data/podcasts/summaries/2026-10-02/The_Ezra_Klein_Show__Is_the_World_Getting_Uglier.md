# The Ezra Klein Show — "Is the World Getting Uglier?"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Debbie Millman (graphic designer and brand identity specialist; associate professor, School of Visual Arts; host of *Design Matters* podcast for over 20 years; designer of logos for Burger King, 7-Up, and Haagen-Dazs).

## TL;DR

- **Vulgarity is ugliness without intention** — Millman distinguishes between deliberate ugliness (which can be subversive or trendy) and vulgarity, which is unintentional and lacks purpose. The Cybertruck, bloviating housing, and contemporary Gen Z fashion exemplify vulgarity, not calculated ugliness.
- **The "drab sublime" vs. intentional extremity** — One critique of modern design is homogeneity and flatness (coffee shops, airports, apartment buildings that feel like "digital renderings"); another is a reaction pushing toward attention-grabbing maximalism (Trump's gilded Oval Office, oversized Gen Z fashion, the Cybertruck). Both can make people feel something, but vulgarity is worse than drabness because drabness makes you feel nothing.
- **Proportionality and mathematics are foundational** — Humans can assess whether objects are out of proportion without knowing the math. Disproportionate things trigger a sense of discombobulation and change "the DNA around us," affecting how we feel in our environment.
- **25+ years of tech minimalism followed the iPod** — Six weeks after 9/11, Apple introduced the iPod and white earbuds, a super-minimalist statement that signaled early-adopter status and cut through post-9/11 chaos. Every tech company copied that aesthetic for the next 25 years because everyone wanted to seem excellent, creating decades of sameness until companies like Samsung recently hired new design leadership.
- **Design is manufactured meaning communicating differentiation** — A crucifix or brand symbol works because societies agree it signifies something. Good design makes you feel better; good design for society makes the world better. Bad design is disrespectful, greedy, or immoral (Millman's example: the terrible billion-dollar TSA security line system in airports worldwide).
- **Market logic is colonizing public spaces, driving sameness** — Homes painted gray for resale value, subway systems saturated with advertising, logos on eggs: the market wants frictionless, tradable, legible-across-distances things, which erodes specificity and individuality. One-third of U.S. home sales in 2021 went to people with no intention of living in them, incentivizing neutral aesthetics.
- **The right has a theory of beauty; the left struggles with one** — The modern right explicitly champions classical and neoclassical architecture, while Trump fetishizes gilt and gold. The left, wary of dictating what beauty should be and rooted in critiques of the past, has no clear visual answer. That asymmetry is politically significant.
- **Convenience and frictionless design risk making life feel soulless** — Vinyl records, physical books, hand-me-down furniture carry sentiment and embodied memory in ways digital alternatives don't, even when the digital versions are objectively more convenient. When design removes all friction, it can flatten emotional resonance and a sense of living rather than being served.

**Vulgarity, Intention, and Proportion**

Millman argues the world is not getting "uglier" so much as "more vulgar." Ugliness can be intentional—subversive, trendy, even glamorous in the right context. Vulgarity, by contrast, is unintentional and lacks any real purpose; nobody sets out to make something truly vulgar. She points to examples: the Cybertruck's imposing, unnecessary scale; "bloviating housing" where houses are squeezed into lots with only inches of grass between structure and sidewalk; contemporary Gen Z fashion that doesn't express a coherent aesthetic but rather an expiration-dated trend.

Proportion, Millman explains, is mathematical and deeply felt. Humans can assess whether something is out of proportion without knowing the mathematics. Disproportionate objects trigger discombobulation and change the "DNA" of the environment around them, making people feel something at a visceral level. Everything makes us feel something; the question is what and whether that feeling is good.

**The Drab Sublime and the Reaction to Sameness**

Klein introduces the concept of the "drab sublime" from an N+1 essay—a world of homogenous, under-thought stuff (flat-pack furniture, municipal infrastructure, commercial design) so pervasive that reality feels like a digital rendering. He contrasts this with a countermovement: the Cybertruck, Trump's gold-encrusted Oval Office, maximalist design trends, and "looks-maxing" in men's spaces. The Cybertruck, though Klein doesn't find it beautiful, at least evokes feeling and appears to reflect somebody's vision, making it preferable to mute, inoffensive buildings that trigger nothing.

**The Tech Minimalism Era and Its Persistence**

A key turning point was Apple's 2001 introduction of the iPod and white earbuds, just six weeks after 9/11. That design was brilliantly minimalist—the device stayed hidden in a pocket, but the earbuds signaled early-adopter status and cut through post-9/11 chaos with a super-clean visual language. Every tech company subsequently adopted that aesthetic for 25 years because everyone wanted to seem excellent and aspire to Apple's standard. Only recently, with new design presidents like Samsung's Mauro Porcini, is that dominance beginning to shift. This decades-long convergence on a single visual language is itself a driver of sameness.

**Design as Manufactured Meaning**

Millman defines design as "the communication of differentiation through manufactured meaning." A crucifix signifies something because societies agree it does—the symbol itself has no inherent meaning. Brands, like religions, operate the same way: they're agreed-upon symbols that telegraph belonging, values, and status. Good design makes individuals feel better and makes society better. Bad design is disrespectful, greedy, or immoral—her example being the TSA PreCheck security system in airports: a billion-dollar system deployed worldwide that is, fundamentally, a design disaster.

**Market Logic and the Loss of Individuality**

Klein brings data: in 2021, nearly one-third of American home sales went to investors or people with no intention of living in them, not as long-term homes. This financialization of housing creates an incentive to paint homes gray—neutral, tradable, high-resale-value colors. Homeowners associations often restrict personalization to protect neighborhood resale values. The market demands frictionlessness, tradability, and broad legibility, all of which erode specificity and personality.

Millman connects this to a broader tension between convenience and individuality. Everything around us expressing something about who we are is a form of resistance—not through cost but through intentionality. Yet when homes are temporary investments with planned obsolescence, individuals don't invest the care they once did in hand-me-down furniture, which carried decades of family memory and emotional weight.

**Public Space and the Colonization of Attention**

Klein laments the advertising saturation in the New York City subway. The MTA generates only about 1% of operating revenue from ads, yet the transit system is plastered with ambulance-chaser lawyers, plastic-surgery ads, and Tinder promotions—turning a remarkable public, egalitarian space into a captive audience for market messages. Poetry and art occasionally replace ads on the subway walls, and those moments are beautiful precisely because they represent a different logic than commerce. San Paulo once removed all billboards, transforming the visual environment; Ed Ruscha created blank-billboard art that is "really quite provocative" in its refusal of message.

The market doesn't hate beauty; it would love to sell beauty. But the market's intentionality is selling product, not enriching public space. As market logic expands into previously non-commercial domains, it imposes its own visual language.

**Politics of Beauty: Right, Left, and Gender**

The modern right has developed an explicit theory of beauty: classical and neoclassical architecture, Roman columns, gilded excess. Trump, in particular, uses the word "beautiful" constantly and has implemented executive orders favoring classical architecture. Tucker Carlson has argued that ugly, dehumanizing buildings convince people they don't matter—a "third rail" argument Millman surprisingly agrees with, though she points out the hypocrisy: the current White House renovations (gold filigree, the reflecting pool, the arch) are making Washington uglier, and the right hasn't protested.

Klein argues the left has more difficulty articulating a counter-aesthetic because it's suspicious of imposing a singular vision of beauty and rooted in critiques of the past, leaving no clear visual answer for the future. The right, being reactionary, can simply reach backward to classical precedent. But the question remains: Can the left develop a positive vision of beauty that's not reactionary and not dictatorial?

Millman notes the masculine coding of certain design languages (the Cybertruck's sharp, aggressive lines) versus feminine-coded curves and sensuality (Apple Park's rounded edges, the Guggenheim). Gender and power dynamics infuse aesthetic politics—it's not a gender war so much as a fight over how power should be visualized and communicated.

**Convenience, Frictionlessness, and Lost Soul**

A producer's observation prompted a discussion of how removing friction from life can paradoxically flatten it. Vinyl records and physical books require effort—you must get up to flip a record, place the needle—yet they create physical, tactile relationships that digital libraries don't. Hand-me-down furniture carries embedded sentiment; new IKEA pieces are temporary, disposable. When design makes life perfectly frictionless, it begins to feel soulless and "smoothed," less alive.

Millman and Klein conclude that the tension is between convenience and individuality, and that extremes in either direction cost us deeply. Some middle ground—a bit of friction, a bit of ease—seems necessary.

**Final Thoughts and Recommendations**

Millman leaves the conversation uncertain whether the left can articulate a compelling counter-aesthetic to the right's invocation of classical beauty. She's an analyst, not a futurist. Her book recommendations span poetry (*The Voice That Is Great Within Us* by Hayden Carruth), Gabriel García Márquez's *Love in the Time of Cholera*, James Joyce's *Ulysses*, and Roxane Gay's *Hunger*—the last of which led her to reach out to Gay, eventually marry her in 2020, and develop a personal and professional partnership that transformed her life.

# Science Friday — "The tilcayo is the new wild cat on the block"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guest:** Paolo Nogales-Ascarunz (National Geographic explorer and biologist; leads the Bolivian Felid Research and Conservation Program studying wildcats including jaguars, ocelots, and margays).

## TL;DR

- **Tilcayo is a three-pound spotted cat from Bolivia — the first new wild cat species described in more than 100 years.** The tiny cat is noticeably smaller and slenderer than other Leopardus-genus cats in the region like ocelots.
- **First discovered as a kitten in the Yungas forest by a local resident** — the finder brought it home during rain, kept it for a year, then realized it was wild when it began chasing chickens instead of behaving like a domestic cat.
- **Paolo first met the tilcayo in 2017 at a rescue center** — he was so struck by its size and markings that he took nearly 100 photographs, spurring 10 years of genetic analysis to confirm it was a previously unknown species rather than a variant of the already-classified tiger cat (Leopardus tigrinus).
- **Initial genetic results were confusing and Paolo abandoned the work, only returning after gaining more training** — he notes that as young scientists, the instinct is to distrust unexpected results, assuming you've made a mistake rather than discovered something new; skepticism is built into scientific training.
- **Small wild cats are frustratingly secretive and difficult to study** — camera traps often capture only ears or tails; Paolo says that a scientist could spend a lifetime working with small cats and never see one in the wild.
- **Paolo funds his cat research through crop genetics work (quinoa and canyawa)** — he pays his bills through agricultural genetics grants while pursuing wild cat conservation out of passion, hoping the tilcayo's fame might attract funding to dedicate fully to cat conservation.
- **Tilcayo sparked extraordinary cultural enthusiasm in Bolivia** — social media exploded with photos, street art appeared with slogans like "long life to the cat" (rather than the president), and political initiatives emerged to protect the species.
- **The tiny cat is becoming an "umbrella" or "flag species" for Yungas forest conservation** — while big charismatic animals like jaguars traditionally drive conservation efforts, the beloved tilcayo is now spurring organizations to protect the degraded Yungas forest ecosystem, which faces deforestation and old mining operations.

**Discovery in the Yungas Forest**

A local resident in the rural Yungas forest found a kitten on the road during rain and brought it home, initially treating it as a domestic animal. After living with the cat for a year, he noticed it was behaving differently—chasing chickens and displaying prey-drive behaviors atypical of house cats. Realizing something was unusual, he brought the animal to Zenda Verde, a wildlife rescue center.

**Paolo's First Encounter and the Decade-Long Confirmation Process**

Paolo first encountered the tilcayo in 2017 while volunteering at the rescue center. He was immediately struck by the animal's extraordinarily small size and slender build, so distinct from other Leopardus cats he knew—jaguars and ocelots—that he took nearly 100 photographs trying to capture its appearance. He initially worked under the assumption that the cat was Leopardus tigrinus, the tiger cat species that had been the catch-all classification for all similar small spotted cats in the region. However, when comparing photographs to descriptions in cat reference books, he noticed the Brazilian variant looked substantially different, with distinct markings and spot patterns.

This discrepancy prompted him to pursue genetic analysis. His initial attempts, using saliva samples, produced confusing results that he doubted and abandoned. He did not share these anomalous findings with colleagues, assuming he had made an error. Only after gaining additional training in genetics years later did he return to the work, this time using hair samples that replicated the earlier results. Paolo emphasizes a lesson learned through his own and peers' experience: young scientists are trained to be deeply skeptical of surprising results, first assuming they have made a methodological mistake rather than discovered something genuinely new. Before publishing, researchers verified the result was not previously described by consulting museum collections and reviewing historical records of naturalists working in Bolivia.

**The Secretive Nature of Small Wild Cats**

Paolo addresses why small cats remain understudied and underfunded relative to larger charismatic species. Small wild cats are extremely secretive, rarely visible to researchers. Camera traps frequently capture only partial images—an ear, a tail—rather than clear identification footage. Paolo notes the frustrating asymmetry: a researcher studying giraffes or elephants sees their subjects; a cat researcher can work for years and see almost nothing. This secretive nature makes small cats difficult to study, observe, and photograph in the wild, which in turn makes them harder to advocate for—funding agencies and the public prioritize visible, well-studied megafauna like jaguars.

**Research Funding and Career Constraints**

Paolo reveals that most of his conservation funding has come from jaguar protection grants, forcing him to frame small cat work alongside larger cat projects to secure resources. To pay his bills, he works on crop genetics—studying quinoa and canyawa varieties—while pursuing wild cat research out of personal passion. He expressed hope that the tilcayo's recent discovery and media surge might attract direct conservation funding, allowing him to dedicate his career fully to cats rather than dividing effort between crops and wildlife.

**Social and Political Impact in Bolivia**

The tilcayo's discovery ignited extraordinary public enthusiasm in Bolivia. Images spread across social media platforms including TikTok, Facebook, and Instagram. Paolo observed people discussing the cat on public transit; street artists created murals celebrating the species, with slogans including "long live the tilcayo" as a counterpoint to contemporary political frustrations. Political initiatives emerged to establish legal protections for the species. This enthusiasm is particularly significant given Bolivia's concurrent economic and political challenges—currency instability, fuel shortages, and corruption concerns—yet the tilcayo offered citizens something to celebrate as distinctly Bolivian.

**Tilcayo as a Flag Species for Ecosystem Protection**

While traditional conservation strategy relies on large "umbrella species" like jaguars and bears to justify protecting broader habitats, Paolo argues the tilcayo is now functioning as a "flag species"—a charismatic focal point driving conservation action. Several organizations have launched initiatives to protect the Yungas forest specifically because it is the tilcayo's habitat and the public loves the cat. This approach yields conservation benefits beyond the tilcayo itself: protecting the forest safeguards multiple species and ecosystems. The Yungas forest has received limited conservation attention despite facing serious threats including deforestation and legacy mining operations; the tilcayo's popularity may reverse that neglect.

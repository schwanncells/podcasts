# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Can we make Social Security better for blue-collar workers?"

**Host:** Adrienne Ma, Waylon Wong. **Guests:** Nari Rhee (director, Retirement Security Program, UC Berkeley Labor Center); Alicia Munnell (founder, Center for Retirement Research, Boston College).

## TL;DR

- **Blue Collar Social Security Fairness Act would lower retirement age to 60** — Congresswoman Haley Stevens' proposal would allow workers with a history of physically demanding jobs to claim full benefits at 60 instead of 67, affecting jobs like construction, roofing, nursing, and manufacturing.
- **Life expectancy gap: top 20% income earners live 13 years longer than bottom 20%** — A National Academy of Sciences study found this disparity, which undercuts Social Security's progressivity since wealthier people collect benefits far longer.
- **Blue-collar workers start careers younger and face early mortality** — Blue-collar workers typically start working after high school while white-collar workers continue education; they also die at younger ages, compounding inequality in Social Security's current structure.
- **Nearly 40% of jobs could qualify as "physically demanding"** — Alicia Munnell warns that defining which jobs qualify would be politically impossible and could expand the program to baristas, dishwashers, and hospitality workers, draining already-strained finances.
- **Social Security trust fund projects depletion by 2032** — If the main fund runs out, all retirees could see benefits cut by 20% or more, making it risky to pay full benefits earlier to a subset of workers.
- **Alternative solutions exist: raise payroll tax to 7.2%, eliminate income cap at $185,000, or reduce high-earner benefits** — Surveys show majorities of Republicans, Democrats, and Independents support these reforms to close Social Security's financing gap.

**Context: September Jobs Report**

The episode begins with monthly labor statistics: unemployment ticked up to 4.2% in September, the economy added 29,000 jobs, and annual wage growth slowed to 3% compared to inflation at 3.4%. Jobs are growing slowly and wage growth is lagging inflation.

**The Stevens Proposal: Early Retirement for Physically Demanding Work**

Congresswoman Haley Stevens, a Democrat from Michigan, introduced the Blue Collar Social Security Fairness Act. The bill would allow workers with a documented history of physically demanding jobs to collect full Social Security benefits at age 60 instead of the current full retirement age of 67. Stevens cites construction, roofing, nursing, and manufacturing—industries prevalent in her Detroit-area district—as examples. The bill would require the government to compile and update a list of physically demanding jobs every three years. Workers would need to have spent roughly 10 to 20 years in qualifying jobs to claim early retirement benefits.

**The Case for Earlier Retirement: Equity and Worker Health**

Nari Rhee argues that the proposal is justified on fairness grounds. Workers in physically demanding occupations often develop health conditions that force them out of the workforce in their 60s; the current system expects them to continue working until 67 but does not account for their physical deterioration. Rhee emphasizes that Social Security is designed as a progressive program, providing lower-income workers with a higher percentage of their pre-retirement income than wealthy retirees receive. However, that progressivity is undercut by a stark life expectancy gap: people in the top 20% income bracket live an average of 13 years longer than people in the bottom 20%. Blue-collar workers amplify this problem: they typically start their careers after high school while white-collar workers pursue additional education, meaning they contribute to Social Security sooner but also tend to die younger. Allowing blue-collar workers to retire at 60 would partially offset this inequity.

A survey by the National Academy of Social Insurance showed that a majority of Americans support allowing workers with a history of physically demanding work and declining health to access benefits sooner, lending public support to Stevens' proposal.

**The Challenge of Defining "Physically Demanding"**

Alicia Munnell raises a critical practical objection: determining which jobs qualify would be enormously complicated. While the Bureau of Labor Statistics maintains a detailed classification system for job physical demands—used to assess disability insurance eligibility—applying it to retirement benefits would invite political disputes. Munnell points out that construction work is obviously physically demanding, but workers in other fields might argue the same: baristas, dishwashers, hotel housekeeping staff, and restaurant workers face long hours on their feet and sustained physical exertion. Reaching agreement on a fixed list could take years or prove impossible.

**Social Security's Financial Crisis: The Core Objection**

Munnell's deeper concern is financial. Social Security is currently paying out more to retirees than workers are contributing via payroll taxes. The system relies on a trust fund to make up the difference, but that fund is projected to deplete by 2032. When depletion occurs, all retirees would face an automatic 20% or greater benefit cut. Allowing a subset of workers—potentially as many as 40% based on BLS job classifications—to claim full benefits at 60 rather than 67 would accelerate depletion and worsen this crisis. Munnell emphasizes that Stevens' proposal targets the symptoms rather than the root problem.

**Alternative Solutions: Tax Increases and Benefit Adjustments**

Munnell advocates for structural reforms that address Social Security's solvency directly. Congress could raise the payroll tax from 6.2% to 7.2%. Alternatively, lawmakers could eliminate the current cap on taxable income; income above $185,000 annually is not subject to Social Security payroll tax, meaning high earners pay a smaller percentage of their income. A third option would reduce benefits for retirees with higher incomes. A survey by the National Academy of Social Insurance found that majorities of Republicans, Democrats, and Independents support these reforms if implemented together to close the financing gap.

Munnell also suggests making it easier for blue-collar workers to qualify for Social Security's disability insurance program rather than retirement benefits. The disability fund remains on stable financial footing, though Congress has previously transferred money from disability to retirement when needed. Addressing the root solvency problem through tax and benefit adjustments would provide a more durable solution than carving out early retirement for a specific occupational category.

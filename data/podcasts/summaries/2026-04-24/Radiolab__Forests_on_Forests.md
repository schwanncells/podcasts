# Radiolab — "Forests on Forests"

**Host:** Latif Nasser (primary), Robert Krulwich (returning emeritus host). **Guests:** Nalini Nadkarni (ecologist, pioneered forest canopy research since 1980s), Karina Mifune (ecologist, studied canopy soil nutrient dynamics in Olympic Peninsula rainforest).

## TL;DR

- **Tree canopies hold 50% of all terrestrial life** — entirely unknown until the mid-1980s when scientists began accessing the forest canopy, discovering a parallel ecosystem teeming with insects, birds, plants, and animals previously thought to be empty.
- **Canopy soils accumulate on tree branches** — layers of moss, lichen, and decomposing organic matter create soil that can be several feet deep in old-growth redwoods, creating miniature "gardens in the sky" identical to the forest floor below.
- **Canopy creatures include earthworms, salamanders, copepods, and flowering plants** — redwood canopies alone contain salamanders that spend their entire lives 100+ feet in the air without touching the ground, and even aquatic copepods (tiny shrimp-like creatures) living in mossy mats with no explanation for how they arrived.
- **Canopy soils are nutrient-rich havens during spring scarcity** — while the forest floor experiences phosphorus and nitrogen shortages in spring, canopy soils have abundant nutrient availability, creating an upside-down resource distribution.
- **Trees grow roots upward into canopy soils** — large trees respond to spring nutrient scarcity by extending roots up into their own canopy soils to access the "good snacks," like opening a secret cabinet.
- **Trees grow on top of trees in fractal layers** — five-foot spruce trees grow in canopy soil on branch nooks, baby maples grow in mature maples, and the pattern cascades upward: little plants on trees, then littler plants on those, creating nested ecosystems.

**The Invisible Ecosystem Above**

The episode builds directly on Radiolab's 2016 "From Tree to Shining Tree" episode, which profiled Suzanne Simard's discovery of underground fungal networks connecting trees and allowing them to communicate and share resources. That story revealed the "forest beneath the forest"—but it was only half the picture. In spring 2026, producer Annie McEwen brings to the show the revelation of the "forest above the forest": an equally complex, previously unknown ecosystem hidden in the canopy. Until the mid-1980s, scientists thought tree canopies were essentially empty. Accessing them required specialized equipment—mountain climbing ropes, platforms, even dirigibles piloted by French explorer Francis Hallé—and once scientists finally climbed up, they discovered something astonishing: roughly 50% of all terrestrial life on Earth lives in tree canopies. As Robert Krulwich notes, this proportion makes sense when you consider the sheer volume of insects, birds, plants, and animals in forests like the Amazon, but it nonetheless struck the scientific community as unbelievable.

**Nalini Nadkarni: From Childhood Wonder to Canopy Pioneer**

Nalini Nadkarni, now known as the "queen of the canopy," first fell in love with trees as a child. She spent afternoons sitting in the maple trees lining her parents' driveway, wondering why some branches had yellower leaves than others and imagining herself following squirrels thread-by-thread through the treetops. When she entered graduate school in the early 1980s proposing to study the forest canopy, her advisors were dismissive—the scientific consensus held that canopies were empty, featureless space with no meaningful research value. But Nadkarni had intuition that "it's not enough to just stand on the ground and look up." With modified mountain climbing equipment, she began ascending the giant old-growth Douglas fir and big leaf maple trees in the Olympic rainforest of western Washington, a temperate rainforest where fog from the Pacific Ocean bathes trees daily in moisture. Her early expeditions revealed something extraordinary: branches covered in thick layers of moss, lichen, and ferns—what she describes as an "unruly green shade carpet."

**Canopy Soils: Miniature Forests in the Sky**

What Nadkarni found beneath those moss mats shocked her: actual soil, packed several inches to a foot deep on branches. This soil had formed over many years as mosses and leaves died and decomposed directly on the branches, creating a mini-ecosystem 100 feet in the air. Inside this canopy soil, Nadkarni found earthworms, invertebrates, and mushroom fungi—the same fungal networks that facilitate tree communication on the forest floor had climbed the trees themselves. When scientists later accessed the canopy of California's old-growth redwoods using cranes and platforms, they found pockets of soil up to three feet deep—deep enough to support flowers, berry bushes, mosses, and lichens. Some of the most remarkable inhabitants were creatures thought to be impossible at such heights: salamanders that spend their entire lives in the canopy without ever touching the ground, and tiny aquatic copepods (shrimp-like crustaceans that form the base of marine food webs) living in mossy mats with no understood mechanism of arrival. These canopy soils are not just fascinating microcosms; they collectively host the majority of the planet's terrestrial organisms.

**The Nutrient Secret: Abundance Above, Scarcity Below**

Ecologist Karina Mifune, studying in the Olympic Peninsula, made a crucial observation while collecting soil samples throughout the year. During the spring growing season—when trees are putting forth new leaves and desperately need phosphorus and nitrogen—the forest floor experiences a nutrient bottleneck. Competition among trees' roots and mycorrhizal networks intensifies as resources become scarce. Yet during these same lean times, the canopy soils Karina sampled contained dramatically higher concentrations of available phosphorus and nitrogen compared to the forest floor. As she told the producers, "downstairs there's shortage. Upstairs there's abundance." The canopy soil becomes a "secret cabinet with all the good snacks" while "all the school children are fighting over the resources" below. It is not merely a pretty ecological curiosity—it is a functional nutrient reserve that serves the very trees above it.

**Trees Grow Roots Upward**

The most startling discovery emerged when Nadkarni, still perched in the canopy digging through soil samples, began tracing unusual root systems running up and down the branches. These roots didn't belong to the visible mosses or ferns; some were as thick as her wrist. Following them like a detective—sometimes using water to unstick roots from soil, sometimes swinging around branches on her rope—Nadkarni traced these roots back to their origin: they came from the tree itself, specifically from branch wood, not the ground. The living tree was sending roots upward into its own canopy soil. Karina theorizes this happens during droughts or spring resource scarcity: the tree essentially recognizes the nutrient-rich soil above it and dispatches roots upward. As she explains using the metaphor of a secret snack closet, the tree has a hidden reserve it can access during lean times. The behavior is not instinctual memory of previous droughts—it appears to be an active response to immediate resource conditions.

**Fractal Forests: Life Stacking on Life**

Both Nadkarni and Mifune emphasize that canopy ecology is a newly emerging field with discoveries continuing to accumulate. Karina reports finding five-foot spruce trees growing in canopy soil pockets, and baby maple trees germinating and growing in the branches of mature maples, turning parent trees into literal nurseries. The fractal recursion does not stop there: small plants grow on these new small trees, and mosses grow on those plants, creating nested layers of life stacked upward. Krulwich playfully observes this inverts the old expression "it's turtles all the way down"—in forests, it's trees all the way up, with each layer supporting littler plants and littler layers, creating what Nadkarni describes as "layers and layers and layers of life" the further one ascends.

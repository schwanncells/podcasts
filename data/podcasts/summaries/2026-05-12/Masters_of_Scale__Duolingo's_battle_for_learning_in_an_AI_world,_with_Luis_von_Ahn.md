# Masters of Scale — "Duolingo's battle for learning in an AI world, with Luis von Ahn"

**Host:** Bob Safian. **Guests:** Luis von Ahn (CEO and co-founder of Duolingo; inventor of CAPTCHA and reCAPTCHA; former Carnegie Mellon CS professor and AI instructor).

## TL;DR

- **Duolingo crossed $1 billion in annual revenue last year** — it reaches more than 100 million active users, with 90% of users non-paying and 10% generating 90% of revenue through subscriptions.
- **An internal AI memo sparked backlash but was misunderstood** — von Ahn's memo proposed hiring and evaluation based on AI usage; the public interpreted it as a mass layoff threat. Duolingo has never laid off employees and actually grew headcount during the memo period.
- **AI quality fails at scale** — while individual AI outputs look impressive, scaling to thousands of artifacts (stories, designs) reveals "20% sloth"; von Ahn pulls back on AI in areas like creative design where human artists maintain an edge.
- **Duolingo shifts to user growth over revenue growth in 2026** — despite knowing the market would react negatively, von Ahn deprioritizes short-term monetization (e.g., ad density) to capture users before the market shifts; 2 billion people globally are learning English, making language learning the largest learning domain.
- **Fun and engagement drive learning more than content quality alone** — von Ahn argues the bottleneck is motivation, not curriculum; Duolingo's approach is to keep learners entertained rather than frustrate them with classroom-level intensity, since users are "one click away from Instagram or TikTok."
- **The owl mascot's "unhinged" brand is now rebalancing** — Duolingo will shift from 80% unhinged/20% wholesome social media to a more balanced split, emphasizing that Duolingo actually works rather than relying on stunts alone.
- **Math and chess are expanding beyond languages** — languages remain the largest subject (2 billion learners globally vs. 1 billion for math); chess has surprisingly become the second most popular Duolingo subject and is "getting the most traction outside of languages."
- **AI translation won't replace language learning** — hobbyists learn for pleasure regardless of AI capability (just as millions play chess despite computers surpassing humans in 1997); professionals learn English for jobs and immigration, requiring genuine fluency.

**The AI Memo Controversy and Lessons Learned**

Von Ahn's internal all-hands email last year proposed that new hires prove AI couldn't do their job and that existing employees be evaluated on AI usage. He was shocked by the public backlash, which interpreted the memo as a layoff announcement. Internally, the memo was uncontroversial; Duolingo is a technology company, and von Ahn taught AI at Carnegie Mellon before launching the app. The misunderstanding arose because external audiences feared AI job displacement. In reality, Duolingo increased headcount during the period, never conducting layoffs.

Von Ahn has since refined his stance. He no longer evaluates all employees on AI usage, after hearing that some were using AI "for AI's sake" rather than for actual productivity gains. He now evaluates contribution to the company, recognizing that AI helps most employees but not universally. The nuance he arrived at: AI is a tool to amplify productivity when it genuinely helps, not a mandate to apply everywhere.

**The Quality Limits of AI at Scale**

While AI demonstrates well individually, von Ahn identified a critical flaw: generative outputs look impressive in isolation but degrade at scale. When Duolingo needs 1 million different stories for language learners, roughly 20% end up being "pure sloth" — low-effort, unusable content. This applies to coding too; engineers saw little speed-up in feature velocity because time saved on working AI outputs was consumed debugging cases where AI failed unpredictably.

Design and creative work are areas where AI falls furthest short. Duolingo employs top artists and designers, and AI has not matched their level of creativity or polish. Von Ahn refuses to compromise quality for speed, even though AI delivers fast. The principle: avoid using AI where quality would decrease, regardless of the speed gain.

**A Strategic Pivot: Growth Over Revenue**

In 2026, Duolingo is deliberately prioritizing user growth over profitability. This is a counterintuitive move for a publicly traded company, and von Ahn knew the stock market would dislike it. His reasoning is twofold. First, AI is improving Duolingo's teaching effectiveness every year; studies show the product is significantly better at teaching than it was four years ago, creating a massive opportunity to expand the user base and capture market share. Second, Duolingo's growth slowed in late 2025 even though it remains exponentially positive; von Ahn finds this unacceptable and wants to accelerate.

The business model supports this flexibility. About 90% of monthly active users are non-paying; 10% pay for premium subscriptions and generate 90% of revenue. Duolingo could easily increase monetization (more ads per lesson, paid features), but von Ahn deliberately holds back. He acknowledges that two ads per lesson would push more users to subscribe, but beyond a point—say, 17 ads per lesson—users simply leave the app. Long-term thinking outweighs short-term revenue extraction.

**Motivation as the Core Challenge of Learning**

Von Ahn identifies motivation as the hardest part of teaching, not curriculum or methodology. Books, for instance, are an incredibly effective learning technology, yet most people lack the stamina to learn from them. The real problem is attention—keeping people engaged in a world of shortening attention spans. This is why Duolingo emphasizes fun; it's one way (not the only way) to maintain motivation. For learning AI, for example, von Ahn would argue that showing immediate results—building a dashboard, seeing something work—keeps people motivated better than abstract study.

In a freemium app, frustration has a different meaning than in a classroom. In a classroom, the learner cannot walk away; Duolingo learners can. Therefore, Duolingo must keep frustration lower than a classroom would, even if it means teaching slightly less per lesson. The trade-off favors more time spent learning at lower intensity rather than less time at higher intensity.

**Rebalancing the Owl's Brand**

Duolingo's mascot, the owl Duo, became a viral sensation through unhinged social media campaigns (twerking, staging his own death, crude humor). This creativity drove growth, but von Ahn is now threading a needle: keep the fun and playfulness but also emphasize effectiveness. The marketing split will move from 80% unhinged and 20% wholesome to something more balanced. The goal is to tell prospective learners that Duolingo actually delivers results—something the "stunts" approach sidestepped.

**Expanding Beyond Languages**

Duolingo now teaches math, music, and chess alongside its core language courses. Von Ahn's principle is to teach subjects that are meaningful to users and take a long time to learn. Languages are by far the largest domain; roughly 2 billion people globally are learning English, more than any other subject including math (1 billion learners). However, chess has become Duolingo's second-most popular subject, attracting surprising traction outside the core user base. Von Ahn predicts math will eventually overtake chess, though current numbers don't yet support that prediction.

When asked whether Duolingo might converge with Khan Academy, von Ahn clarified the differences. Khan Academy uses high-quality educational videos and targets schools; Duolingo aims for the general population using a mobile-game approach. The businesses are complementary rather than convergent.

**Resisting the "AI Will Replace Learning" Narrative**

AI translation and automation have prompted some to question whether language learning remains relevant. Von Ahn dismisses this concern across two user segments. Hobbyists learn languages and play chess for intrinsic enjoyment, regardless of whether computers can outperform humans (computers beat the world chess champion in 1997, yet millions still learn chess). Professionals learn English because they actually need fluency for jobs or immigration to English-speaking countries; AI assistance cannot substitute for genuine proficiency.

More broadly, von Ahn argues that a society where nobody learns anything would be chaotic. Even with advanced AI, people need to learn—to develop logical thinking, professional skills, and meaning in life. The question is not whether learning remains necessary but what people will choose to learn.

**Stakes and Long-Term Vision**

Duolingo's underlying ambition is societal: can billions of people find meaning through learning rather than doomscrolling? At 100 million active users, Duolingo has reached an unprecedented scale for voluntary education, but von Ahn considers this insufficient. He hopes to reach billions in a decade. His optimistic bet is that long-term alignment exists between profit and education quality; a company prioritizing learner outcomes will ultimately make more money than one extracting revenue at all costs. This conviction drives his willingness to temporarily sacrifice stock price for sustainable growth.

Von Ahn also applies unconventional hiring practices aligned with this philosophy. Duolingo's rule: "We'd rather have a hole than an a-hole." Job candidates are evaluated not just by interviewers but by limo drivers sent to pick them up; mistreatment during the ride has occasionally disqualified otherwise strong candidates. The principle is that company culture and human treatment matter as much as competence.

# The Ezra Klein Show — "Trump vs. the Bond Market"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Robin Wigglesworth (editor, Financial Times' Alphaville blog; co-host, *A Story of Money* podcast; author, forthcoming *A Fabulous Debt: The Epic Story of How Bonds Built the Modern World*).

## TL;DR

- **U.S. Treasury debt exceeded $40 trillion recently** — the government now spends more annually on interest payments than on the entire defense budget, a pattern not seen since World War II.
- **Bond yields have risen persistently to 4–5%** — Scott Bessent, Trump's Treasury Secretary, has attempted multiple interventions (enlarged buyback programs, signaling about yields not reflecting fundamentals), but yields keep climbing because the market views these moves as ineffective and a sign of desperation.
- **Hedge funds now own ~8% of the Treasury market, up from ~2%** — more than Japan, China, and Saudi Arabia combined. Because they use heavy leverage to magnify returns, rapid interest rate rises can force them to liquidate, causing sudden Treasury yield spikes unlike behavior during normal market downturns.
- **Trump wants lower bond yields but is pursuing contradictory policies** — he wants the Federal Reserve to cut rates while simultaneously running large deficits and spending heavily, which naturally raises yields. The Fed raising rates (not cutting) is the most credible signal that could calm bond investors.
- **Foreign Treasury ownership is declining and geopolitical risk is rising** — China and other countries are "tiptoeing away" from the Treasury market; the Trump administration's unpredictability and the war with Iran (which has disrupted the Strait of Hormuz, raising energy prices and inflation expectations) have shaken confidence.
- **A 50% chance of U.S. debt crisis in the next decade is commonly cited among bond investors surveyed** — yet almost all said they made no change to portfolio strategy, a disconnect Wigglesworth attributes to human nature (people acknowledge risks but don't act until crisis hits them directly).
- **The most likely path to higher yields (6–7%) would involve runaway inflation** — this would imply the Federal Reserve gave up on controlling inflation and the economy entered stagflation; lower yields would signal recession or crisis, neither a good outcome.
- **Three recommended books on finance and economics:** *Barbarians at the Gate* (history of American business through private equity); *The Prize* by Daniel Yergin (history of the oil market and 20th century through oil); *Lords of Finance* by Liaquat Ahmed (interwar period central banks and the Great Depression).

---

**Why U.S. Treasuries Matter to Everyone**

Klein opens by establishing why Treasury bonds, often overlooked by the general public, affect nearly every financial decision ordinary Americans make. Treasury yields set the baseline "risk-free rate" that governments, companies, and households use to price borrowing. A mortgage, auto loan, or credit card rate is the Treasury yield plus a spread. When Treasury yields rise, so do all downstream consumer costs, making affordability a pressing political issue. The Treasury market trades roughly $1 trillion daily and is the most liquid bond market on Earth, making it central to how capital flows globally.

Wigglesworth explains that U.S. Treasuries are attractive to global investors — central banks, pension funds, sovereign wealth funds, foreign governments — primarily because they are perceived as safe (the U.S. is unlikely to default) and extraordinarily liquid (you can buy or sell massive amounts without moving the price much). This dual appeal has kept U.S. borrowing costs historically low, even as the national debt grew enormous.

**The Debt Burden: $40 Trillion and Interest Now Exceeds Defense**

The U.S. federal government debt recently crossed $40 trillion. This matters because rising interest rates on the debt have increased annual interest payments dramatically. The government now spends more each year servicing interest on the debt than it spends on the entire defense budget — a milestone last seen after World War II. Wigglesworth notes this reflects both the large stock of debt and the fact that older bonds issued when rates were 1–2% are being refinanced at 4–5%.

Historically, Wigglesworth reminds Klein that the 1990s saw fears the U.S. government might run out of debt (due to budget surpluses), an almost unimaginable situation today. The financial crisis in 2008 and the COVID pandemic reversed that trajectory, and governments worldwide have remained in large-deficit mode even as economic conditions improved, a "hard habit to kick."

**Scott Bessent's Bond Market Interventions**

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has adopted what Wigglesworth calls a "kitchen sink approach," deploying multiple tools to push Treasury yields lower. These include enlarging the Treasury's buyback program (purchasing longer-dated Treasury bonds from the secondary market and issuing fresh, liquid bonds to pay for it). However, the scale is trivial relative to the market — a few billion dollars when the Treasury market trades over $1 trillion daily. Wigglesworth compares it to "putting out a wildfire with a water pistol."

Bessent's public statements assert that yields don't reflect underlying fundamentals, echoing John McCain's infamous "the fundamentals of our economy are strong" quote from 2008 (which did not age well). Wigglesworth sees in this either strategic signaling or, more likely, a mixture of political pressure and the Treasury Secretary's own knowledge that these tools won't work. Bessent, a former currency and bond trader, understood market dynamics; yet he pursued interventions he likely knew would fail. This incoherence, Wigglesworth suspects, reflects Trump's desire for lower yields and Bessent's attempt to appease the president even while recognizing the action's futility.

**Inflation, Central Banks, and the Role of Forward Guidance**

Rising yields stem partly from lingering inflation concerns. Post-COVID inflation peaked but hasn't fully returned to the Federal Reserve's 2% target. The recent war with Iran, which closed the Strait of Hormuz, disrupted energy supply and pushed up oil prices, reviving inflation fears. Bonds are hurt by inflation because a fixed-rate bond that pays 5% loses its real purchasing power if inflation rises to 5% or higher.

The Federal Reserve, now chaired by Kevin Warsh (replacing Jay Powell), could signal inflation-fighting resolve by raising interest rates, which would lower bond yields almost immediately as investors gain confidence the Fed will contain prices. However, Trump has indicated preference for lower rates, and Warsh has hinted at reducing "forward guidance" — the Fed's practice of pre-announcing future policy. Wigglesworth thinks this is philosophically interesting but practically overstated; central banks change their minds on forward guidance frequently when data shifts, and people often ignore forward guidance anyway.

**Hedge Funds as a Destabilizing Force**

A major structural shift in the Treasury market is the rising role of hedge funds. In the 2000s, foreign central banks and sovereign wealth funds were the dominant Treasury buyers, treating them as a stable reserve asset regardless of yield. This "savings glut" has shrunk; into the void, hedge funds have stepped in. Hedge fund ownership of Treasuries has grown from roughly 2% to nearly 8%, meaning they now own more Treasuries than Japan, China, and Saudi Arabia combined.

The risk is that hedge funds use leverage — borrowing to magnify their returns. A small leverage ratio (e.g., borrowing $900M to control $1B in Treasuries with $100M of capital) means that if borrowing costs rise even slightly, the math breaks: the fund must liquidate to survive. During normal downturns, bond investors flock to Treasuries as "safe haven," pushing yields down. But when hedge funds are forced sellers, the opposite happens: yields spike as leveraged players dump holdings. This occurred in March 2020 and again in April 2025 ("Liberation Day"), when Treasury yields shot higher despite the economic stress that normally sends yields lower. Wigglesworth calls hedge fund leverage "one of the biggest fault lines running through the financial system right now."

**The Trump Administration's Contradictory Approach**

Historically, presidents facing rising bond yields pursue one or both of two strategies: appoint a Fed chair who markets respect, and pursue deficit reduction/fiscal contraction. Trump has done neither. He pushed out Powell, appointed Kevin Warsh (more credible than some candidates but still seen as beholden to Trump), and pursued massive tax cuts and spending — "huge amounts of spending, huge amounts of tax cuts" — with no serious deficit reduction efforts.

Wigglesworth compares the dynamic to Henry II's "will no one rid me of this troublesome priest?" — Trump signals displeasure with bond yields, and Bessent scrambles to obey, even when the obedience doesn't make sense. Trump wants multiple goods simultaneously: lower bond yields, lower Fed interest rates, economic growth, and lower inflation. Some of these are incompatible. If the Fed cuts rates (as Trump wants), that typically pushes yields down in the short term but may allow inflation to re-accelerate, ultimately pushing yields back up. The market sees the incoherence and doubts the administration's seriousness about controlling inflation.

**Declining Foreign Ownership and Geopolitical Risk**

Wigglesworth notes that things previously "unimaginable" are now "not unthinkable" — the idea that foreign demand for Treasuries might weaken. China and other countries have "tiptoeing a little bit away from the Treasury market." This reflects both economic shifts (less demand for reserves in some countries) and geopolitical anxiety: the war with Iran, Trump's unpredictability, the sense that U.S. institutions are less stable than before.

Foreign ownership of Treasuries remains crucial because the U.S. runs a current account deficit (spending more than it produces), and foreigners fund much of the government's borrowing. If foreign demand weakens, domestic demand must make up the gap, which would require higher yields to attract more U.S. savers.

**On Bond Vigilantes and Debt Crisis Scenarios**

Klein asks whether "bond vigilantes" — the amorphous collection of investors who can collectively decide to stop buying a government's bonds — pose a real threat. Wigglesworth acknowledges the phrase is overused but notes it has "a bit of truthiness." In a credit-dependent economy, the power to deny or raise the cost of credit is enormous. However, the U.S. is different from smaller countries like Pakistan or Sri Lanka because it borrows in its own currency and can literally print dollars. A conventional default is "vanishingly unlikely." But unconventional debt crises are possible: runaway inflation from excessive money printing, financial system breakdown if the Fed co-opts policy, or a loss of confidence that forces a painful adjustment.

When Wigglesworth asked bond traders what a U.S. debt crisis would look like, the consensus was: not like anything else in the world. Most didn't fear outright default. But they were aware of tail risks.

On the puzzling NBER survey showing bond investors estimated a 50% chance of a U.S. debt crisis within a decade yet made no portfolio changes, Wigglesworth invokes Saint Augustine's plea: "Lord, make me chaste, but not yet." People intellectually grasp climate change, debt crises, pandemics, but few restructure their lives until the crisis arrives. Bond investors similarly recognize risk but don't act until it's imminent.

**Scenarios for Bond Yields Through 2028**

Klein asks about the likelihood of Treasury yields hitting 6–7% by the November 2028 election. Wigglesworth refuses to predict but offers scenarios. Higher yields (6–7%) would imply elevated inflation and likely mean the Fed has tacitly given up on control, signaling entry into stagflation. That's not a healthy outcome. Lower yields would signal recession or deep crisis. Healthy outcomes lie in the middle: moderate inflation, steady growth, yields stable in the 3–4% range. The AI build-out is a wild card — if it proves productive, it could juice growth and support higher yields naturally; if it's a bubble, a collapse would lower yields but amid recession.

---

The episode captures a moment of unusual tension in markets and policy: Treasury yields moving higher as the U.S. debt burden grows, a Treasury Secretary attempting interventions he likely knows won't work, hedge funds poised as a potential shock amplifier, foreign confidence wavering, and the Federal Reserve under new leadership with an unclear stance. Wigglesworth stresses that bond markets synthesize enormous amounts of information (growth, inflation, institutional stability, geopolitics) and that trying to "jawbone" them into submission — as Bessent appears to be attempting — rarely succeeds.

**Host:** Kenny Malone. **Guests:** Dan Pashman (host of The Sporkful food podcast); Pete Nietzsche and Tom Orton (Rutgers University agricultural researchers); Miguel Gomez (agricultural economist, Cornell University; specializes in specialty crop supply chains).

**The Rise of Branded Produce**

The supermarket produce section has undergone a dramatic transformation, shifting from uniform commodity crops to branded varieties with premium pricing and legal protection. Kenny Malone and Dan Pashman document this trend—which they call "Honeycrisp-ification"—using examples like Honeycrisp apples, Cotton Candy grapes, and Cutie mandarins. These branded fruits and vegetables stand out visually and cost more than standard options, representing a fundamental change in how produce is grown, marketed, and sold. What was once a simple tomato or apple section now features dozens of specialty options, each claiming unique flavor, appearance, or other qualities.

**The Legal Framework: Plant Variety Protection and Bayh-Dole**

Two crucial legislative changes enabled this explosion of branded produce. On Christmas Eve 1970, President Richard Nixon signed the Plant Variety Protection Act, which allowed breeders to own intellectual property rights to new crop hybrids—a right that previously did not exist. A decade later, the Bayh-Dole Act of December 12, 1980 gave federally funded researchers (including those at public universities) the default right to own and monetize whatever they created with federal funding. These two laws fundamentally changed agricultural incentives. Before 1970, a breeder could develop a superior tomato or apple, but anyone could legally propagate and sell it. After Bayh-Dole, public universities—which receive substantial federal research funding—could control and profit from their plant breeding innovations.

**Honeycrisp: The Breakout Success**

The Honeycrisp apple, released in 1991 by the University of Minnesota, became the first major produce success story under the new legal regime. The university licensed Honeycrisp trees to growers for roughly one dollar per tree and has earned over $20 million in cumulative licensing revenue. This success inspired other universities to pursue agricultural biotech as a profit center. The University of Washington followed with the Cosmic Crisp apple, which has generated over $30 million in licensing fees. These financial windfalls demonstrated to university administrators that plant breeding could be a major source of institutional revenue, fundamentally shifting the incentives for agricultural research at public institutions.

**The Scarlet Sunrise Tomato: From Concept to Trademark**

At Rutgers University, agricultural researchers Pete Nietzsche and Tom Orton spent six years (12 generations of breeding) developing the Scarlet Sunrise tomato. Their inspiration came from the Isis candy cherry tomato, which was sweet and had an attractive red-and-yellow bicolor appearance but cracked easily during transport—a fatal flaw for commercial tomato production. To solve this problem, they crossed the Isis candy with a hardy red grape tomato, selecting across generations for sweetness, distinctive coloring, firmness, disease resistance, and ripening consistency. The result is a small, bicolor (yellow with red blush) grape tomato that tastes significantly better than standard red grape tomatoes, with good balance between firmness and juiciness. In 2019, they handed the invention to Rutgers' intellectual property office, which named it Scarlet Sunrise (combining the university's "Scarlet Knights" brand with the bicolor appearance) and registered it for trademark and patent protection.

**Escaping the Commodity Trap**

According to Miguel Gomez, an agricultural economist at Cornell, the fundamental driver behind the proliferation of branded produce is the need for growers to escape what economists call the "commodity trap." When growers cultivate a standard commodity crop like red soybeans or standard red grapes, they compete solely on price—there is no product differentiation, so the only competitive lever is to undercut rivals, leading to a "race to the bottom" where profit margins collapse. The solution, Gomez explains, is differentiation: creating or branding a product that stands apart so growers can charge premiums and avoid competing purely on price. Some differentiation strategies are simple—like Love Beets, which packages and brands ordinary commodity beets with attractive labeling and preparation (pickling, balsamic drizzle)—without using proprietary genetics. Others, like Scarlet Sunrise, involve creating a genetically distinct variety with intellectual property protection.

**Consumer Demand and Feedback Loops**

Gomez emphasizes that the explosion of branded produce reflects not just producer strategy but genuine consumer demand. The availability of sophisticated food media—including podcasts like Dan Pashman's Sporkful—has educated consumers to taste and appreciate subtle differences between produce varieties. This creates a feedback loop: growers invest in branded varieties because consumers are willing to pay premiums for novelty and flavor; consumers, in turn, see more options and become more adventurous in their produce choices. However, Gomez cautions that there may be limits to this dynamic. In the apple category, he believes the market has passed the saturation point—there are now so many apple varieties (Honeycrisp, Cosmic Crisp, Pink Lady, Gala, and dozens more) that consumers face a "search cost" burden when choosing, and total apple consumption is not rising to accommodate all the new varieties. The tomato category, by contrast, still has room for differentiation and new entries.

**The Compact Tomato Innovation: A Game Changer**

The most exciting discovery from the Scarlet Sunrise project was unexpected: while developing their new tomato, Pete and Tom noticed one experimental plant was markedly smaller—about half the size of a standard tomato plant—yet still produced high-quality fruit. This compact plant variant could dramatically improve tomato growing economics. Standard tall tomato plants produce fruit from top to bottom on staggered ripening schedules, forcing growers to send hand-pickers through fields multiple times over several weeks. Compact plants have a shorter, more synchronized ripening window, requiring far fewer picking passes. Additionally, compact plants need less staking, tying, and support infrastructure. Tom Orton estimates this innovation could reduce growing costs by 25 to 50 percent—a potential transformation for an industry where labor is the largest expense. Though Scarlet Sunrise itself may remain a specialty tomato with limited commercial reach due to current growing costs, this dwarf variety breakthrough could eventually reshape tomato production globally, making grape tomato cultivation economically viable at scale for growers worldwide.

# Code Switch — "Is the U.S. 'empire' beginning to show cracks?"

**Host:** Gene Demby. **Guest:** Daniel Immerwahr (historian, Northwestern University; author of *How to Hide an Empire: A History of the Greater United States*; expert on U.S. territorial holdings and imperial infrastructure).

## TL;DR

- **At WWII onset, U.S. had more colonial subjects than Black Americans** — a fact revealing how completely the colonies have been erased from American historical consciousness and how Japan's attack on Pearl Harbor was simultaneous with attacks on the Philippines, Guam, and Wake Islands.
- **The U.S. has ~750 military bases spread globally** — a scale most Americans don't know, partly because the military won't disclose exact numbers; these bases were controversial enough to topple Japanese prime ministers over domestic politics.
- **U.S. officials stopped using the word "colony" around WWI** — replacing it with euphemisms like territories, holdings, and possessions; the shift was deliberate, designed to distance America from European imperialism while maintaining the same control structures.
- **Trump is "cannibalizing" rather than maintaining the empire** — his unilateral Iran strikes and Venezuela intervention ignore the interconnected geopolitical consequences that constrained previous presidents; he treats global dominance as optional rather than systemic.
- **Working-class Americans haven't benefited from U.S. hegemony since the 1970s** — while global elites and Asian countries prospered under the post-WWII order, the U.S. middle and working classes saw stagnant wages; Trump's right-wing critique of "free riding" by allies appeals precisely because those benefits feel invisible.
- **Declining U.S. hegemony could produce either chaos or multilateralism** — the worst-case scenario is authoritarian replacement (China or Russia); the hopeful case is a transition to a world order maintained by multiple medium countries working together rather than one hegemon.

**The U.S. as a Hidden Empire**

Gene Demby frames the episode with a real conversation at an AAPI student conference: Pacific Islander student leaders questioned whether Islanders should even participate in Asian American organizing, arguing their history was colonization—the U.S. coming to them—rather than immigration. This distinction matters because while Asian Americans arrived in America, Pacific Islanders (Philippines, Hawaii, Samoa) were subsumed into America. Some argued Islanders share more with Native Americans and indigenous peoples than with Asian Americans.

Immerwahr explains that most Americans don't conceptualize the U.S. as an empire because it looks nothing like the archetypal European empires—no mustachioed men in pith helmets. Instead, the U.S. has pursued what he calls empire but refuses to name it. The opening of his book came from a personal epiphany: while teaching U.S. history, he intellectually *knew* the Philippines had been colonized for 50 years, but seeing it in Manila—streets named after U.S. presidents, a transit system built from repurposed U.S. Army jeeps—made him realize he had never actually *contemplated* what that meant for his understanding of American history.

**Rebranding Empire: Territories, Holdings, Possessions**

Immerwahr argues the U.S. deliberately obscures its imperial nature through language. The country's founding identity is rebellion against British empire—and that founding mythology is real. The name "United States of America" was designed to signal something different: states (not colonies) united in a union (not an empire), consensually joined rather than conquered. That self-conception has shaped policy in ways distinct from British openness about imperialism. Britain had an Empire Day celebrated in schools; the U.S. has always been uncomfortable with its overseas holdings, "sweeping them under the rug" rather than celebrating them.

Around 1898, when the U.S. seized territories, officials freely used the word colony. But by WWI, in the 1910s, governing officials stopped using the term and created euphemisms instead. Immerwahr quotes officials saying, "We don't really use the C word around here." This shift happened because the U.S. was building post-WWII legitimacy on a claim of difference: *we* are untainted by empire, so you can trust our military and our 750 bases spread globally. That claim is empirically false but has been essential to how America presents itself as a trustworthy global power.

**The Scale and Invisibility of American Empire**

Demby highlights a statistic that reveals the depth of America's imperial amnesia: at the onset of WWII, there were more colonial subjects of the United States elsewhere in the world than there were Black Americans in the country. Yet when historians talk about WWII, they focus on Pearl Harbor; almost no one mentions that Japan also attacked U.S. bases in the Philippines, Guam, and Wake Islands on the same day. These colonies are so "disappeared" from American consciousness that they literally fall out of the war's founding story.

Immerwahr points out this invisibility is not accidental or harmless. Living in the shadows is dangerous. Colonial subjects lose military protection, federal support, and basic political acknowledgment. The U.S. has 750 military bases globally, but the exact number is classified because the military won't disclose it. If a Chinese or French military base appeared in Texas, Americans would panic; instead, Americans take for granted bases in Germany, the UAE, and throughout the Pacific.

**Military Bases, Local Power, and Geopolitical Friction**

The presence of U.S. bases creates distinctive local economies and power structures. American military personnel typically have more disposable income and legal privilege than local populations. Service economies develop around bases—sex work, music venues, bars—as locals find ways to separate American dollars from American pockets. But bases are not just peacetime infrastructure; they are forward-deployment platforms allowing the U.S. to move troops and planes rapidly anywhere on the planet.

This creates persistent friction. Japan had two prime ministers lose power over domestic disputes about hosting U.S. bases. Host nations pressure leaders to accept bases (often with economic inducements and military assurances), but once American service members are present, incidents happen—Jeeps hit people, disagreements arise about jurisdiction. Japan wants to try American soldiers in Japanese courts; the U.S. military insists they be tried on base. This cycle repeats across the globe, creating genuine political hazards for leaders who host American power.

Immerwahr uses Iran as an example. Iran has felt surrounded by U.S. bases and perceives the U.S. as a unique existential threat. This perception is not irrational; it is baked into Iranian government legitimacy. The Islamic Republic justifies itself partly as anti-imperialist, standing up to the U.S. "Great Satan." That stance wouldn't exist without the material fact of American military encirclement.

**Trump as Empire Cannibal**

Immerwahr makes a surprising argument about Trump's military actions in early 2026: he is not maintaining the empire but cannibalizing it. Trump has been a consistent critic of U.S. hegemony since the 1980s—not from the left (that U.S. hegemony is bad for the world) but from the right (it is not paying out enough for America). He sees U.S. military spending as subsidizing other countries, allowing Japan and Europe to "free ride" while the U.S. foots the bill.

Previous presidents, nervous about Iran for decades, privately discussed military strikes but held back. Why? Because they understood the chessboard: bombing Iran could trigger a strait-of-Hormuz closure, a refugee crisis, Iranian counterattacks on Gulf states, and regional destabilization that would harm distributed U.S. interests worldwide. They possessed a broader strategic sense that "the whole world is the backyard of the United States," so you can't simply solve one problem without considering all consequences.

Trump philosophically rejects this. He wants the U.S. to be a competitor and victor, not a global policeman. When he says "I don't like Iran, so I'm going to bomb it," he is accepting downstream consequences he doesn't care about because he is "not playing chess." This approach, weirdly, represents a relinquishing of U.S. power—abandoning the notion that America has ongoing interests in controlling the Middle East. It is abandonment dressed as aggressiveness.

**Economic Beneficiaries of Hegemony and Its Decline**

Immerwahr and Demby discuss who actually benefits from U.S. hegemony. The global economic order since WWII has undeniably paid off for America and the American elite. But Trump is right that it has not benefited working-class and middle-class Americans since the 1970s. Asian countries are on the upswing, global elites are thriving, but median American wages have stagnated. This is why Trump's right-wing critique resonates: to millions, U.S. hegemony feels like it benefits everyone except them.

Demby notes the weirdness of current moment: defenders of U.S. hegemony are essentially admitting to people, "No, this is good for you, you just don't feel it," while Trump is saying, "We're being robbed by this." That unsaid admission is now being spoken aloud.

**The Shape of a Post-Hegemonic World**

When Demby asks what a world without U.S. hegemony would look like, Immerwahr offers two scenarios. A scared version imagines chaos or replacement by authoritarian powers (China or Russia maintaining global order more brutally). But a more hopeful version, articulated by Canadian PM Mark Carney, frames this as a violent but transitional period leading toward a world order maintained by multiple medium countries working multilaterally—something closer to what the international community has nominally wanted.

Immerwahr emphasizes this is empirical. We don't know which future emerges. But he expresses fear of the first scenario while remaining hopeful about the second. The decline of U.S. hegemony is already underway; Trump's tariffs and saber-rattling are accelerating it. Whether that decline produces a more equal and democratic world or a more chaotic and authoritarian one remains an open question.

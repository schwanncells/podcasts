# Unchained — "How the DOJ and SEC Cases Against BitClout's Nader Al-Naji Collapsed"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guest:** Nader Al-Naji (founder of DeSo blockchain, formerly BitClout; computer science graduate from Princeton, former engineer at Google and D.E. Shaw; pioneered algorithmic stablecoins and decentralized social media).

## TL;DR

- **Arrested in July 2024 before a family vacation to Turkey** — U.S. Attorneys arrested Al-Naji at the airport, claiming he was fleeing. He had booked the trip two weeks in advance and had gone to Turkey at the same time every year for four years; both he and his lawyers were told the DOJ had dropped the case.
- **DOJ charged wire fraud (~$3M), SEC charged unregistered securities offering** — Both agencies alleged Al-Naji defrauded investors through BitClout token sales. The SEC also claimed the DeSo blockchain itself was an unregistered security and cited an out-of-context quote suggesting it was "fake decentralized."
- **DOJ dismissed the case in March 2025 after explaining the "other half of the story"** — Prosecutors had observed unusual transaction flows (Bitcoin into wallet, then into Al-Naji's personal account, then dispersed) and assumed fraud. When Al-Naji's defense team explained the debanking crisis that forced him to hold company funds temporarily and his lawyer-approved structure, the DOJ quickly agreed there was no case.
- **SEC dismissed in March 2026 "with prejudice" without costs or fees** — This dismissal, citing reassessment of the evidentiary record, is extremely rare and indicates the SEC concluded no wrongdoing occurred. Al-Naji emphasizes the dismissal was not a settlement, meaning no admission of wrongdoing.
- **Debanking was used as a tactical pressure tool** — Al-Naji describes being debanked over a dozen times, a pattern he and lawyers linked to SEC subpoenas. He explains the government's strategy: banks receive subpoenas and immediately terminate accounts, which prevents targets from paying legal fees and causes lawyers to drop them.
- **BitClout was a viral sensation in 2021 with creator coins on bonding curves** — The platform let users buy and sell "creator coins" tied to profiles (pioneering what would become Pump.Fun's meme coins). High-profile users including Logan Paul, Mark Andreessen, Snoop Dogg, and celebrities like Kim Kardashian participated, and Al-Naji personally helped set up several notable figures' accounts.
- **Al-Naji's background includes Basis (2017), a failed algorithmic stablecoin that raised $140M** — He returned the entire amount to investors (~94 cents on the dollar, paying nothing to himself) after realizing bank-backed stablecoins would win. This demonstrates a pattern of lawful conduct and good faith that later became relevant to his defense.
- **Williams & Connolly, a government-hostile defense firm, secured swift dismissals** — Unlike most defense firms that hire ex-prosecutors sympathetic to the government, Williams & Connolly is known for targeting procedural violations. The firm identified that the FBI agent who took Al-Naji's phone violated protocol and that an SDNY prosecutor violated a court order by releasing information despite Al-Naji's explicit request not to unseal charges.

**The Basis Story and Early Crypto Background**

Nader Al-Naji entered cryptocurrency in 2012 while in college at Princeton, mining 24 Bitcoin on free campus electricity and later traveling to Japan to meet Mark Karpelès, the CEO of Mt. Gox, where he spent two hours discussing blockchain technology. His entrepreneurial path began in 2017 when he started Basis, a company pioneering algorithmic stablecoins. The thesis was that Tether and other bank-backed stablecoins would be shut down by regulators, making algorithmic approaches the only viable long-term solution. Al-Naji raised over $140 million from investors including Stan Druckenmiller, Kevin Warsh, and Andreessen Horowitz. However, in 2018, when Circle and Coinbase launched USDC, he realized the bank-backed approach was winning. Instead of continuing to chase investor capital, Al-Naji returned the entire $140 million to investors, paying them approximately 94 cents on the dollar while drawing no salary for himself. He had held over 75% equity and board control of Basis, so he "could have just paid myself a lot for the rest of my life." This decision to return capital—discussed directly with legal counsel—became pivotal evidence of his character during the later investigation.

**BitClout Launches with Creator Coins and Goes Viral**

Beginning in early 2019, Al-Naji built an alternative blockchain designed to power decentralized social media at scale. Ethereum's 15-second block time and high storage costs (approximately $1 per tweet) made it impractical for social applications. By late 2020, he launched BitClout (now called DeSo, for decentralized social), which brought storage costs close to zero. To bootstrap the network, Al-Naji introduced "creator coins"—bonding curve tokens tied to user profiles where anyone could buy or sell them at algorithmically determined prices. The feature went viral: he preloaded approximately 1,000 top profiles from Twitter/X with tradeable coins, and profile holders could claim accrued trading fees by posting about BitClout. This created a powerful incentive loop. The platform raised over 4,000 Bitcoin through a price-escalating token sale (starting at 50 cents per coin) and attracted an extraordinary range of high-profile users including Logan Paul, Snoop Dogg, Gwyneth Paltrow, Tiffany Trump, and venture capitalists like Mark Andreessen. Al-Naji notes that Andreessen claimed his profile with him personally, and he met Peter Thiel one-on-one to show him the platform, with Thiel joking "Elon is this high?" referring to the amount in the coin. The platform was especially popular in Hollywood, and Al-Naji attended a celebrity-filled party hosted by Michael Kivas where he sat next to Kim Kardashian for an hour and met attendees including Sergey Brin, Eric Schmidt, and CAA founder Mike Ovitz. The concept of creator coins later inspired friend.tech and eventually Pump.Fun, though Al-Naji emphasizes that DeSo pioneered the bonding curve meme coin concept.

**The Government Investigation Begins**

In mid-2022, shortly after BitClout was listed on Coinbase, Al-Naji learned that some major coin holders' accounts had been subpoenaed. He initially assumed this was routine, but a few months later, several early investors and team members told him they had been approached by the SEC directly and that their lawyers had forbidden them from communicating with him. Al-Naji learned this partly because some people ignored their lawyers' advice and called him on Signal to warn him. Later in 2022 (around the same time as the FTX collapse), FBI agents raided his house in Beverly Hills. Al-Naji humorously recounts asking to see their badges because he was skeptical, and when they asked him to unlock his phone, he initially declined on principle. However, he eventually agreed, and they left without raiding the house. Immediately afterward, he called all current and former employees, discovering that the FBI had targeted five of them on the same day. He then made a strategic decision: he offered to cover all their legal fees through a single lawyer, which allowed the group to coordinate through their counsel and remain close-knit through the ordeal (and led one former employee to later rejoin the project).

**The Half Story: Debanking, Unusual Flows, and the Structure**

Al-Naji explains that the prosecutors and SEC observed a "half story" that looked suspicious: Bitcoin flowing into a wallet, then into his personal account, then dispersed to other accounts and family members. Without context, this appeared fraudulent. However, Al-Naji says the full story reveals lawful behavior. BitClout was launched without a corporate entity—following Bitcoin's model of being "just coins and code"—and only involved a corporate entity when helping exchanges purchase coins through a helper company. Al-Naji was heavily debanked (over a dozen times) by banks responding to SEC subpoenas, which prevented the startup from maintaining a normal banking structure. As a workaround, money was temporarily held in his personal account and carefully tracked in accounting records, as approved by his lawyers. He describes this as being "above board" and documented: when both his main and backup bank accounts shut down simultaneously, his lawyer advised him to send money to family members as a precaution, and they explicitly reported these as gifts to the IRS. Al-Naji argues that a criminal would use cryptocurrency, not fiat, and wouldn't report the transfers. He emphasizes: "If I was going to be a criminal, I would have sent it in crypto. And I definitely wouldn't have reported it."

Al-Naji also details the debanking tactic more broadly. He describes being debanked over a dozen times and explains that the SEC's practice is to send banks a subpoena, which triggers immediate account termination. The stated goal, according to his lawyers, is to prevent the target from paying legal fees, causing defense lawyers to drop clients and prosecutors to win by default. Al-Naji says three separate law firms advised him to send money to them in advance as a security measure, anticipating that his accounts would be frozen. The experience of debanking is particularly harsh: banks close accounts instantly without explanation (claiming legal restrictions), relationship managers avoid contact, and checks are mailed on a two-week delay—a practice Al-Naji suspects allows banks to earn interest on the funds while the customer is unable to access them. He mentions losing approximately $50,000 in two separate instances due to outdated addresses.

**The Arrest and the Strategic Defense**

In July 2024, while boarding a plane to Turkey for a family vacation (the same trip he took annually for four years, booked only two weeks in advance), Al-Naji was arrested in front of his wife and son. He spent one night in jail and, due to a clerical error, was sent to federal prison for two days. He describes the experience vividly, including confusion about prison gang dynamics and receiving oatmeal from someone trying to secure his loyalty. He characterizes the arrest as baseless, noting his lawyer said he would be "the stupidest fleer all time" to book a ticket two weeks in advance if actually fleeing.

After his release, Al-Naji pursued what he calls a "scorched earth" defense strategy: he called a dozen law firms and selected Williams & Connolly, which he identifies as the country's top defense firm and the only major firm that refuses to hire ex-prosecutors and instead maintains an institutional stance of opposing government overreach. Al-Naji explains that most defense firms tout their ex-prosecutor hires, but Williams & Connolly "hates the government" and focuses on getting government officials fired for procedural violations. The firm identified multiple violations: the FBI agent's method of taking the phone was "super bad and could get him fired," and the SDNY prosecutor who authorized the press release about the arrest violated a court order (Al-Naji had explicitly requested that charges remain sealed, and the judge confirmed it was his right).

**The DOJ Dismissal in March 2025**

Al-Naji's lawyers explained the full context to the junior prosecutor who brought the case, but she didn't have the authority to dismiss. They then met with her supervisor, who understood the situation and said, "I kind of get it. I don't think anything actually wrong happened here. We're gonna let it go." Al-Naji expected a swift dismissal before year-end, but the transition to a new administration meant the supervisor left before filing the paperwork. Al-Naji and his team had to present the same case again to the new DOJ leadership in New York, but the new boss "got it immediately," and the case was dismissed in March 2025. Al-Naji credits reasonable judgment at the DOJ level, noting that while the arrest was unjustified given the lack of evidence, the agency ultimately did the right thing once it understood the full facts.

**The SEC Dismissal in March 2026 and the "With Prejudice" Language**

The SEC took considerably longer, not dismissing until March 2026—nearly a year after the DOJ. Al-Naji attributes this to the SEC being more bureaucratic and more anti-crypto than the DOJ. His lawyers had to present to the SEC multiple times, at one point explaining to a room of a dozen SEC officials why DeSo is not a security. Eventually, a sufficiently senior SEC official understood the case and realized it was wrong. Critically, the SEC dismissed the case "with prejudice," meaning it can never be brought again against DeSo. They also required Al-Naji to sign a release stating he wouldn't sue for harassment—a condition Al-Naji describes as "extremely rare" and a sign of how much the SEC understood it had erred. The SEC added language citing a "reassessment of the evidentiary record," essentially acknowledging that the facts were not what they thought when the case was filed. The dismissal came without costs or fees, meaning Al-Naji paid nothing to the government, another signal of complete vindication (settlements typically require payment). Al-Naji notes that such outcomes "do not happen every day."

**Specific Allegations and Context**

Al-Naji addresses two specific allegations that he found particularly frustrating. First, prosecutors alleged he spent approximately $1 million on rent for a Beverly Hills mansion, implying personal excess. He clarifies that this was his team's office across a two- to three-year period, not a single personal expenditure, and everyone on the team worked and lived there. Second, the SEC quoted Al-Naji as saying DeSo was "fake decentralized," which he says was taken completely out of context from a message to Rob Leshner and Tarun Chitra. In that same message, Leshner had asked whether BitClout really needed to be decentralized, given scaling challenges. Al-Naji responded with a two-part message: first acknowledging that "fake decentralized probably works for a lot of use cases," but then immediately stating in the same message, "With that said, my view at the end of the day is that the best spot for the platform is actually when it is designed as Bitcoin with people in posts, meaning fully decentralized from day one." The SEC cited only the first part in its complaint. Al-Naji emphasizes this was bad faith and that DeSo is what he designed and believes in: a fully decentralized blockchain.

**Why Brian Armstrong Mattered and the Anti-Crypto Mandate**

When the DOJ dismissed its case, Al-Naji publicly thanked Brian Armstrong (Coinbase CEO) and his Fair Shake PAC, which ran political campaigns against anti-crypto members of Congress. Al-Naji explains that he was not suggesting Armstrong directly intervened but rather that Armstrong's work bringing crypto-friendly legislators into office reduced the "anti-crypto mandate" that had driven the charges in the first place. He believes that had the Biden administration's anti-crypto stance remained strong through Congress, these cases would never have been brought. By introducing legislators who understand crypto, Fair Shake shifted the political environment in which prosecutors operate.

**The DNA Swab Declination Story**

While appearing for bail processing, Al-Naji was placed near Andrew Left (a short seller) and told to submit to DNA swabs. Al-Naji objected on constitutional grounds, telling the FBI agents that he declined and that forcing him without judicial approval could jeopardize their case. The agents brought the warden, who insisted the swab was mandatory. Al-Naji held firm: "If you want me to do it, you can make me do it, but I decline." The warden angrily left and instructed the agents to have the judge approve the swab at bail hearing. However, the agents forgot to bring it up in court, and Al-Naji was never swabbed. When he later saw Andrew Left, Left (whose own lawyers were highly paid and sophisticated) asked if Al-Naji had been swabbed. Upon learning he hadn't, Left remarked with respect, "Effort," seemingly impressed that a "little kid" had outmaneuvered the system.

**Gratitude and the Broader Mission**

Despite the ordeal, Al-Naji emphasizes gratitude. He remained positive even during his two days in federal prison, viewing it as a unique experience. He notes that he now has a "government audited track record," a humorous way of saying the government has scrutinized his conduct exhaustively and found no wrongdoing. He credits health, family, and a positive attitude for sustaining him. Al-Naji stresses the importance of the government being responsible when making allegations, since reputational damage from false charges cannot be undone. A lawyer he worked with noted that the likelihood of winning a trial against the government is only 30 percent even in the "absolute best case," due to the government's inherent power and reputation.

**DeSo and the Future**

Looking forward, Al-Naji is focused on building DeSo and its applications. He emphasizes that centralized platforms like Meta and X can ban accounts and quietly suppress reach, whereas DeSo is fully decentralized and transparent—any suppression is immediately visible on-chain. He believes DeSo is the only platform that can break the dominance of centralized social media algorithms. The project also includes focus.xyz, an app supporting subscriptions and crypto payments for content creators, all with content verifiably stored on the blockchain. Al-Naji expresses joy at being able to return to uninterrupted work and gratitude for the timing (noting excitement about AI coding tools). He reiterates his belief that a transparent, decentralized blockchain is the right long-term approach to social media.

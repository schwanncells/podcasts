# Throughline — "Why the Wall Was Built"

**Host:** Randa Abdel Fattah, Ramtin Arablouei. **Guest:** Rachel St. John (professor of history, UC Davis; historian of U.S.-Mexico border development and policy).

## TL;DR

- **August 27, 1918: Battle of Ambos Nogales killed at least 129 Mexicans and 4 Americans** — a customs inspection dispute escalated into a military battle that lasted over two hours, with Mexican civilians joining the fight and the mayor of Nogales, Mexico, dying in the conflict.
- **The first U.S.-Mexico border fence was built in response to the 1918 battle** — government officials decided they needed to physically demarcate the border to prevent such incidents; the fence was built as a joint effort, not as an anti-Mexican barrier.
- **1897 McKinley proclamation created a 60-foot clear strip through the border** — before the fence, a presidential order established a cleared zone with boundary monuments (including a white obelisk on saloon owner John Brickwood's porch), demolishing homes and businesses in Ambos Nogales.
- **The railroad (finished 1882) transformed Ambos Nogales into a prosperous cross-border trade hub** — early movement between Mexico and U.S. towns was encouraged for economic benefit; a local newspaper called the two towns "one, divided by an imaginary line only."
- **Mexican Revolution (1910+) and World War I created twin security crises** — border violence from revolutionary raids, Mexican emigration, German spy fears, and arms/money smuggling triggered deployment of military, intelligence, customs, and immigration officials.
- **Immigration restrictions and Border Patrol formalized border control by the mid-1920s** — the 1924 immigration law set strict quotas and established the Border Patrol; the infrastructure (fences, personnel, enforcement) that defines the border today was in place by the mid-1920s.
- **Modern context: 700+ miles of border wall with $50 billion allocated for expansion** — as of 2025, the Trump administration's One Big Beautiful Bill Act earmarked $50 billion of a $170 billion immigration enforcement package specifically for border wall construction and reinforcement.

**Ambos Nogales as a Cross-Border Town**

The episode begins in Ambos Nogales, a border town split between Arizona and Sonora, Mexico. In the early 1800s, it was a small settlement for railroad workers and served by saloons and brothels. The railroad arrived in 1882 and brought merchants and traders, transforming the town into a prosperous economic hub precisely because movement between the U.S. and Mexico was free and encouraged. Saloon owner John Brickwood famously built his bar on the exact border line, selling American liquor duty-free on the U.S. side and Mexican cigars duty-free on the Mexican side. A local Nogales newspaper captured the era's attitude: "We speak of the two towns as one, for they are really such, being divided by imaginary line only."

**Marking the Border: From Monument to Clear Strip**

As the town developed, government officials grew frustrated with the boundary's invisibility. Customs officers complained they could not police the space or determine where people were crossing. In response, the U.S. surveyed the border and placed a new boundary monument on Brickwood's saloon porch—a giant white obelisk. But marking the line with a stone was only the beginning. In 1897, President William McKinley issued a proclamation to create a 60-foot-wide, two-mile-long clear strip of land running through Ambos Nogales to demarcate the border more clearly. The project demolished homes and businesses, including Brickwood's saloon. For a few years, the border remained a cleared strip until other forces intervened.

**The Mexican Revolution and World War I: Twin Crises**

In 1910, the Mexican Revolution destabilized Mexico and changed the border's character. Revolutionary factions raided U.S. border towns seeking control of ports of entry and the customs revenue they generated. Simultaneously, more Mexicans began emigrating northward as violence spread. Violence along the border increased sharply. Overlapping with the revolution came World War I, which introduced a new fear: German spies infiltrating through the border. Historian Rachel St. John notes that "all of a sudden, people who had long been neighbors were suspicious of each other." The U.S. government responded by deploying multiple agencies—the military to protect towns, intelligence officers to track spies, customs agents to watch for smuggling of guns and money, and immigration officials to manage refugee flows.

**The Battle of Ambos Nogales: August 27, 1918**

Amid this tension, a triggering incident occurred on August 27, 1918, just after 4 p.m. A Mexican carpenter named Zeferino Gil-La-Madrid was leaving the U.S. after finishing work, carrying a bulky package. American officials ordered him to halt for inspection. Mexican officials told him to keep coming. A U.S. customs official raised his rifle to force Gil-La-Madrid back for inspection. In the disputed moment that followed, someone—from which side is still unclear—fired the first shot. Violence erupted across the border. Mexican civilians grabbed guns and joined the fight, immortalized in a corrido that celebrated the Nogalenses' resistance against the numerical American force. The battle lasted more than two hours. According to casualty accounts, at least 129 Mexicans and at least 4 Americans were killed, including the mayor of Nogales, Mexico. Hundreds more were wounded. A Mexican consul attempted to negotiate by proposing both sides raise white flags, but an American soldier refused, vowing to burn Nogales, Sonora if Mexico did not surrender within ten minutes. Mexico raised a white flag.

**The First Fence and the Birth of Immigration Control**

After the battle, both sides expressed regret, characterizing it as "an unfortunate affair" started by "irresponsible persons." But damage was done. Government officials on both sides concluded they needed a fence to clearly demarcate the border space and prevent future violence. The wire fence built through Ambos Nogales was one of the first U.S.-Mexico border fences. St. John emphasizes that this fence was built "in a very different mindset" from modern border walls—it was not seen as an imposition by the U.S. but as a joint effort to demarcate shared space. The fence was not designed to keep Mexicans out. Immigration was not a serious concern on the southern border until the late 1800s; early concerns focused on Chinese immigrants, not Mexicans. By the mid-1920s, however, the infrastructure of the modern border took shape. Congress passed the restrictive 1924 immigration law setting strict quotas, and established the Border Patrol to control immigration. The tools and personnel—fences, manpower, and law enforcement—that define the border today were in place.

**Modern Border Wall and Expansion**

Today, over 700 miles of border wall stand between the U.S. and Mexico. In 2025, President Trump's One Big Beautiful Bill Act allocated over $170 billion across four years toward immigration enforcement, with roughly $50 billion designated for new border wall construction and reinforcement. The administration stated it aims to complete the entire southern border wall by the end of Trump's second term.

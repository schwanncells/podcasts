# The Ezra Klein Show — "Why Are Palantir and OpenAI Scared of Alex Bores?"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Alex Bores (New York State Assemblymember; co-authored and passed the Raise Act, one of the first major AI safety regulations in any state; former Palantir data scientist and federal civilian liaison, 2014–2019; candidate for Congress in New York's 12th district).

## TL;DR

- **$2.5M spent opposing Bores by a super PAC partially funded by Palantir and OpenAI co-founders, with potential to reach $10M** — the "Leading the Future" PAC was explicitly formed to attack legislators who support AI regulation, as acknowledged in their own public statements.
- **The Raise Act passed in 2025, requiring public safety plans, third-party audits, and mandatory reporting of critical AI incidents** — Klein notes it is "the strongest AI safety bill in the country," which embarrasses him given how soft it is compared to what's needed.
- **Bores worked at Palantir 2014–2019 and left over the ICE contract controversy** — he refused to work on civil immigration projects and was empowered to block them; later, executives declined to add contractual guardrails against deportation system use, prompting his departure. He was later attacked by Palantir executives using a Bloomberg article alleging sexual harassment, which he disputes and says was politically motivated retaliation.
- **Bores's AI regulatory framework includes 43 distinct policy ideas, including AI dividends (funded via wealth tax, token tax, or government warrants in AI companies), mandatory pedagogy updates for schools, age verification for certain AI interactions, and government capacity building on AI expertise** — he proposes using private capital's demand for data center buildout to modernize the grid via incentive-based regulatory requirements.
- **Klein argues that while companies make specific claims about AI harms (50% job loss for entry-level white-collar workers, autonomous vehicles replacing taxi drivers, existential risk), their promises about benefits are vague ("solve drug discovery," "create abundance")** — Bores agrees citizens are right to be skeptical, noting a 2014 tech-optimism era has given way to 80% of Americans wanting benefits and some regulation, with only 10% wanting either full pause or unconstrained development.
- **Public opinion on AI has shifted faster than legislation:** surveys show bipartisan concern (unusual for polarized issues), yet super PACs funded by AI founders are now defining the political economy of regulation, creating a scenario where supporting AI governance risks career destruction from well-funded opposition.
- **Bores skeptically supports autonomous vehicles but not yet in NYC:** Whimo tests failed in light rain (pulled over and froze, relying on human support), suggesting the "ready now" narrative is premature. He calls for graduated permits with high fees to incentivize grid upgrades and worker transition support, rejecting simple yes/no framing in favor of speed-of-transition questions.
- **A positive public AI agenda, not just harm reduction, requires government investment in infrastructure, research, and modernization** — Bores cites Empire AI (New York's GPU cluster for public universities), IRS tax prep automation, and streamlined drug discovery as examples of what government direction could unlock, but notes current systems (mainframe-dependent state infrastructure, fragmented databases, paper-based processes) lack the foundational capacity to benefit from AI.

**The Super PAC Attack and Palantir's Reversal**

Klein opens by describing a coordinated attack on Bores in New York's 12th congressional district from a super PAC called "Leading the Future," funded by Palantir co-founder Joe Lonsdale, OpenAI co-founders Greg Brockman and Andre Horowitz, and others. The attack falsely claims Bores built tech enabling ICE deportations and profited substantially. The attack interests Klein not because of its accuracy (Bores did work at Palantir, but the deportation claim is misleading) but because of who's funding it: Palantir and OpenAI co-founders are bankrolling attacks against a politician specifically for proposing AI regulation. Klein notes the principle is what matters—"It is important that the democratic process remains more powerful than companies"—yet the very founders claiming to believe this are funding a super PAC designed to ensure it is not.

Bores explains that Leading the Future is dedicated to destroying anyone who might regulate tech or AI in ways the founders dislike. The Raise Act—which Bores co-authored and passed in 2025—is one of the first major AI safety bills in any state. Klein notes with dark humor that the super PAC is attacking Bores partly on the grounds that he once worked at Palantir, the very company funding the attack against him. Bores adds that executives at Palantir are upset he is pushing for AI regulation and have called out the company's past practices. He has been an effective legislator: in just over three years in the New York State Assembly, he has passed 30 bills and was recognized by the Center for Effective Lawmaking as one of the most effective freshman legislators.

**From Disney Picket Lines to Palantier: A Political Education**

Bores's politics began in second grade when his father, a Disney graphics and videotape technician, was locked out for over a year during a contract dispute. Disney cut off healthcare benefits, including for a colleague about to begin chemotherapy; the union stepped in and paid for treatment. Bores's father brought him to the picket line, teaching him both that healthcare must be universal and that collective action is necessary to change power dynamics. Alone, a worker is crushed; with a union or movement, one has a chance.

At Cornell, Bores studied industrial labor relations and computer science—disciplines that seemed different but increasingly intertwined. At ILR, he learned economic theory, collective bargaining, and campaign strategy focused on defending workers. He ran a successful campaign against Nike, leveraging the university's code of conduct to pressure Nike to make amends to 1,800 workers laid off in Honduras without severance. The strategy: clear demands, education through teach-ins and creative actions, escalation to force administrative response. Nike capitulated within three weeks of Cornell's contract cancellation, paying workers in full and providing job training and healthcare.

Bores wanted to be a lawyer but was discouraged; instead, he worked at Cornerstone Research, an economic litigation consulting firm where he developed data modeling and analysis skills while staying close to legal work. By the time he encountered Palantier in 2014 under the Obama administration, he had embraced expanding government capacity while protecting privacy. Palantier's pitch resonated: co-founder Alex Karp, a German philosophy student fearful of fascism, wanted to build systems allowing government to keep people safe while protecting civil liberties. The theory was that fascism emerges when government fails to provide for citizens; Palantier could solve that failure while safeguarding rights.

**The Palantir Years: From Optimism to ICE**

Bores worked at Palantier 2014 to 2019, initially as a data scientist and eventually leading federal civilian relationships. He focused on the Department of Justice, CDC (for epidemic tracking), and Veterans Affairs (hospital staffing). The company was aggressively anti-AI at the time, believing data integration—not machine learning—was the true source of value. Organizing and connecting disparate data required hard work; AI was seen as marketing layered on top of the real work.

The transition from the Obama DOJ under Loretta Lynch to the Trump DOJ under Jeff Sessions changed priorities drastically. Bores, as project lead, had the authority to choose three mutually agreed-upon case types. When the new leadership proposed the opioid epidemic (great), violent crime (acceptable if not a dog whistle), and civil immigration (unacceptable), Bores refused the third. He was empowered by his contract to do so, and while leading the DOJ project, Palantier did not work on civil immigration.

However, the company signed a separate contract with HSI (Homeland Security Investigations), part of ICE. Historically, HSI focused on antihuman trafficking and counterfeiting under Obama. Under Trump, the administration tried to shift the work toward ERO (Enforcement and Removal Operations)—the deportation apparatus. Internal conversations ensued about what was happening, but not all employees could see classified projects. A fundamental question arose: why not write protective language into the contract, as Palantier had elsewhere, forbidding use for deportations? Executives made clear they would not add those guardrails and would renew the contract without them. Bores made plans to quit.

A Bloomberg article shortly before his departure alleged that Bores had made "sexually explicit comments" to a coworker and that his exit interview revealed burnout and excessive travel as his real reasons for leaving. Bores categorically disputes this. He says he expressed concerns about the ICE work internally, began interviewing months earlier, and had an offer in hand. He retold a story (about a tissues manufacturer's odd uses of tissues during a company presentation) that someone objected to; HR had one conversation with him, and that was the end. He was not disciplined, no letter was written. Multiple current Palantier employees backed his account of opposing ICE work. Bores notes the executives were upset about his pushing for AI regulation and his past criticism of the company. A year after he left, Palantier emailed and called him asking him to return. Bores sees the Bloomberg story as a desperate political hit, especially coming from a super PAC partially funded by Palantier co-founders.

**The Raise Act: Philosophy and Provisions**

In 2022, Bores was elected to the New York State Assembly. By 2025, he had co-authored and passed the Raise Act. When the bill was being developed, industry itself was warning about AI's risks: executives had signed a letter treating AI extinction risk as equivalent to nuclear war; many had made voluntary safety commitments with the Biden White House; yet no binding federal regulation followed. Companies also told Bores they were willing to embrace safety standards, but competitive pressure forced them to cut corners if competitors did. The solution: establish a regulatory baseline no one could fall below.

The final bill included three strong provisions. First, companies must create a public safety plan following industry best practices on how they test for specific risks, record those tests, and act on the findings. Second, companies must report critical safety incidents to the government, with "critical" specifically defined to catch early warnings of failures that might suggest bigger problems ahead, even if no one has yet been harmed. Third, companies cannot release a model if it fails their own safety test—inspired by tobacco and fossil fuel companies that knew their products were harmful but continued selling them.

Two provisions were cut. One would have forbidden releasing a model that fails internal safety tests. The other would have required third-party audits—not by government, but by independent parties, similar to financial and security audits. Klein notes that in the current landscape, even this "strongest AI safety bill in the country" is embarrassingly soft.

**The Economics of AI: Dividends, UBI, and Transition**

Klein pushes Bores on the tension between proposing universal basic income (UBI) and the reality that AI job displacement will be graduated. A truck driver earning $80K–$120K annually suddenly receiving $37K in UBI while Klein, a podcaster, continues earning his podcast salary, is clearly worse off. Bores agrees. His framework addresses this through multiple mechanisms, not UBI alone. Changing tax policy to charge for AI use and discount hiring humans would slow transition and protect jobs. Investment in job retraining programs, while having a poor track record, should still be pursued. Licenses or permits requiring multi-year training could be grandfathered—a truck driver's CDL could be required for 5–10 more years, giving the worker time to convert training into equity.

On the broader question of full automation and human replacement, Bores is skeptical but takes the possibility seriously. He notes this is the first technology being built explicitly to replace all human labor and exceed humans at everything. The metric for success is how well AI mimics human labor and surpasses it. Government must take this seriously.

The AI dividend is an insurance policy. If massive job displacement occurs, how does society function? Bores proposes funding mechanisms: a wealth tax (previously discussed), a token tax on commercial AI use (effectively taxing AI output while subsidizing labor, shifting tax policy with AI rather than against it), or taking warrants in AI companies. Warrants—long-dated, out-of-the-money options—pay off only if companies become extremely valuable, meaning they replaced labor at scale. If the government locks in warrants now, VCs celebrate the public participating in upside; if it tries to implement them after success, it looks like seizing means of production.

**Data Centers as Regulatory Leverage**

Bores proposes using data center buildout demand as an opportunity to modernize the electrical grid. AI companies are desperate for speed and have nearly unlimited private capital. If regulation requires that a certain percentage of power be renewable and that companies pay not just for grid connections but for grid resilience improvements elsewhere, with preferred interconnection queue positions for compliant operators, then incentives align. Dirty operators get pushed to the back of the queue. Klein notes his long skepticism about promised grid modernization—the Smart Grid was promised in 2009; the Inflation Reduction Act promised upgrades—yet the grid remains archaic. His cynicism is warranted, Bores concedes, but the difference now is private capital willing to move fast; precise regulation can redirect that speed toward public benefit.

**Autonomous Vehicles: Speed vs. Readiness**

Klein raises the Whimo (autonomous vehicle) question facing New York City: extend testing permits and accelerate deployment, or drag feet and keep them out? Bores insists there is a speed question, not a yes/no one. Most Americans want some benefits but think deployment is moving too fast. He raises practical concerns: Whimo failed in LA's light rain, freezing and relying on human support operators. Requiring human backup suggests a different kind of labor displacement than advertised—outsourcing the hard cases to remote workers. Whimo is not ready for NYC's environment. It will be eventually, but timing matters. The benefit (safer roads, better mobility for people with disabilities, no drowsy drivers) exists, but transition policy matters.

**Kids, Pedagogy, and Writing Skills**

On AI and children, Bores sees both risk and opportunity. A personalized tutor at a student's learning level could supplement classroom teaching and parental support—but parents and teachers must have visibility into all interactions, and strong data protection is essential. Broader mental health impacts and unnecessary products (like ChatGPT-powered teddy bears) require scrutiny. His framework calls for age verification for certain interactions, mental health monitoring, pedagogy updates, parental/teacher visibility into AI interactions, strong data privacy, and gradual exposure in high school and college so students understand the tools they will use.

On pedagogy specifically: essays can still be assigned, but take-home essays now go through ChatGPT (every hand went up when Bores asked high school students in Albany). Teachers should require essays written by hand or in Google Docs (traceable keystrokes), updating the teaching method for the new context. Klein notes that writing skill has visibly declined in applicant pools over recent years, and that writing is how people strengthen ideas. The work of writing is the work of thinking.

**Government Capacity and Public Investment in AI**

Bores emphasizes that AI has been developed almost entirely in the private sector—unlike the internet (DARPA developed the precursor) or the space race (government-led). Government needs expertise to govern well. Yet federal and state hiring practices cannot compete with tech-sector salaries. Solution: make government hiring easier, pay more, and prioritize AI expertise the way states fund football coaches. Bores pushed his first major bill to move New York off mainframes to cloud computing; the Assembly Speaker codes in Fortran, and state systems still run on languages from decades past. Modernization is foundational infrastructure work.

Klein points to the Anthropic-DOD controversy: companies are creating "cyber super weapons" that government must oversee, yet government involvement itself risks turning these systems into government tools used for any administration's purposes. Both private control and government control are uncomfortable. Bores says Congress needs to settle this via legislation. If superintelligence is likely (which Bores takes as given), the question of where such power lies is too important to leave to executive branch whims or private-company autonomy.

**A Positive Agenda for AI: Empire AI and Tax Automation**

Klein shifts to the question of positive AI vision, not just harm reduction. If AI will advance, the case for moving forward requires benefits to materialize, not just promises. Bores highlights Empire AI, New York's state-funded GPU cluster offered to public universities for cheaper research access. The federal government should scale this, directing grants toward AI safety, alignment, and drug discovery—domains where public investment can yield public goods. Klein notes that the clearest case is drug discovery: if AI generates new molecular candidates faster, the bottlenecks shift from discovery to preclinical testing and FDA approval. Using AI's economic pressure to unblock those systems could multiply benefits.

Bores agrees. He scanned all New York State statutes through an LLM to identify obsolete laws requiring paper where digital alternatives exist. He drafted a 60-page bill cutting regulatory cruft. The same approach applies to drug discovery: identify choke points and alleviate them. Klein offers a concrete example: the IRS could build an AI tax-prep system allowing Americans to upload forms and instantly get prepared taxes, cross-checked against IRS data. This is technically trivial but blocked by tax-software lobbying. It would give every American an "AI accountant."

Bores notes he has pushed a bill to let different state agencies share data citizens provide for benefit enrollment, so signing up for one benefit auto-qualifies for another. When he spoke with New York's tax department about free e-filing for New Yorkers, they said their data is too corrupted to automate safely. It may take five years to clean. That brings it back to data integration—the unsexy, foundational work Palantier always emphasized. Bores concurs that the technical capability for automated tax prep might not be the limiting factor; the underlying data accuracy is.

**The Political Economy of AI Regulation**

Klein concludes with a meta-point: at the exact moment Congress should be constructing regulatory structures, the AI industry has accumulated enough capital to function like a political death star. Crypto did the same. Anthropic, notably, funds a pro-regulation super PAC supporting Bores, so there is some division. But the broader worry is that to regulate AI, you may need to sign up your own AI patron. Leading the Future's explicit goal is to extract so much political pain in Bores's race that future politicians run from AI regulation. They want to make him an example.

Bores fires back: they are coming after him because he passed a bill, not because his ideas are fringe. He got praise from the left and a retweet from OpenAI's chief futurist. The company and funded policy documents that echo pieces of his framework. But frameworks are cheap; political capital and execution are rare. The Trump administration issued an executive order targeting states that regulate AI, including the Raise Act. Why? Because Bores might actually get something else done.

**Books**

Bores recommends three books. First, *A Theory of Justice* by John Rawls—the best framework for understanding rights and justified inequalities; Klein notes Rawls suggests reading a third of the book for basics, half for depth, and skipping the rest. Second, *World Eaters* by Katherine Boyle—marketed as anti-VC but actually a nuanced exploration of venture capital's "growth at all costs" imperative and the negative externalities it creates, especially relevant as AI scales. Third, *Bird by Bird* by Anne Lamott—a reminder to break down overwhelming work into smaller pieces, and a model of good writing in an era when writing skill is degrading.

# Death, Sex & Money — "Rick Steves Says Travel is the Antidote to Fear"

**Host:** Anna Sale. **Guest:** Rick Steves (travel writer, TV host, activist, author of *On the Hippie Trail: Istanbul to Kathmandu and the Making of a Travel Writer*; founder and sole proprietor of Rick Steves' Europe; advocate for marijuana legalization and criminal justice reform).

## TL;DR

- **Travel is the antidote to fear-based isolation** — Rick argues that "the most frightened people in our society are the people buried deep in the middle of it with no passports, whose worldview is shaped by fear-mongering commercial TV news." Understanding comes from travel, which flips fear into empathy.
- **His 2008 Iran documentary was preventive humanization** — Rick traveled to Iran during saber-rattling over the potential for war, creating a TV special showing Iranians as regular people with families, art, and beauty. When war broke out in 2026, he thought: "I think it's kind of good style to know people before you bomb them."
- **The 1953 US-backed coup explains current Iranian theocracy** — The US and Britain overthrew democratically elected Mossadegh to control Iranian oil, installed the Shah, and sparked cultural backlash that led to the 1979 Islamic Revolution. Understanding this context is essential to grasping why ordinary Iranians supported a theocratic government.
- **Iranians rejected Western hedonism to protect their children** — Fear of cultural degradation—"our little girls being raised like Britney Spears"—motivated mothers to support the Islamic Revolution, not political oppression alone. Rick emphasizes: "They're worried about the ethics and the morality of their children."
- **Americans are 4% of the planet but think they're exceptional** — Travel reveals that exceptionalism is a delusion shared by all large cultures and nations. Understanding the "other 96%" is necessary for wisdom and humility.
- **Central America taught Rick that travel complicates political views** — Visiting El Salvador and Nicaragua during civil wars in the 1980s exposed him to Reagan-era propaganda and "American greed and economic policy and military policy and colonialism," forcing him to reassess his blind trust in government.
- **Rick channels travel lessons into ambitious philanthropy** — Over millions in donations, he's funded a 25-unit apartment building for abused women (later gifted to the YWCA), purchased a $2+ million hygiene center for 800 homeless people, and spent ~$1 million on marijuana legalization advocacy—all rooted in seeing European harm-reduction approaches work.
- **Solitude and faith sustain him on the road** — Rick spends most of his birthdays overseas alone, embracing "me time" and finding companionship through paid guides and his Christian faith. He views the road as "school, playground, or church," sometimes expressing his Lutheran faith evangelically in nature.

**The Immediate Stakes: War, Knowledge, and Humanization**

Rick opens the conversation by reflecting on the timing of his 2008 Iran special—made when he feared American military action, inspired partly by watching Iraq's cultural heritage threatened by bombing. He traveled to Iran to "humanize a country of 70 million people," knowing it might otherwise be destroyed before Americans understood who lived there. When bombs fell in 2026, he woke thinking: "I made that show because I was afraid Iran would be bombed like Baghdad was being bombed."

The episode touches on the human cost of war and the psychological distance that allows civilians to dismiss casualties as "collateral damage." Rick argues that if 150 schoolgirls were killed in America, "life would grind to a halt," but the same bombing in Iran becomes a statistic. His central plea: people need to know who they're bombing. He deliberately avoids contacting his guides and friends in Iran, Palestine, and Russia to protect them from being associated with an American—a reminder of how geopolitics creates dangerous isolation even between former friends.

**Understanding Iran: Colonialism, Cultural Backlash, and the 1953 Coup**

Rick traces the roots of Iran's theocratic government to Western intervention. In 1953, the US and Britain overthrew Mohammad Mossadegh, Iran's democratically elected leader who sought to nationalize Iranian oil. "We put in a puppet, a guy who played ball with us on our terms"—the Shah, whose Western orientation created a cultural rift. The Shah was popular among cosmopolitan urban Iranians with European connections but deeply offensive to rural, traditional Iranians who saw Western values (including shorter miniskirts in Tehran than Paris) as a moral threat.

Rick connects this to contemporary family values. Iranian mothers supported the Islamic Revolution not primarily from political conviction but from fear that Western hedonism would corrupt their daughters. "I want you to go home and tell the truth. We're strong, we're united, and we just don't want our little girls to be raised like Britney Spears," one woman told him. Rick emphasizes that understanding these motivations—rooted in love and protection, not mere fundamentalism—is essential for Americans to grasp Iran's political reality. Cultural heritage also matters: Rick was shocked by the sparseness of Iran's National Museum, learning that Persia's greatest artifacts had been taken to European museums. Colonialism, both cultural and political, shapes Iranian consciousness.

**The Exceptionalism Trap: What Travel Reveals**

A core theme animates Rick's work: Americans believe they are exceptional, but travel reveals this as a universal delusion. "The only thing exceptional about Americans is our ability to think that in God's eyes we could be exceptional," he says. Large countries and cultures are naturally ethnocentric; smaller nations know they're "just one of a lot of different groups." Travel to the "other 96%" of the planet punctures this bubble.

Rick recalls Thomas Jefferson's insight: "Travel makes a person wiser, if less happy." He frames this as a choice—do you really want to "complicate your life by turning over this log and looking what's under it?" Many people don't. They prefer the comfort of their existing worldview, which is why safety fears are often really about fear of worldview disruption. When his father told him "don't be duped" before a trip to El Salvador in the 1980s, he was essentially saying: don't mess with the propaganda you've absorbed.

**Central America and Political Awakening**

Rick's trips to El Salvador and Nicaragua during the civil wars transformed his political consciousness. He voted for Ronald Reagan believing in "peace through strength" and trusted his government blindly. But traveling to the barrio, sleeping outside barns with displaced families, and hearing local radio broadcasts—"a mobile radio station that moves from place to place in the mountains so they won't be discovered and killed by government troops"—opened his eyes to "American greed and economic policy and military policy and colonialism." These weren't abstract concepts; they were lived realities of people he met.

He emphasizes that he didn't assess the situation "from the rooftop garden of an intercontinental hotel" but by getting out and living among communities affected by US-backed conflict. This experience became the template for his later work: travel as political and moral education.

**Dismantling Travel Anxiety: Embracing Discomfort**

Rick recalls his 1978 hippie trail journey as a coming-of-age "hajj"—interpreting the Islamic pillar not just as traveling to Mecca but as "leaving home and getting to know the world," celebrating "God's great creation" and its diversity. He kept a meticulous 60,000-word journal, documenting the trip with photos, setting the foundation for his later teaching.

A turning point in his pedagogy came when he realized he was inflicting discomfort too harshly on early tour groups. He'd deliberately keep accommodations uncertain, creating "the specter of homelessness" and stress to force cultural shock. After finding a young woman shivering in a terrible Italian hotel, too anxious to tell him she was suffering, Rick realized: "You don't hit somebody on the head with a broader perspective. You introduce them in a comfortable way a broader perspective. You guide them to it, maybe. You invite them to it."

He experimented with silence and ritual—taking groups to Taizé, a monastic community where people confronted chanting, icons, and enforced quiet (challenging for Americans). Half loved it; half hated it. But the point stuck: discomfort curated thoughtfully, not brutally, expands perspective. The mark of a good traveler is recognizing anxieties and proactively tackling them.

**Fear, Safety, and the Shift in Language**

Rick observes a linguistic and psychological shift: Americans used to say "bon voyage," but now say "have a safe trip." He interprets this not simply as concern about physical danger but as a subconscious message: "Don't rearrange your political sensibilities. Don't change. You're going to be all messed up." Statistically, he notes, travel is safer now than when the old greeting was common, yet anxiety has grown—a sign of fear-mongering media creating "synthetic intelligence in our society...making us all afraid" because "we're easier to shape and manipulate when we're fearful."

Rick acknowledges that travelers are anxious about tangible things—water safety, tipping customs, political reactions to their government—but he finds the deeper fear is vulnerability itself. Anna Sale adds her own insight: travel anxiety isn't just about physical danger but about being visibly clumsy or out of place. Rick celebrates clumsiness: pushing instead of pulling at a door, shaking your head wrong in Bulgaria, wearing a goofy Nepali hat. "Let them, it's like going to show and tell...It's sharing," not a competition of superiority.

**Solitude, Faith, and the Road as Sanctuary**

Rick reveals that he's comfortable with solitude and has spent almost every birthday for 30 years overseas, alone. He's "really good at saying goodbye" and loves returning home to Edmonds, Washington (where he can see his junior high school from his window), but he embraces the "me time" of traveling solo. He distinguishes this from loneliness: he hires guides to be companions, attends to his work, and finds fellowship through his Christian faith. "I can walk through a teeming hive of people in Istanbul, and I'm not alone... I've got a friend in Jesus."

Nature and art trigger transcendence for Rick. In the Alps, on a ridge between lakes stretching to Germany and peaks like the Agra Monk and Jungfrau, he experiences what he calls an "evangelical" moment—an expressive, emotional connection to the divine despite being Lutheran (a tradition of restraint). He's evolved from childhood church attendance to adolescent skepticism to adult faith as a chosen dimension of his life. "I see God in laughter. I see God in tears. I see God in nature. I see God in art. I taste God in beautiful food and wine."

**The Philosophy of Vicarious Consumption and Stewardship**

Rick's approach to wealth is rooted in recognizing diminishing returns on personal consumption. Beyond meeting basic needs, buying more—yachts, luxury—doesn't increase happiness; it complicates life. Instead, he practices "vicarious consumption," investing surplus wealth into people who are at the steep part of the diminishing-return curve, where a shower or a safe bed transforms their world.

He traces this realization to his early career, when he had $100,000 saved for retirement and realized it was "just sitting there." He bought duplexes to house homeless families, eventually purchasing a 25-unit apartment building filled by the YWCA with abused women and children escaping domestic violence. He retained ownership but felt no need to reclaim it and eventually gifted it outright. This became his template for "entrepreneurial philanthropy"—challenging other modest investors to put nest eggs into affordable housing rather than banks.

**Travel Lessons Become Advocacy: Marijuana, Mass Incarceration, and Harm Reduction**

Rick's most visible philanthropic cause—spending ~$1 million on marijuana legalization—stems directly from his travels in Europe, where cannabis is treated as a health and education issue, not a criminal one. Prisons and lawyers vanish; doctors and counselors appear. He sees this as pragmatic harm reduction tied to three threads: civil liberties, racial justice (marijuana prohibition is racist), and simple effectiveness.

Similarly, his opposition to mass incarceration comes from European contrasts. "You need judges and you need lawyers and you need prison, cops" in America; "in Europe, you need doctors, you need counselors, you need a helping hand." Rick is explicit: he's not pro-drugs; he's against counterproductive prohibition that empowers organized crime and destroys lives. He's unafraid to alienate customers who disagree ("Europe's going to be more fun without you"), viewing honesty about values as non-negotiable even if it costs money.

His recent hire of a philanthropic coordinator revealed a consistency in 30 years of "haphazard" giving: all donations reflect travel-informed sensibilities about human dignity, community care, and systemic harm reduction. His advocacy and giving are in lockstep.

**Recent Tangible Impact: The Linwood Hygiene Center**

In late 2025, Rick learned that Seattle's Linwood Hygiene Center—serving 800+ homeless and low-income people with showers, haircuts, and meals—was closing. He bought the center for over $2 million to keep it open. This exemplifies his philosophy: a shower is transformative for someone experiencing homelessness; the investment yields outsized human benefit compared to any luxury he might buy.

Rick frames this as a direct echo of his travels. He's experienced hunger and deprivation—begging a Serbian stranger for bread while surrounded by others eating well—enough to understand the ache of scarcity. He slept outside barns in El Salvador and Nicaragua, witnessing communities needing basic infrastructure (a community center, a visiting doctor, a government service) to be empowered. "These are the souvenirs that I get from my travels," he says, and he wants to model that others with modest wealth can do the same.

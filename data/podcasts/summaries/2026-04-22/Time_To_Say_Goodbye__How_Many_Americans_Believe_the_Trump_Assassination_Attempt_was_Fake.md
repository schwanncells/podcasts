# Time to Say Goodbye — "How Many Americans Believe the Trump Assassination Attempt was Fake?"

**Host:** Tyler Austin Harper. **Guest:** Jay Stillman (journalist, UC Berkeley J-School faculty, opinion columnist).

- **Congress is full of sexual predators:** Eric Swalwell resigned after sexual assault allegations; Democratic leadership knew but said nothing. Both parties are essentially trading "perverts" like baseball cards so the math works politically—internal complaints exist but transparency is blocked.
- **Swalwell exemplifies empty-suit resistance Democrats:** He became prominent through cable news outrage about Trump, delivered nothing substantive, then was revealed as a predator. The class of performative resistance politicians is now on the ropes.

Anomalies fuel it: shooter had zero internet footprint, Secret Service spotted him but did nothing until after the shot, cheap rifle with unmagnified sight couldn't reliably shoot an ear at 150 yards.
- **Conspiracy becomes normalized:** When half the country distrusts institutions and half believes in election fraud, staged assassinations, and Congress-led sex trafficking, democracy fractures. Trust cannot be recovered with existing institutions.
- **Democrats may embrace aggressive consolidation:** James Carville reportedly said Democrats will impeach Thomas and Alito, pack the court, grant statehood to Puerto Rico and D.C., and lock in structural dominance—framed as fighting back against Republican norm-breaking.

**The Collapse of Higher Education as an Institution**

The episode opens with discussion of Ivy Day and the anxiety-driven social media culture around college admissions. Stillman notes that the emotional weight of these acceptances and rejections creates a "punishment spectacle" where students who've been prepared obsessively since age 10 face very public failure. The hosts observe that this reflects a deeper crisis: the college degree as a "ticket to a better life" has collapsed. The loans are no longer worth it for many students.

Stillman, who taught at UC Berkeley's journalism school, describes a profession in acute crisis. Large swaths of higher education have become either automated (like computer science, where AI now does the actual programming) or irreproducible (half the social sciences don't replicate, yet churn along as "zombified" fields that can't be killed because they attract tuition-paying students). He explains the impossible position faculty now face: students arrive underprepared to do sustained reading; professors can either do massive extra work to bring them up to standard, accept drastically lowered standards, or look the other way while students use AI to cheat. Without additional pay or support from administration, many choose to "meet students where they are"—which amounts to abandoning standards.

The core problem is structural. Administrators push AI-grading and expand enrollment counts precisely because they want to shrink faculty costs. When a professor tries to fail a student for clear cheating, a "student success bureaucrat" will negotiate them down to a withdrawal, which then disappears from the transcript if the student takes five classes the next semester. It is nearly impossible to fail anyone. This isn't softness—it's driven by the fact that time-to-degree is a major metric in U.S. News rankings, which determines donor funding. The entire system is rigged to hide failure.

**Congressional Corruption and the "Pervert Horse Trade"**

Eric Swalwell, a Bay Area congressman who represented the Asian-dominated suburbs south of Oakland, became a national figure as a "resistance lib"—frequently on cable news denouncing Trump. He ran for president, then shifted to running for California governor. Then sexual assault allegations from multiple women emerged. Swalwell posted a defensive video ("This is between me and my wife") before resigning from Congress within 12 hours. Democrats forced him out.

The hosts find the broader pattern more scandalous than even Swalwell's conduct. Congress is "riddled" with sexual predators, both Democrats and Republicans. Internal complaints exist and are documented, but legislators have blocked efforts to make these inquiries public. The effect is a system where the two parties essentially trade sex offenders back and forth to keep the numbers balanced—"pervert horse trading" where two of theirs cancel two of ours. As one host puts it, everyone already knew about these people; silence was a deliberate choice to avoid scandal. Some journalists have been bragging ex-post that they "knew" about Swalwell since 2013 but never reported it, using that knowledge as proof of insider status rather than as a reason to investigate.

Swalwell is emblematic of a broader class of centrist Democratic politicians—Cory Booker, Ruben Gallego, and others—whom the hosts see as pure vehicles for personal ambition. They have no core beliefs, just poll-tested positions. They fail constantly yet "fail up" into higher office. When their ambitions finally exceed their utility, they're exposed and discarded. The hosts believe this class of performative resistance Democrats, who talked big about fighting Trump but delivered nothing, is now losing all credibility.

**The Trump Assassination Conspiracy and Epistemological Crisis**

On April 22, Joyce Carol Oates tweeted skepticism about whether the Trump assassination attempt in Butler, Pennsylvania actually happened. The hosts assign it a rating on their "conspiracy truth scale" (1 = obviously wrong like Bigfoot, 10 = obviously true like moon landing being fake). Stillman rates it 4.5; Harper rates it 3.5 to 4. Both think it's unlikely but strange enough to merit scrutiny.

The factual oddities fueling the conspiracy are real: the shooter had zero internet presence—unusual for any teenager in 2026. He was spotted on the rooftop for an extended period; people yelled at Secret Service, but nothing happened until after the shot. The rifle was a cheap AR-15 with an unmagnified red dot and a 16-inch barrel, not a precision weapon. At 150 yards, such a setup would typically group 4–6 inches, making a pinpoint ear shot extraordinarily difficult, let alone a controlled one that would allow Trump to raise his fist and shout "Fight" while a photographer captured the perfect image.

Harper stresses the ballistics implausibility: if this was a real attempt, the perpetrator would have chosen a much better rifle. If it was a staged shot meant to hit the ear, the logistics are absurdly difficult. The conspiracy is more plausible to many Americans because the basic facts are genuinely odd and the government has conducted no credible public investigation. As the conspiracy spreads virally and algorithmic amplification rewards controversy, more people adopt it.

Stillman notes that the real danger is epistemological: when millions of Americans—not just fringe figures but educated people like Joyce Carol Oates and prominent comedians—believe the president staged his own assassination, the country has entered unrecoverable territory. He and Harper argue about whether the word "conspiracy" is even appropriate; Stillman contends that "conspiracy" is a media-invented term designed to preemptively delegitimize any claim that challenges official narratives. When the Epstein files revealed that powerful people across Wall Street, elite universities, and the White House were "cozy" with a sex trafficker, calling that a "conspiracy" frames it as a debunked theory rather than a documented crime network.

The hosts identify a deeper problem: mainstream journalists know things (about predators, about incompetence) but trade silence for access. They attack people selectively based on political alignment, not based on evidence. This feeds the public's sense that institutions are lying systematically. Once that trust is broken, the public won't believe official denials even when they're true.

**Democracy in Freefall**

Both hosts express despair about institutional recovery. Stillman sees no unifying vision being offered by either party. He believes the next 10 years will be decisive: if Democrats don't aggressively break up AI companies, nationalize key infrastructure, and legislate against deepfakes and surveillance, the country will move into "a very, very dark place" where reality itself becomes uncaptureable. Harper notes that at least some recent Democratic messaging—James Carville's reported call to impeach Supreme Court justices, pack the court, and grant statehood to Puerto Rico and D.C.—articulates a vision of fighting back, even if it's "blue fascism."

The hosts note that large percentages of educated Americans (they estimate ~75% in liberal neighborhoods) would not be shocked if the assassination was real. When that is the baseline, when half the country operates from a different factual reality, and when Congress is known to harbor untouched predators, cynicism becomes rational. The problem is that cynicism of that magnitude is corrosive to democratic function. It cannot be undone by electing "better" people because the structural incentives that produce this corruption remain in place.

**Media Credibility and Access Journalism**

Harper, who writes opinion columns and occasionally texts with politicians, addresses the question of journalistic responsibility. He acknowledges that reporters can't always publish allegations without survivors willing to go on record; such a story risks legal destruction. But he sees a distinction between "I tried to investigate this and couldn't get corroboration" and "I knew this was true the whole time and stayed silent to preserve access." The sports media do this routinely: when a coach or GM is fired, beat reporters dump everything they previously knew about misconduct, clearly having traded silence for daily access.

Stillman argues that the real issue is that institutions select for people without core beliefs—pure ambition. That inevitably produces a higher rate of predators and abusers. The selection mechanism is invisible but powerful: people who are willing to compromise their stated principles, who calculate costs and benefits, and who chase power regardless of the stated reason for wanting it, are the ones who rise. That's who gets "foisted on us" by Democratic leadership.

Both hosts conclude that there is no good resolution on the horizon. The institutions that might impose accountability (Congress, media, courts) are themselves compromised. Cynicism is widespread and justified. And the window to address the deepest threat—AI-enabled reality distortion—is rapidly closing.

# The Indicator from Planet Money — "The Iran war puts the petrodollar regime to the test"

**Hosts:** Waylon Wong, Adrian Ma. **Guest:** David White (historian, University of North Carolina, Greensboro; author on U.S.-Middle East relations and the petrodollar system).

## TL;DR

- **The petrodollar system was born from the 1973 oil embargo.** After the U.S. supported Israel in the Yom Kippur War, Arab countries imposed an oil embargo that triggered a global oil shock and quadrupled oil prices in late 1973.
- **Saudi Arabia agreed to price oil in U.S. dollars in exchange for military and economic support.** Treasury Secretary Bill Simon and Secretary of State Henry Kissinger negotiated an arrangement formalized in June 1974; the specific terms are not publicly documented, but the U.S. pushed Saudi Arabia to invest heavily in U.S. Treasury bonds.
- **Saudi Arabia became the largest foreign buyer of U.S. Treasuries—accounting for 12% of the federal deficit between 1974 and 1976.** This "petrodollar recycling" was a major influx of foreign capital into the U.S. financial system.
- **The petrodollar regime has remained durable for 50 years,** underpinning the U.S. dollar's status as the world's reserve currency. Oil-producing countries peg their currencies to the dollar and invest most overseas assets in dollars.
- **China is actively challenging the petrodollar regime.** In 2023, China and Saudi Arabia signed a $7 billion cooperation agreement and are expanding trade priced in Chinese yuan rather than dollars, targeting the foundation of dollar reserve status.
- **If the petrodollar system collapses, the cost of living for Americans will rise.** Imports would become more expensive (increasing inflation), and without foreign Treasury purchases, U.S. government borrowing costs would increase, raising interest rates on mortgages and auto loans.
- **Iran is testing the system with yuan-denominated tolls in the Strait of Hormuz,** suggesting the petrodollar regime may be vulnerable to pressure from geopolitically unstable regions.

**The 1973 Oil Crisis and the Birth of the Petrodollar**

In October 1973, Egypt and Syria attacked Israel during the Yom Kippur War. The U.S., under President Richard Nixon, provided military aid to Israel. Arab oil-producing countries retaliated by imposing an embargo on U.S. oil imports, triggering the first global oil shock. Oil prices quadrupled in the final months of 1973, creating severe shortages in the United States.

This crisis forced the U.S. to rebuild its relationship with Saudi Arabia, the world's largest oil exporter. White notes that both sides had reasons to distrust each other—the Saudis were angry about U.S. support for Israel, but they also relied on the U.S. for security guarantees, investment opportunities, and business relationships. Both nations faced "a rupture point," but declassified documents show mutual motivation to patch things up.

**Negotiating the 1974 Agreement**

Treasury Secretary Bill Simon and Secretary of State Henry Kissinger traveled to Saudi Arabia in 1974 to negotiate what would become the petrodollar system. The arrangement was straightforward: Saudi Arabia would price oil exports in U.S. dollars, and the U.S. would provide military and economic support in return. While historians debate whether the U.S. offered a discount on Treasury bond purchases, the core bargain is clear: the U.S. pushed Saudi Arabia and other Arab oil-rich states to conduct business in dollars and to invest heavily in U.S. Treasury securities and the broader U.S. economy.

In June 1974, the U.S. and Saudi Arabia signed a formal agreement on military and economic cooperation with Henry Kissinger and the Saudi Minister of Commerce and Industry present. This formalized the petrodollar system, though the most sensitive details remain undisclosed.

**The Petrodollar System in Practice**

Though oil had been priced in dollars before 1974, the new arrangement locked Saudi Arabia and other regional producers into dollar-denominated commerce and dollar-based investment. Saudi Arabia became the largest foreign buyer of U.S. Treasuries. Between 1974 and 1976, its Treasury purchases alone accounted for 12% of the federal deficit—an enormous capital inflow that financed U.S. borrowing.

The flow of petrodollars declined in the 1980s as oil prices fell, but the broader regime endured. Oil-producing countries continued to peg their currencies to the dollar and held most of their overseas investments in dollar assets. This created a powerful incentive structure: these countries' financial health was tied to dollar stability, and the cost of dumping dollar assets in bulk would hurt them as much as it hurt the U.S.

**China's Yuan Offensive**

The durability of the petrodollar system has been tested before, but a new challenger has emerged. China, seeking to establish the yuan as an alternative reserve currency, is directly targeting the petrodollar regime. In 2023, China and Saudi Arabia signed a $7 billion economic cooperation agreement. China is now Saudi Arabia's largest trading partner and is pushing for more bilateral trade to be denominated in yuan rather than dollars. This represents a fundamental assault on the architectural foundation of dollar reserve status.

**Economic Consequences for Americans**

If the petrodollar system collapses, the consequences for American households would be immediate and painful. Without petrodollar recycling, foreign demand for U.S. Treasuries would decline, pushing up U.S. government borrowing costs and raising interest rates across the economy. This would make mortgages, auto loans, and other consumer credit more expensive. Additionally, a weaker dollar (the likely result of lost reserve currency status) would drive up the price of imports, triggering inflation on everything from electronics to clothing to fuel.

That said, White cautions that the transition is not simple. Countries holding large dollar reserves would suffer losses if they dumped them, and there is no obvious alternative to the dollar or U.S. Treasuries that offers the same liquidity and safety.

**Signs of Vulnerability**

Despite its 50-year track record, White argues the petrodollar regime is not sacrosanct. One recent development: Iran has reportedly begun collecting tolls in Chinese yuan for ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz, a critical chokepoint in global energy flows. While this move may be more symbolic than economically significant, it signals that oil-exporting nations—especially those at odds with the U.S.—are exploring alternatives if the U.S. proves to be an unreliable geopolitical partner.

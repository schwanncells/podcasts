# Code Switch — "With 'deed theft' scammers are targeting Black homeowners"

**Host:** Gene Demby. **Guests:** Chi Osei (New York City Council member, representing Bed-Stuy; activist involved in 2020 racial justice protests); David Brand (housing reporter, WNYC and Gothamist); Isatou Barry (law professor, Brooklyn Law School; runs Housing Justice Center clinic; housing attorney).

## TL;DR

- **Deed theft complaints rose 240% from 2023 to 2025** — over 500 complaints recorded in 2025 alone, with 3,500 total citywide from 2014 to 2023, concentrated in Brooklyn neighborhoods like Bed-Stuy and Crown Heights.
- **Scammers target homeowners in gentrifying Black neighborhoods** — central Brooklyn and southeast Queens, where Black middle-class families have owned homes for generations, are now experiencing rapid property value increases making them high-value targets.
- **Michael Baker's family lost a home owned since 1963** — after inheriting the property and struggling with back taxes, he signed what he thought was a financing deal but actually transferred the deed; the property changed hands multiple times and he was evicted despite having owned it for nearly 59 years.
- **Deed theft exploits legal ambiguity and document complexity** — once fraudulent deeds are filed in official property records, courts struggle to unwind transactions; additional liens from lenders and multiple subsequent sales create layers of legal defense that make recovery difficult.
- **Gentrification creates financial pressure and scammer opportunity** — homes worth a few thousand dollars in the 1960s now sell for $3 million; financial distress, elderly homeowners, and immigrants make families vulnerable to predatory offers from sophisticated investor rings.
- **Enforcement hasn't kept pace with the crime surge** — despite New York AG Letitia James making it a priority, prosecutors lack the manpower to investigate cases; shoplifting gets more media attention than people losing their homes.
- **Chi Osei was arrested during a deed theft eviction protest** — in April, he was arrested trying to stop a resident's eviction; New York Mayor Eric Adams created an Office of Deed Theft Prevention two days later.
- **Prevention strategies include wills, transfer-on-death deeds, and legal aid** — housing attorney Isatou Barry recommends estate planning to clarify intent; free legal services and family conversations about property succession are critical defenses.

**The Gentrification-Deed Theft Connection**

Gene Demby opens the episode by reflecting on his own childhood in South Philadelphia—a neighborhood he once felt embarrassed about, with boarded-up houses and dilapidated infrastructure. That same block has transformed dramatically: the little row house his family rented now sells for over $400,000 after renovations, the bandos are gone, and the neighborhood has been renamed to match gentrification marketing (Graduate Hospital or Southwest Center City). The same pattern appears across American cities where neighborhoods once deemed undesirable are now highly sought after. Bedford-Stuyvesant in Brooklyn, once the second-largest predominantly Black neighborhood in the country after Chicago's South Side, was synonymous with crime and poverty in the 1990s but now features brownstones selling for $3 million.

Council member Chi Osei explains that deed theft is exploiting this transformation. Neighborhoods like Bed-Stuy and Crown Heights, where Black middle-class homeowners—many descended from immigrants who bought during the redlining era when no one else wanted to live there—now face predatory scammers targeting the rising property values. The crime connects directly to gentrification: as neighborhoods become desirable, investor rings identify vulnerable homeowners and attempt to steal properties worth millions for fractions of their true value.

**How Deed Theft Schemes Work**

Chi Osei describes multiple tactics scammers use. The traditional method involves door-to-door solicitation or phone calls claiming to help pay off mortgages or finance repairs. Sometimes intermediaries use family connections. Scammers slip deed transfers into paperwork while homeowners believe they're signing loan documents or assistance agreements. The deception is sophisticated and systematic—investor rings study the city's legal system, identify financially distressed homeowners, and execute coordinated schemes.

David Brand reports that the scammers are typically not giant corporations but organized rings of investors with deep knowledge of property law, family disputes, and the city's recording system. They identify homeowners described as "house rich, cash poor"—people with valuable property but limited liquid assets who might be susceptible to offers of quick money. They also target elderly homeowners who die without wills, triggering family disputes over property that create exploitable confusion. Many victims are immigrants or seniors unfamiliar with property law.

**Michael Baker's 59-Year Loss**

David Brand highlights a case that exemplifies the scale of the problem. Michael Baker inherited a Brownsville property his parents bought in 1963 for $23,000. After his father died, Michael faced tens of thousands of dollars in unpaid property taxes. A contact suggested he sign papers to get a loan, claiming new owners in an LLC would help him pay the taxes while he retained occupancy. Instead, Michael signed over the deed. The person he trusted then flipped the property to another company for hundreds of thousands of dollars. The property changed hands multiple times; Michael was sued for eviction and lost. He was physically removed from his own home by city marshals and became homeless, losing not just the property but his record collection, furniture, and his ability to house family members including his niece and newborn grandnephew.

**The Labyrinth of Legal Defenses**

Once deed theft occurs, victims face an almost impossible legal battle. David Brand explains that once documents are filed in the city's official property record system, they gain a layer of legality that's extremely difficult to challenge. Courts ask victims to prove deception or fraud—to show they didn't knowingly sign documents. When subsequent purchasers and lenders get involved, claiming they made good-faith purchases or loans, the chain of ownership becomes nearly impossible to unwind. Lenders have financial stakes in defending the chain, prosecutors lack resources to investigate complex fraud cases, and judges struggle to apply common sense when legal documents appear in order.

The burden falls entirely on the victim. They must hire attorneys to prove they were scammed, while scammers hide behind layers of legal entities and subsequent innocent purchasers. Even when victims have video evidence or can prove deceptive tactics, the courts' focus on documentation—"the papers are in order, you signed them"—leaves little recourse.

**Scope and Targeting**

The scale of deed theft in New York City is substantial. From 2014 to 2023, the attorney general's office received 3,500 complaints; from 2023 to 2025, complaints increased 240%. In 2025 alone, over 500 complaints were recorded—the largest annual number in recent years. These numbers likely undercount the actual problem because many victims, especially elderly people and immigrants, don't realize they've been scammed or don't know how to report it.

David Brand notes the crime is concentrated in historically Black neighborhoods with significant generational homeownership. Bed-Stuy, Crown Heights, and southeast Queens have concentrations of beautiful, valuable properties owned by families who bought when redlining locked them into those neighborhoods. Now that cities are safer and neighborhoods desirable, those same properties attract scammers and predatory investors.

**Historical Context: Redlining to Deed Theft**

David Brand connects deed theft to a longer history of discrimination against Black homeowners. The neighborhoods where deed theft is most prevalent are the same ones that became predominantly Black because of redlining—neighborhoods where only Black people could get mortgages, where white investors avoided buying. Black families bought Bed-Stuy brownstones not out of preference but because those were the only neighborhoods available to them. Now, those same neighborhoods' soaring values make them targets for new predatory schemes.

The pattern reveals a cruel irony: families that sacrificed to build generational wealth in neighborhoods they were segregated into are now losing those assets through sophisticated fraud as those neighborhoods become valuable.

**Enforcement and Prevention Efforts**

New York AG Letitia James has prioritized deed theft enforcement. However, enforcement hasn't kept pace with the crime surge. Prosecutors lack sufficient manpower to investigate cases; each case requires detailed analysis of property records, financial transactions, and legal documents. David Brand observes that media coverage of shoplifting gets front-page treatment while people are losing multimillion-dollar homes with minimal enforcement response.

In April, Chi Osei was arrested while trying to stop the eviction of a resident he believed was a deed theft victim. The incident garnered attention and catalyzed action: Mayor Eric Adams created the Office of Deed Theft Prevention two days later. Chi emphasizes that stopping deed theft is his number-one priority as a council member, calling it "one of the most vile crimes in the city."

**Defense Strategies**

Isatou Barry, a housing attorney running the Housing Justice Center, outlines prevention and protection measures. First, homeowners should keep copies of their deeds and understand their property records. Second, transfer-on-death deeds allow homeowners to specify who inherits the property after death, creating a legal record of intent. Third, adding a name to the deed or mortgage creates a paper trail. Most importantly, families need to have difficult conversations about property succession and create wills or estate plans.

Barry emphasizes that free or low-cost legal aid is critical—homeowners in financial distress shouldn't have to turn to predatory lenders who then steal their homes. Estate planning services and legal assistance through nonprofits can prevent families from becoming vulnerable in the first place.

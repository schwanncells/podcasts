# The Economics of Everyday Things — "25. Private Jets"

**Host:** Zachary Crockett. **Guests:** Anthony Tivnan (president and co-founder, Magellan Jets — one of the nation's leading private jet brokers with 20+ years in the industry, serving ~1,200 clients across 80+ countries); Chuck Collins (program director, Institute for Policy Studies; great-grandson of Oscar Mayer; gave away his inherited trust fund at 26 and now focuses on wealth inequality and the harms of ultra-high-end consumption; member, Patriotic Millionaires).

## TL;DR

- **Private jet industry has more than doubled in 20 years** — 23,000+ private planes globally today, with 5.3 million flights last year; most are used by ultra-wealthy individuals and corporations.
- **Buying in ranges from $7M to $150M** — a light jet like the Phenom 300 runs $7–10M; a Gulfstream G650ER (Kim Kardashian's) costs $150M; ongoing expenses (fuel, hangar, pilots, maintenance) easily exceed $1M/year.
- **Charter costs $6,000–$20,000/hour** — depending on jet size; membership cards start at $100,000 for guaranteed access; fractional ownership splits costs among up to 32 shareholders, like "a timeshare for fancy airplanes."
- **Private jets use 16% of U.S. airspace but contribute only ~2% of FAA funding** — critics call this a de facto public subsidy for the ultra-wealthy; jet owners can pay as little as $0.22/gallon fuel tax vs. commercial travelers paying multiple stacked fees.
- **The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act lets jet owners deduct the full purchase price in year one** — the business-vs-pleasure line is easily blurred; one example cited: a pheasant hunting trip to Argentina classified as a business expense.
- **Private jets burn 10–20x more carbon per passenger than commercial flights** — Elon Musk's 171 flights last year burned 222,000 gallons of fuel, generating carbon emissions 200 times the average American's annual footprint.
- **41% of flights from one Boston-area airport were under an hour, 14% under 30 minutes** — Kylie Jenner's famous 17-minute L.A. flight would have taken 40 minutes by car; critics say many of these trips are avoidable.
- **Policy proposals range from fuel tax hikes to outright bans** — a pending U.S. bill would raise the private jet fuel tax from $0.22 to $1.95/gallon; France has proposed banning private jets entirely.

**How to Fly Private: The Ownership Spectrum**

Tivnan explains that private aviation access comes in several tiers. Outright ownership starts at $7–10M for a light jet like the Phenom 300 and climbs to $60–75M for a Gulfstream 650 or Global 8500; Donald Trump's Boeing 757 runs about $100M. Beyond the purchase price, fueling a mid-sized plane costs roughly $8,000 a tank, hangar fees at a major airport run $12,000/month, and pilot salaries, maintenance, insurance, and depreciation can push total annual operating costs above $1 million.

For those who want access without full ownership, fractional shares allow up to 32 people to jointly own a specific aircraft, splitting costs by usage. Charter is the most flexible option: $6,000–$8,000/hour for a light jet, $8,000–$10,000 for mid-size, $12,000–$15,000 for super mid-size, and $14,000–$20,000 for heavies. Frequent charter customers can buy jet card memberships — prepaid hour blocks starting around $100,000 — for guaranteed access at locked-in rates. Tivnan compares the jet card to Airbnb: "When you're done with it, you walk away and you have no other responsibilities to that asset."

**Why Corporations Pay**

Magellan doesn't own any jets itself. It acts as a broker, monetizing empty legs (return flights with no passengers) and idle hangar time on behalf of corporate and individual owners. Tivnan says corporations are often happy to pay a $15,000 premium to save a single hour of flight time if the passengers are highly paid executives. He cites Walmart CEO Doug McMillon, who earns $24 million per year — roughly $7,000 per hour — and uses one of the company's 20 jets to visit stores nationwide and return to Bentonville the same day. Beyond productivity, private jets are also used for client entertainment: "Whether it's entertaining customers, picking them up, golf events, sports games, really important account — let's go ahead and book the Gulfstream."

Private planes also reach 5,000 U.S. airports vs. roughly 500 served commercially, allowing executives to fly directly to smaller markets without connections or rental cars.

**The Subsidy Problem**

Collins argues that private aviation receives a hidden subsidy from commercial travelers and taxpayers. Private jets account for about 16% of FAA airspace use but contribute only about 2% of the costs of running that system. Commercial passengers pay a 7.5% federal excise tax, a passenger facility charge, a flight segment tax, and a security fee — adding roughly $20 in taxes to a $60 Boston-to-New York ticket. Jet owners, by contrast, often pay only a fuel tax of $0.22 per gallon if they own their aircraft outright. The FAA's small airport network — which no commercial airline serves — is funded largely by this system while serving primarily private fliers.

The 2017 Tax Cuts and Jobs Act added another benefit: full first-year expensing of a new private jet, provided it is used for business. Collins notes that the definition of "business" is elastic. Trips to Argentina for pheasant hunting have qualified. "You and I could fly to Aruba, play a round of golf, and have a business conversation, and that would be a business flight."

Private jet lobbyists have spent $68 million since 2010 influencing lawmakers. Their recent campaigns have also focused on privacy: jet owners can petition the FAA to remove their names from public tracking registries, and fractional ownership obscures passenger identity further. Collins says some in European aviation security circles worry that the next major attack won't come via commercial aircraft — citing the lack of any screening: "Nobody's frisking you, nobody's taking your water bottle."

**Carbon Footprint and Short Hops**

Collins identifies the environmental impact as his primary concern. Private jets emit 10–20 times more carbon per passenger than commercial aviation. Musk's 171 flights last year produced 200 times the average American's annual carbon footprint. And a large share of these flights are short: Collins's analysis of a Boston-area airport found 41% of departures were under an hour, 14% under 30 minutes — trips with readily available alternatives.

Tivnan pushes back on the framing, arguing that aviation overall represents about 3% of global carbon emissions, and private aviation is "less than three percent of that three percent." Magellan and similar operators offer voluntary carbon offset credits to clients who want to neutralize their flight emissions.

**Policy Responses**

A bill currently before Congress would raise the private jet fuel tax from $0.22 to $1.95 per gallon, directing the revenue toward clean and affordable transportation investment. France has proposed a full ban on private jet flights. Tivnan opposes both, arguing that higher costs for corporate flight departments will ultimately be passed on to consumers: "What you're doing is just creating a higher cost for the companies that really run the country." Collins responds by noting that commercial business travel functioned fine throughout most of the 20th century without the modern private jet industry: "Somehow we had healthy commerce and businesses."

Collins's preferred solution is personal: he recently persuaded a wealthy friend to sell his jet. If that means a three-and-a-half-hour train ride instead of a 40-minute flight, he says, "So sorry."

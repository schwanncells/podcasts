# Science Friday — "Isabella Rossellini on how acting connects to animal cognition"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guests:** Isabella Rossellini (Italian actor, model, and director; holds a master's degree in animal behavior; operates a farm raising Lincoln Longwool sheep); Carl Safina (ecologist; PhD in ecology; author of *Beyond Words: What Animals Think and Feel* and other books on animal consciousness).

## TL;DR

- **Animal cognition has "completely flipped"** — where science once held that only humans could think or feel, brain-imaging studies now show rats dreaming with the same neural patterns as humans and dogs activating their "love" brain regions when shown photos of familiar people.
- **Anthropomorphism debate resolved** — Safina argues that observing emotional bonds or fear responses in animals isn't projection but observation: all vertebrates share the same nervous system and mood-regulating chemicals, so calling contextually fearful behavior "fear" is insight, not fantasy.
- **Isabella Rossellini wanted to study ethology at 14** after reading Konrad Lorenz's *King Solomon's Ring*, but was intimidated by science; she finally earned her master's in animal behavior in her 50s and calls older-age risk tolerance — "what the hell, I just want to know" — her enabling factor.
- **Acting and animal observation share a mechanism** — Rossellini describes acting as working "inside out" (generating genuine emotion that shows externally) rather than mimicking surface expressions, which parallels Darwin's research on emotional expression across humans and animals.
- **Animal personalities are real and heritable** — Rossellini's Lincoln Longwool sheep O'Keeffe seeks out affection, while others flee from touch; domestication has selected for docility over 10,000 years, but individual personality variation persists.
- **Safina's screech owl Alfie** — rescued as a near-dead chick, bonded to the family, has lived wild but returned to their yard for eight years, raised 26 wild babies, and had two mates with starkly different personalities (the second is aggressive near the nest box, while Alfie tolerates Carl climbing a ladder inches away during feeding).
- **"Permission to observe"** — a zoo director's line "if a dog seems happy, it's a happy dog" was Safina's barrier-breaking moment; both guests argue the anti-anthropomorphism rule was itself unscientific, functioning more like a religious prohibition than a methodological constraint.
- **Science's value is its questions, not its answers** — Safina's closing point: people fear science because they think it has the answers; what makes science powerful is that it has the questions.

**Isabella Rossellini's Path to Ethology**

Rossellini first encountered ethology at 14 through Konrad Lorenz's *King Solomon's Ring*, which she describes as sparking an immediate and lasting obsession: "it was literally as if a little lamp all went up into my head." She was deterred from pursuing science formally — intimidated by math and chemistry, discouraged from agricultural studies as Italian small farms collapsed — and defaulted to the family business of acting and modeling. She returned to school in her 50s for a master's in animal behavior, explaining that older-age conferred the willingness to risk failure: "when you're young, you have to prove yourself… but when you're older, you just say, well, what the hell?"

**The Shift in Animal Cognition Science**

Safina describes the field as having "completely flipped" from his childhood, when the consensus held that only humans could think or be conscious and all other animals were "little biological machines." Neurobiological advances changed that: brain imaging of dreaming rats shows the same activation patterns as dreaming humans; MRI experiments with dogs shown photos of strangers versus familiar people show the love-associated brain regions lighting up selectively for known individuals. Rossellini notes that as a child she was continually told her dog's apparent happiness and attentiveness to her mood was mere projection — science now vindicates her intuition.

**The Anthropomorphism Debate**

Safina offers a precise definition: anthropomorphism is projecting human inner states onto non-humans — attributing thought to a coffee cup. But observing that a dog or elephant forms emotional bonds with offspring, or acts frightened specifically in the presence of a threat, is observation, not projection. The justification is evolutionary and neurological: all vertebrates share the same fundamental nervous system architecture and the same chemicals that generate mood and motivation. Safina argues that the blanket anti-anthropomorphism rule was itself an unscientific imposition — "almost more of a religious type feeling that you may not do this" — that constrained open observation rather than protecting it. His reframing: when you observe contextually appropriate fear behavior, calling it fear is insight.

**Acting as Animal Observation**

Rossellini draws a direct parallel between method acting and animal behavior study. Good acting, she explains, is "inside out" — an actor recalls a real emotion until the face follows naturally, rather than manufacturing a surface expression. This is the same mechanism Darwin described in *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals*: emotion generates external signals, and those signals are evolutionarily conserved across species. Rossellini also connects acting's emphasis on reactive listening — "it's reacting, not only acting, because you connect" — to how humans and animals communicate through body language without shared language.

**Animal Personalities and the Farm**

Rossellini raises Lincoln Longwool sheep on her farm — a threatened heritage breed — and named her favorite O'Keeffe after Georgia O'Keeffe. She notes that domestication has bred livestock for docility over millennia (wild mouflon would be dangerous to approach), but that individual personality variation within that docile range is striking and largely undiscussed in animal science curricula: some sheep seek touch, others flee from it, a few will still ram.

**Alfie the Screech Owl**

Safina and his wife Patricia rescued a screech owl chick — found dehydrated and covered in fly eggs — eight years ago. The owl couldn't fly after recovery and the family bonded before they could release her. When they finally did, Alfie disappeared for a few days and then returned permanently. She has since lived wild while maintaining regular contact with the family, raised 26 wild chicks with two successive mates, and demonstrated clearly distinct personalities: her first mate was relaxed around the family, while her current mate of four years is aggressive near the nest box and will strike at intruders. Alfie herself remains calm enough that Safina can open the nest box and observe feeding from two inches away.

**Permission and the Spirit of Science**

Both guests converge on what Safina calls "the permission of it" — the experience of being told by a zoo director "if a dog seems happy, it's a happy dog" as a genuinely barrier-breaking moment. Rossellini echoes this: the prohibition on anthropomorphism created a kind of dictatorial constraint that prevented open observation. Safina's closing formulation: science's power is not that it has the answers but that it has the questions.

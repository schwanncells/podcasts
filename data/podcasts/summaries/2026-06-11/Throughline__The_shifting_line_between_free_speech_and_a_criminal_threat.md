# Throughline — "The shifting line between free speech and a criminal threat"

**Host:** Rund Abdelfattah. **Guests:** David L. Hudson Jr. (associate professor of law, Belmont University Law School; First Amendment fellow, Freedom Forum); Mary Anne Franks (professor of law, George Washington Law School; president and legislative and tech policy director, Cyber Civil Rights Initiative; author of "Fearless Speech" and "The Cult of the Constitution").

## TL;DR

- **Watts v. United States (1969) defined "true threats"** — Robert Watts, 18, made hyperbolic comments about shooting President LBJ during a Vietnam War protest; the Supreme Court ruled 9-0 that true threats require specific intent and context, not merely impolite or inflammatory speech.
- **"True threat" requires narrow definition** — The Watts factors distinguish actual threats (serious intent to cause harm) from political hyperbole: context of the statement, conditional language, and listener reaction all matter for determining whether speech is protected.
- **Virginia v. Black (2003) addressed cross burnings** — Court ruled that burning a cross can be a true threat when intended to intimidate (like Elliot and O'Meara terrorizing a Black neighbor), but not when part of KKK ritual speech (Barry Black's case). Justice Clarence Thomas's rare oral argument intervention highlighted the historical terror symbol of the burning cross.
- **Counterman v. Colorado (2023) required "substantial risk" of harm awareness** — Billy Counterman, who sent hundreds of harassing messages to musician Coles Whalen, was acquitted because prosecutors failed to prove he consciously disregarded a substantial risk his messages would be perceived as threatening. The decision prioritized speaker intent over reasonable recipient perception.
- **Counterman's collateral harm to abuse victims** — The 7-2 ruling gave stalkers and domestic abusers more protection than it offers critics of government officials. Prosecutors must now prove the defendant knew their threatening behavior created risk of terror, not just that a reasonable person would feel terrorized.
- **Online speech changed the threat landscape** — The internet makes threats easier to make and harder to trace; a single statement reaches millions instead of 70 listeners, creating ambiguity about context and intent that courts have struggled to resolve across 50+ years.
- **Power imbalance is overlooked** — Franks argues courts focus on protecting individuals' speech rights against public officials but ignore how governments themselves use fear and intimidation against dissenting voices, inverting the original free speech principle.

**The Watts Case and the Birth of "True Threats"**

In August 1966, Robert Watts, an 18-year-old protester with the W.E.B. du Bois Club, made an offhand comment at an anti-war gathering on the Washington Monument grounds. During a discussion about racial discrimination in the Vietnam War draft, Watts said he would "put in my scope" President LBJ if drafted. An Army Counterintelligence Corps investigator monitoring the protest took the remark seriously and Watts was arrested under a 1917 law criminalizing threats against the president.

Watts's arrest echoed a much earlier case: Luther Baldwin, arrested under the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798 for a drunken rant hoping a cannon would fire through President Adams. Both were prosecuted for speech that was never intended as a literal assassination threat. The Supreme Court took up Watts's appeal in 1969 without hearing oral arguments—a signal the Court viewed it as straightforward. By then, New York Times v. Sullivan had already established that "debate on public issues should be uninhibited, robust and wide open" and may "include vehement caustic and sometimes unpleasantly sharp attacks on government and public officials."

The Court issued a unanimous ruling in favor of Watts. In doing so, it formally introduced the legal term "true threat" and established what became known as the Watts factors: the context in which statements were made (a political discussion group, not a conspiracy), whether the threat was conditional (only if drafted), and the audience's reaction (they laughed). Justice William O. Douglas wrote separately that the case represented a repeat of the "sorriest chapters in our history" under the Alien and Sedition Acts.

**Cross Burning and the Intent Question**

Thirty years later, the Supreme Court took up two cross-burning cases in 2003 that further refined what constitutes a true threat. Barry Elton Black, a KKK leader from Pennsylvania, traveled to Carroll County, Virginia in 1998 and burned a 30-foot cross at a Klan gathering on a highway with the property owner's permission. In a separate case, Richard Elliot and Jonathan O'Meara burned a cross on the lawn of their Black neighbor, James Jubilee, after Jubilee complained about their gun shooting.

Both men were charged under a Virginia state law criminalizing cross burning to intimidate. The Supreme Court ruled that cross burnings could be true threats only when the purpose was intimidation, not all cross burnings automatically. Justice Clarence Thomas, the Court's sole Black justice at the time, spoke during oral arguments for the first time in six to eight years. He emphasized the historical context: nearly 100 years of KKK terror, lynching, and the cross as a tool of reign of terror. He argued that while intent matters, the impact on Black people who see a burning cross—who know it historically preceded violence—cannot be ignored.

The Court's ruling distinguished Black's case (ritual speech celebrating Klan identity) from Elliot and O'Meara's case (intentional intimidation targeting a specific neighbor). The Court defined true threats as requiring "the speaker means to communicate a serious expression of an intent to commit an act of unlawful violence to a particular individual or group of individuals." Yet Thomas dissented, believing the action of burning a cross was inherently threatening regardless of the speaker's stated intent.

**The Counterman Ruling and Stalking**

In 2014, Billy Counterman, a Colorado man with a diagnosed mental illness, became obsessed with local musician Coles Whalen. Over years, he sent hundreds of Facebook messages—blocking, reblocking, creating new accounts—in what Whalen described as a campaign of obsessive contact. His messages indicated he knew personal details about her and was watching her. Whalen became so afraid he would appear at her concerts that she stopped performing; her bandmates described her as terrified, constantly scanning crowds and exits.

When Whalen's family discovered Counterman had previously been convicted of making violent threats against female family members, she reported him to police. Counterman was convicted under Colorado's stalking law, which criminalized repeatedly communicating with someone in a way that would make a reasonable person suffer serious emotional distress. He challenged the conviction on First Amendment grounds, arguing prosecutors must prove he intended to terrorize Whalen, not merely that she felt terror.

In June 2023, the Supreme Court ruled 7-2 in Counterman's favor. Justice Elena Kagan's majority opinion held that prosecutors must show the defendant acted with "conscious disregard" of a substantial risk that their statements would be perceived as threatening—not just that a reasonable person would feel threatened. The ruling aimed to prevent hyperbolic political speech from being prosecuted, preserving Watts-like protection for venting and exaggeration.

But Franks noted the collateral damage: domestic abusers and stalkers received the same protection as political speakers. A person terrorizing a private citizen now has more legal cover than a person criticizing the government. The Counterman ruling required proof of the defendant's subjective awareness, even when hundreds of messages objectively convey predatory behavior.

**The Persistent Tension Between Context and Intent**

Fifty-eight years separate the Watts case from Counterman, yet the core question remains: Is a threat determined by the speaker's intent, the reasonable listener's perception, or the historical/social context in which it's made? Hudson acknowledged that the progression of cases reveals courts struggling to draw this line. The 1960s civil rights and Vietnam era demanded protecting anti-government speech; the post-9/11 and Obama eras brought real assassination attempts and online threats at unprecedented scale.

The internet fundamentally altered the threat landscape. Unlike Watts's 70-person audience or a cross burned in one neighborhood, online speech reaches millions globally, persists indefinitely, and can be reinterpreted constantly. Prosecutors have exploited this ambiguity; Hudson noted that "there are many abusers who literally get together and talk about the boundaries of the First Amendment on how they can get away with things."

Franks raised a deeper critique: the entire doctrinal frame focuses on protecting individual speakers against governments and public figures, ignoring the reverse—how governments use intimidation and fear against dissenting citizens. The original free speech principle was asymmetrical: citizens needed protection from state repression, not the other way around. Yet modern doctrine treats threats symmetrically, as if speech rights for stalkers and speech rights for political dissidents occupy the same moral plane.

Hudson conceded that the challenge is fundamental: figuring out the boundary between real threats and hyperbolic or perceived threats "seems to get it completely right." The cases show why: some speech reveals troubled individuals who pose genuine danger; other speech is a "safety valve" that allows venting and prevents violence. Sometimes one, sometimes the other—and the law cannot cleanly separate them. As the nation grows more polarized and threatens against public officials multiply, more Watts cases will emerge mixed with genuinely dangerous speech, making prosecutors' and judges' work harder without clear doctrinal guidance.

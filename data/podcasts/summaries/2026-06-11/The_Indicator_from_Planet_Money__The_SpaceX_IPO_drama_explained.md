# The Indicator from Planet Money — "The SpaceX IPO drama explained"

**Host:** Ricky Mulvey. **Guest:** Darian Woods (co-host); Alex Maturi (former CEO, S&P Dow Jones Indices); Campbell Harvey (finance professor, Duke University; economist who identified Treasury yield curve as recession predictor).

## TL;DR

- **SpaceX offering only 4% of shares in IPO** — the largest IPO ever, but the company remains 96% privately owned by Elon Musk, employees, and private investors, changing how companies use public markets.
- **Nasdaq reduced waiting period to 3 weeks** — previously companies had to trade for at least a few months before joining the Nasdaq 100 index; now they can join in as little as three weeks after IPO.
- **Nasdaq counts SpaceX shares as 3x what they actually are** — for index purposes, the exchange is treating the 4% free float as if it were 12%, forcing index funds to buy as if triple the shares are publicly available.
- **This rule change will artificially inflate demand and stock price** — Campbell Harvey estimates half of the demand for SpaceX shares will come from index investors forced to match the inflated 12% weighting, raising the price before retail investors can buy.
- **Rule changes benefit large private companies going public** — OpenAI and Anthropic could see similar advantages if they go public; the changes were announced just a month before SpaceX's IPO.
- **Retail investors shut out of tech wealth growth** — only accredited investors (high earners or millionaires) could buy shares of SpaceX while it was private and growing; regular people can only buy after the IPO at inflated prices.

---

**IPOs Have Changed Purpose**

Alex Maturi explains that historically, IPOs were primarily a way for companies to raise capital for factories and expansion. SpaceX and similar mega-private companies no longer need public capital—they've grown to "trillion-dollar behemoths" using private dollars alone. Today, an IPO serves a different purpose: to monetize existing shareholders' positions and provide early investors and employees a way to cash out. The company is offering a small slice of ownership to the public simply to establish a market price for the entire company.

**The Nasdaq's Accelerated Timeline and Index Rules**

The Nasdaq recently made two significant rule changes that benefit companies like SpaceX. First, it dramatically reduced the waiting period before a new company can join its top 100 index. Previously, companies had to trade for several months and the Nasdaq added/deleted index members once per year. Now a company can join the Nasdaq 100 in as little as three weeks. Maturi warns this is problematic because stocks are volatile just after an IPO—"you get a lot of dislocations early on, the price runs up, it collapses"—and companies need time to settle before joining an index.

**The Free Float Problem**

Campbell Harvey identifies the more fundamental issue: how the Nasdaq counts company size for its index. SpaceX's free float—the percentage of shares available to the public for trading—is just 4%. But when SpaceX is added to the Nasdaq 100, the exchange will weight it as if 12% of shares are publicly available (multiplying by three). This forces index funds to buy SpaceX stock as if the company offered triple the actual number of shares.

**The Demand Storm**

The practical consequence is significant. Campbell estimates that roughly half of the demand for SpaceX shares will come from index funds mechanically forced to match the inflated 12% weighting instead of the actual 4% free float. This artificial demand will raise the price before retail investors even enter the market. The Nasdaq has said the new approach is an improvement over the old system, which could have forced index funds to buy even more stock. But Campbell argues the real solution is to weight companies only by the value of shares actually available to the public.

**The Wealth Inequality Angle**

Harvey highlights a deeper problem revealed by this episode: regular retail investors missed the entire growth of massive private tech companies like SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic. Only accredited investors—people earning significant income or with a net worth exceeding $1 million—could buy shares during the private phase and benefit from the company's rise. By the time a company goes public, much of the wealth creation has already happened, leaving retail investors to buy at inflated prices.

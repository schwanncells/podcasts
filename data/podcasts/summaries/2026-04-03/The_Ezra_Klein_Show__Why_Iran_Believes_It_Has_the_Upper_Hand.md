# The Ezra Klein Show — "Why Iran Believes It Has the Upper Hand"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Suzanne Maloney (vice president and director of the foreign policy program at the Brookings Institution; advisor to multiple Democratic and Republican presidential administrations; author and editor of several books on Iran).

## TL;DR

- **Iran shut the Strait of Hormuz and considers itself winning** — tanker traffic through the strait fell from 130–140 per day before the war to just a handful; Iran controls passage through ~20% of global daily oil and gas exports.
- **Trump's 15-point peace plan demands things Iran can't accept** — no enrichment, no nuclear weapons, end to proxy support, end to ballistic missile program; Iran rejected direct talks, calling negotiations a ruse after being bombed twice while diplomatic talks were underway.
- **Iran's counterproposal is partly self-executing** — Iran wants compensation, control over the strait as a permanent toll booth, and sanctions relief; the sanctions piece has already half-happened (US lifted sanctions on Iranian oil during the war).
- **The regime survived despite Khamenei's death on day one** — successor Mojtaba Khamenei hasn't been seen in public and may be grievously injured, but IRGC-aligned military officials are running the state; Maloney says successors are "more radical, more hardline" than those they replaced.
- **Neither of Trump's regime-change theories worked** — the Iranian people didn't rise up (after mass executions in January left them terrorized), and there were no pragmatic "Venezuela option" replacements below Khamenei because Iran is a deeply institutionalized regime, not a personalistic one.
- **Only about 30% of Iran's missile capabilities have been taken out** — Iran still holds a stockpile of highly enriched uranium and the technical expertise to eventually rebuild its nuclear program; Maloney says whatever restraint Khamenei exercised on weaponization "is likely to be eliminated" in the aftermath.
- **The economic clock is running against the US** — global markets have yet to fully price in the strait closure; $4–6 gas prices are coming, with food, fertilizer, helium, and chip supply chains to follow; as shortages become material rather than notional, Iran's leverage grows rapidly.
- **Maloney calls this a "Suez moment"** — the US launched a war without a plan for day two, alienated allies who weren't consulted, and may leave China and Pakistan to broker the exit; Iran may emerge with the Strait as a permanent toll booth, stronger deterrence lessons, and a conviction that the only real security is nuclear capability.

---

**A War Without a Coherent Narrative**

Maloney opens by flagging the fundamental confusion in following this war through Trump's public statements, which she says have become "self-contradictory" to the point of being untrackable as policy. Within a single day Trump veers between claiming the war is nearly over and threatening dramatic escalation, between saying negotiations are going great and that there's no one to talk to. She advises watching both Trump's language on social media and what is actually happening on the ground, because "the president has often said one thing and done something different." The Iranians, she notes, are "well aware of and very much prepared for" this gap. Her core assessment: Trump is clearly trying to find a way to "declare victory and walk away from the conflict."

**Why Iran Thinks It's Winning**

The reason Iran believes it has the upper hand is the Strait of Hormuz. Before the war, 130 to 140 tankers crossed the strait daily; that traffic has dropped to just a handful. In the war's opening days, Iran struck ships transiting the Gulf and persuaded insurers, shipping companies, and oil companies to avoid the waterway without Iranian assurance of safe passage. Because the strait carries roughly 20% of the world's daily oil and gas exports, the closure has already produced severe price spikes across Asia, with effects spreading toward Europe. Maloney frames the asymmetry clearly: Iran can afford to wait because every day the stoppage continues, the economic pain on the global economy — and on Trump's political standing — intensifies. "For the Iranians, this is an existential crisis. They're prepared to wait this out as long as they can."

**The Economic Time Bomb**

The conversation dwells on timing because both sides understand that the economic effects of the strait closure are about to shift from notional to material. The period of tankers that had already cleared the strait before the war are still arriving at ports, masking the coming crunch. That buffer is ending. Fertilizer supply into Europe is about to tighten; chip production is threatened by helium shortages (helium passes through the Gulf); food prices are next. Maloney says Prime Minister Modi compared the situation to COVID and its supply chain disruptions — a "very apt comparison, particularly if this extends over the course of another month or so." She confirms there is no historical precedent: "We've never had a prolonged closure of the Strait of Hormuz." As she puts it, Iran's weapon — choking the strait — is "about to become a much more potent and powerful weapon because the shortages become real and material as opposed to notional."

**Trump's 15-Point Plan vs. Iran's Counterproposal**

The US sent Iran a 15-point peace plan that reprises long-standing demands: a durable commitment to no enrichment, no nuclear weapons, an end to support for proxies, and an end to the ballistic missile program. Iran has refused direct negotiations — partly because of bitterness over being bombed twice during diplomatic talks (the June 2025 war and the current conflict, both preceded by negotiations that Iran now views as cover for planned strikes). Iran's five-point counteroffer asks for compensation for war losses, sanctions relief, and, most significantly, permanent control over the Strait of Hormuz as a toll booth. Maloney notes that while reparations from America seem unlikely, the sanctions piece has already partly materialized: the US lifted sanctions on Iranian oil during the conflict, and with global energy supplies squeezed, that oil commands higher prices. She argues Iran's counterproposal is "to some extent" less a negotiating position than an articulation of what their post-war strategy will be anyway.

**Who's Running Iran Now**

Ayatollah Ali Khamenei was killed on the first day of the war after 37 years as Supreme Leader. His son Mojtaba Khamenei has been named his successor but has not appeared in public; Trump told the Financial Times "the son is either dead or in extremely bad shape." Maloney says the question of Mojtaba's condition is "almost irrelevant at this point" — he can function as a cipher while IRGC-aligned military officials run the state. The current Speaker of Parliament, Mohamed Bahar Khalibaf (a former IRGC commander), is among those effectively in charge and has publicly posted that "the aggressor must be punished." The regime's deeply institutionalized structure — with Supreme Leader representatives embedded in every administrative office — means it can function without visible top leadership. Maloney adds that Mojtaba's potential injury actually plays into the regime's own themes of martyrdom and sacrifice, and that the shift toward military leadership over clergy is something the regime is "leaning into."

**Why Regime Change Failed**

Trump entered the war with two theories of regime collapse. The first: the Iranian people would rise up. The second: a "Venezuela option" where killing the top leadership would surface more pragmatic, transactional figures who would cut a deal. Neither happened, and Maloney explains why both failed for the same underlying reason. Iran is not a personalistic regime — it is a deeply institutionalized one. It came to power through a popular revolution and spent 47 years ensuring it could not be toppled the way it toppled the monarchy. When Khamenei died, there was joy in Iran, but the population had been slaughtered in large numbers during January protests just weeks earlier and remained terrorized. No organized political movement existed to fill the vacuum. As for the Venezuela option: the figures who rose to replace the killed leadership are not pragmatic moderates but hardliners more closely aligned with the Revolutionary Guard. "This is a regime that came to power through a popular revolution," Maloney says — decapitation strengthens it.

**The Nuclear and Missile Picture**

Despite two rounds of strikes (the June 2025 war and the current conflict), Iran's nuclear program has not been eliminated. Maloney says Iran retains the technical expertise to rebuild and may still hold a stockpile of highly enriched uranium, possibly buried under Isfahan or dispersed at other sites. "There isn't the level of visibility and confidence that we have hit every possible element of the program." Crucially, she argues that Khamenei himself had been "one of the sources of some constraint on the decision to move forward or not with a weapons program," and that this restraint "is likely to be eliminated" in the aftermath. On missiles: the US assesses roughly 30% of Iran's missile capabilities have been destroyed, but Iran still has launchers, missiles, and the know-how to rebuild production facilities. Maloney notes Iran appears to have been "improving their accuracy over the course of this war" and retains a large, flexible drone capability.

**What Iran Is Learning**

Maloney argues Iran is drawing a set of dangerous lessons from this war. First, time can be on their side — seizing the strait gave them a weapon that strengthens rather than weakens over time. Second, the resilience that sustained Iran through the 1980s war with Iraq (when they were cut off from international weapons and battered economically) applies here. Third, and most consequentially: negotiations cannot be trusted. The Obama-era deal was ripped up by Trump; Iran was bombed twice during diplomatic talks. The lesson being embedded at the leadership level is that "your only true safety is your deterrence capability" — the ability to impose pain on the global economy through the strait, through strikes on Gulf infrastructure (data centers, luxury hotels, airports in Dubai and Riyadh), and ultimately perhaps through a nuclear weapon. Maloney calls this "a set of very dangerous lessons" being institutionalized by a regime that studies its own wars obsessively — noting that the Revolutionary Guard produced a hundred-volume study of the Iran-Iraq war.

**Israel's Strategy and the Lebanon Front**

Maloney distinguishes US and Israeli war aims. Israel understood what it wanted, she says — the end of the Islamic Republic, or failing that, the permanent degradation of Iranian capabilities. The regime's survival is "a tremendous disappointment" for Netanyahu, but Israel is "very satisfied with the military objectives" it has achieved and is "prepared to maintain a long, hot war." She questions whether Israel's "mowing the lawn" strategy — which failed against Hamas over the long run — can work against Iran, particularly once Iran controls the Strait of Hormuz and can pressure the international community to restrain Israeli strikes. Meanwhile, Israel has launched a significant invasion of southern Lebanon following Hezbollah missile attacks, a front Maloney says "deserves much, much more attention." She warns that Israeli occupation of southern Lebanese territory risks repeating the cycle of the 1980s, strengthening radical groups, and destabilizing a country with "so much potential, so many educated people."

**The "Suez Moment" and China's Role**

Maloney introduces the broader geopolitical frame: this may be a Suez moment — a juncture that marks the end of American global leadership. She says China, Pakistan, and others are already trying to position themselves as mediators because a prolonged strait closure hurts everyone. Crucially, Trump has scheduled a summit in Beijing for May 14–15, and "he would presumably need to have this in his rearview mirror" before going. That deadline gives all parties a stronger push toward settlement — but it is not a settlement the US will be driving. The US entered without consulting allies, launched strikes without a plan for day two or three, and now faces a situation where "the rest of the world is going to have to pick up that mantle." Maloney notes that the Gulf states — Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar — invested heavily in Trump's family and "I can't imagine they're going to be very happy holding the bag for this crisis."

**A Bleak Conclusion**

Maloney does not soften her assessment when Klein asks directly: is the likely outcome — Iranian regime intact, Strait of Hormuz as a toll booth, sanctions partially lifted, US withdrawing without achieved objectives — a war the US lost? "I think that's correct." She says there is "very little evidence that we're going to be able to come out of this war with a different regime in Iran, with less control over the Strait of Hormuz." The long-term danger, she argues, is an Iran that emerges emboldened, having survived the worst two technologically superior adversaries could deliver — and having learned that the Strait is a credible weapon, that negotiations are a trap, and that nuclear capability is the only reliable deterrent. "A regime that has been very dangerous, even at its weaker moments" will become more so.

# The Chopping Block — "Is Canton a Real Blockchain? Ethereum's Cypherpunk Dilemma, AI Security Chaos"

**Host:** Haseeb Qureshi (managing partner, Dragonfly Capital). **Guests:** Tom (DeFi investor and recurring panelist); Tarun Chitra (founder and CEO, Gauntlet — crypto risk research and market simulation firm); Evgeny Gaevoy (CEO, Wintermute — one of crypto's largest market makers).

## TL;DR

- **Canton is a DRW-incubated enterprise blockchain whose state is not publicly visible** — validators must receive a two-thirds vote from existing validators plus post substantial stake to join; DRW still holds a large share of the token supply, making ownership highly concentrated.
- **Canton's claimed $200+ billion TVL cannot be independently verified** — unlike public chains where anyone can query an RPC node to check balances and total supply, Canton's state is only visible to validators; panelists call this "privacy through obscurity" rather than cryptographic privacy.
- **Evgeny's contrarian take: the Ethereum Foundation is, for the first time, ahead of the curve** — by doubling down on cypherpunk values, EF is positioning Ethereum as a sanctuary for users who have nowhere else to go as Bitcoin, Solana, and most of the industry get co-opted by TradFi.
- **The stablecoin precedent shows why permissionless chains matter** — Circle would never have found its global user base through any sales process; the permissionless chain discovered those users, and Canton cannot replicate this dynamic.
- **Circle and Tether's dominance over stablecoins has effectively ended governance-by-fork on Ethereum** — whichever chain they choose is canonical; a minority fork is economically worthless because all RWA state disappears on the other side.
- **Drift, a perp DEX on Solana, was hacked for roughly $270 million** — including over $150 million in JLP positions and $50 million in USDC, making it one of the largest DeFi hacks in history; the attack vector was unknown at time of recording.
- **The Axios NPM package — 100 million weekly downloads, 175,000 dependent packages — was potentially compromised by a North Korea-linked supply chain attack** — a remote access trojan was pushed through a maintainer account, hitting a large fraction of the internet's JavaScript infrastructure on the same day as several other major security events.
- **AI is accelerating offensive cybersecurity faster than defensive capabilities can keep up** — every LLM can now produce "underhanded C competition" backdoor code that is nearly impossible to detect in code review, breaking the open source trust model that the industry has depended on for decades.

**What Is Canton, and Does It Count as a Blockchain?**

Canton is an enterprise-focused blockchain incubated by DRW, one of the largest proprietary trading firms in TradFi. To become a validator, a firm submits a business proposal and needs approval from two-thirds of existing validators, plus must post substantial stake. The network's state is not publicly visible — validators can see it, but no outside party can query an RPC node to check total supply, holder balances, or transaction history. Canton has landed significant institutional clients and claims over $200 billion in TVL, but that figure cannot be independently verified by anyone outside the validator set.

The debate flared when ZK Sync (a Dragonfly portfolio company) published a thread challenging Canton's claim to be a permissionless blockchain. Crypto OGs including Mert and Eli Ben-Sasson of Starknet joined in, arguing that a chain whose state is opaque, whose validators are permissioned, and whose assets can be fully controlled by issuers does not meet the definition of a blockchain. Canton defenders counter that it is pragmatically designed for financial institutions that require privacy and control and that it meaningfully improves on traditional settlement rails like T+1 and SWIFT.

Tom draws a distinction between Ethereum's asset-layer restrictions (where a tokenized security might require KYC but the base chain remains open) and Canton's architecture (where even the existence and movement of assets is hidden from public view). He frames Canton's privacy as "privacy through obscurity" — a model that historically degrades or gets broken — rather than cryptographic privacy. Tarun notes the irony of Canton marketing DeFi-branded products like DEXes while the mechanics of those products are entirely opaque and unverifiable.

**What Public Blockchains Actually Offer That Canton Cannot**

Haseeb argues the core value of public blockchains is not that they are a better database — it is that they expand the reachable market in ways no sales organization could. Circle did not find its global stablecoin user base; the permissionless chain found those users for it. The same dynamic, he argues, applies even to KYC-gated tokenized securities: the barrier is low enough that anyone who can pass a standard identity check can participate, which is the vast majority of the world's population. Canton, by contrast, serves only a known, pre-approved set of counterparties — a closed loop that could have used a traditional shared database to achieve the same result.

Evgeny concedes that Canton probably improves on existing clearing infrastructure and disintermediates some custodians and prime brokers. He draws a parallel to Nathan Fielder's The Rehearsal: institutions that always could have offered 24/7 settlement suddenly feel empowered to do it once they frame it as "being on a blockchain" — a kind of permission-granting mind trick. But the structural properties of a public chain — operating without human approval, running on weekends, available to anyone — are absent. Tarun questions the legal enforceability of Canton-settled transactions and whether, if challenged in court, Canton settlement would carry the same weight as settlement through a traditional custodian.

Haseeb raises the broader point that Canton's role may be primarily as a ledger: if Goldman knows all its counterparties already and can get them on Canton, the openness of a public blockchain was never needed in the first place. The case for public blockchains has always been that the market is bigger than you can see — that stablecoin users existed who no business would have been able to source. Canton cannot make that argument.

**Ethereum's Cypherpunk Dilemma and the EF Mandate**

Evgeny offers what he calls his most contrarian view: the Ethereum Foundation is, for the first time in its history, ahead of the curve. With Bitcoin being co-opted by TradFi through MicroStrategy and the strategic Bitcoin reserve, and Solana effectively competing with Canton to become the next Nasdaq, there is no longer a cypherpunk home in crypto — except potentially Ethereum. The EF's renewed emphasis on decentralization and cypherpunk values positions Ethereum as a sanctuary for users who have nowhere else to go, rather than as a TradFi infrastructure layer. He notes that Solana's post-FTX comeback is impressive from a throughput standpoint but is essentially a competition over who becomes the next TradFi exchange venue.

Haseeb pushes back, arguing that the debate is not whether decentralization matters but what Ethereum needs at the margin. His position: Ethereum already has the cypherpunks — they have nowhere else to go anyway — and Ethereum's goal has always been to be the world computer, which means absorbing the whole world as stakeholders, not keeping the chain governed by a small group of ideologues. He uses Linux as the analogy: the vision was not that Linus Torvalds rules the world, but that everyone has free, auditable software. Institutions on Ethereum get a seat at the table; that is the intended end state. He adds that Vitalik's "sanctuary technology" framing is specifically about Ethereum remaining usable by people who have no other option — political dissidents, the unbanked — not about cypherpunks controlling governance.

Evgeny acknowledges the short- and mid-term price implications of the EF's direction are likely negative. The cypherpunk positioning is a long-term bet: if it works and Ethereum becomes the Linux of blockchains, it could be a highly valued asset. If it does not, and Canton and similar networks capture the economic activity, Ethereum fails. Tom notes that the cypherpunk niche is real but probably much smaller than the scale Ethereum has been built up to serve over the past six or seven years, and that this is where the disconnect comes from.

**Circle, Tether, and the End of Governance by Fork**

Haseeb authored the original argument that Circle and Tether's dominance over stablecoin supply has effectively ended governance-by-fork as a viable Ethereum coordination mechanism. On any minority fork, every stablecoin and RWA issuer would need to choose which chain to support, and the chain without Circle and Tether becomes economically worthless almost immediately. This does not mean Circle is unilaterally setting Ethereum's roadmap — in practice, during every major upgrade including the merge, Circle has followed the EF's direction because their interests are aligned. But if interests ever diverge, negotiation rather than forking is the only tool left.

Evgeny extends the argument: the more TradFi institutions settle on Ethereum, the more ultimate stakeholders there are whose interests must be managed, moving Ethereum further from a cypherpunk sanctuary toward a corporate governance model. Haseeb's response is that this is the correct outcome — if the rest of the world joins Ethereum, they all get a seat at the table, just as the internet's openness gave anyone access to any website. The vision of Ethereum was never cypherpunk rule; it was permissionless world infrastructure.

**The Drift Hack and a Bad Week for Cybersecurity**

Haseeb reports that Drift Protocol, a perpetuals DEX on Solana, was hacked for approximately $270 million — at the time of recording, one of the largest DeFi exploits in history. The stolen assets included over $150 million in JLP positions held in Drift's vault, roughly $50 million in USDC, and a smattering of other tokens. The attack vector was not yet publicly known.

The Drift hack landed during what Haseeb describes as an exceptionally bad week for cybersecurity broadly. The Axios NPM package — a ubiquitous HTTP library with 100 million weekly downloads and 175,000 dependent packages — was reportedly compromised through a North Korea-linked supply chain attack that pushed a remote access trojan through a maintainer's account. Separately, Mercore was hacked, and there was a Cloud Code-related leak. Anthropic also revealed it had developed a cybersecurity tool so capable that it is restricting access to white hats and open source maintainers rather than releasing it publicly. Panelists note that Anthropic's Nicholas Carlini — a longtime classical security researcher not given to hype — used a model harness to discover zero-days that had sat undetected in legacy code for decades, and treat his assessment as credible rather than aggrandized.

**AI Is Breaking the Open Source Trust Model**

Tarun and Haseeb argue that the combination of AI capabilities and the open source contribution model is becoming deeply unstable. The "underhanded C competition" — a long-running contest to hide malicious behavior in innocent-looking C code — was historically an extremely esoteric skill. Because years of competition submissions are in LLM training data, any model can now produce similar constructs on demand. A single popped maintainer account, or a fabricated identity that has been contributing to open source for years, can push a backdoor nearly impossible to detect in a standard code review. Tarun notes that the curl maintainer recently stopped accepting new PRs entirely after being flooded with bots — a sign that the contributions-from-anyone model is already straining.

Tarun draws a parallel to the late 1990s and early 2000s, when self-replicating worms hit Fortune 500 companies en masse before the industry hardened with containerization and restricted execution environments. The difference now is time: that hardening cycle took years, whereas the AI-accelerated threat offers essentially no reaction window. The current moment feels like a "speed run" of the same historical arc, but compressed into a period where the defensive capability cannot catch up.

The panel's rough consensus is that the naive open source model — where maintainers are assumed default-benevolent and anyone can push a PR — is probably ending. The likely outcome is that high-trust infrastructure packages migrate to foundations or large companies that underwrite maintainer organizations and no longer accept contributions from random accounts. The code may still be open, but it will be controlled by entities that are themselves underwritten and accountable. For crypto specifically, Haseeb predicts open-sourcing smart contracts may decrease as the asymmetry between attacker compute budgets — North Korea has allegedly spent upwards of $100 million pursuing $300 million prizes — and defender resources grows wider. One counterforce he notes: zero-knowledge proofs of code allow protocols to prove the absence of admin keys without revealing source, potentially enabling a model where contracts are closed-source but still auditable in limited ways.

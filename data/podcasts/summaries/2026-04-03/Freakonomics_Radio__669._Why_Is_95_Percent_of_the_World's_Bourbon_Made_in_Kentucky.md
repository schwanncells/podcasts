# Freakonomics Radio — "669. Why Is 95 Percent of the World's Bourbon Made in Kentucky?"

**Host:** Stephen Dubner. **Guests:** Ken Trotsky (labor economist and chair of the economics department, University of Kentucky; 20-year Kentucky resident who studies bourbon market economics); Brad Patrick (executive in residence and lecturer, University of Kentucky Gatton College of Business and Economics; Bourbon Fellow, James B. Beam Institute for Kentucky Spirits; consults with the bourbon industry); Danny Kahn (Master Distiller and Distillation and Aging Operations Director, Sazerac — overseeing Buffalo Trace, Barton 1792, and Collingwood Distillery); Andrew Muhammad (agricultural economist, University of Tennessee; two decades of research on trade issues across beer, wine, and spirits).

## TL;DR

- **95% of world bourbon is made within a 45-minute drive of Lexington, Kentucky** — the state now holds 16 million aging barrels, up from 4 million roughly 20 years ago, and has grown from about 6 distilleries to 120-plus.
- **Demand has been falling since 2022** — Jim Beam has paused production at its flagship distillery for a full year, and layoffs and consolidation are hitting other major producers, leaving 16 million barrels in warehouses and no clear exit.
- **Pappy Van Winkle 20-year illustrates the boom-to-bust cycle** — a bottle Trotsky's wife bought for $120 as a Christmas gift would now fetch $2,500–$3,000; during the boom, 65 cases were stolen from a warehouse with a black market value of $100,000.
- **Federal regulations require "straight bourbon" to be stored in new charred oak at 125 proof for at least 2 years** — a 1964 Congressional resolution gave bourbon protected status similar to Scotch or Champagne; master distiller Danny Kahn defends each rule on flavor grounds, but economist Andrew Muhammad calls the new-barrel requirement "a bit convenient" as soft protectionism.
- **The three-tier distribution system creates extreme price distortions** — Blanton's sells for $74 at the Buffalo Trace gift shop, $130 at a typical Kentucky liquor store, and $400 at a shop in La Jolla, California; the system dates to post-Prohibition efforts to limit mob influence and remains largely unchanged.
- **Tariffs are compounding the glut problem** — the EU imposed a 25% retaliatory tariff on American whiskey during the first Trump trade war; Canada simply pulled American products off shelves entirely, and Muhammad notes small craft distillers are least able to absorb those shocks.
- **Kentucky levied $75 million in barrel taxes in 2025** — an annual tax on aging bourbon as it sits in storage; the tax is being phased out and will be eliminated by 2043.
- **The industry's creative response is RTDs and tourism** — Sazerac acquired Buzz Balls (canned cocktails growing 20–25%), and the Kentucky Bourbon Trail has become a significant tourism draw; Patrick predicts consolidation similar to Scottish whisky, which went from 140-plus distilleries down to five major players.

**Why Kentucky?**

Trotsky and Kahn both trace Kentucky's dominance to a combination of infrastructure, tradition, and genuine geographic advantages rather than any single factor. Once a few distilleries established themselves, the region accumulated corn supply chains, cooperages, expertise, and warehouse capacity — creating high barriers to entry for new geographic competitors. Kahn points to Kentucky's water as genuinely distinctive: it flows through limestone caverns, picking up calcium and other minerals that matter for enzymatic reactions during cooking and for yeast functionality. Those minerals stay behind during distillation and do not carry over into the distillate, making Kentucky water a real production asset and not just marketing.

The concentration is not legally required. Bourbon does not have to be made in Kentucky — only in the United States. That legal structure has led Muhammad to observe some soft protectionism in the rules. The new charred oak barrel requirement, for instance, benefits barrel makers and copper and corn industries; meanwhile, the finest Scotch, Irish, and Japanese whiskeys are routinely aged in used bourbon barrels from Kentucky and Tennessee. Kahn defends each rule on flavor grounds and can explain the chemistry, but he does not directly address the industry lobbying history that shaped those rules.

**How Bourbon Is Made**

Kahn walks through the five legal requirements: at least 51% corn in the mash, distilled in the United States, distilled to no more than 160 proof, stored at no more than 125 proof, and stored in a new charred oak container. In practice, Sazerac distills to around 135–140 proof rather than the legal maximum of 160, because the lower proof retains more flavor compounds that become precursors to complexity during aging. Distilling to 160 and then diluting to 125 would produce a lighter, more neutral spirit.

The barrel production process is more elaborate than most drinkers realize. Oak staves are cut and stacked in the field for approximately a year — a step called seasoning — during which rainwater rinses out harsh tannins and microbial activity breaks down wood components in ways that produce important flavor precursors later. After seasoning, a cooperage assembles the barrel and applies intense direct flame for about 30 seconds, creating char. The char functions like a water filter, absorbing certain unwanted compounds, while the layer of cooked wood just below the char yields the extractable flavor components — caramel and butterscotch from hemicellulose, baking spices and vanilla from lignans, and mouthfeel and color from tannins. Different components are soluble at different alcohol levels, so the barrel entry proof (no more than 125) is a tool for manipulating which flavors get extracted.

**Time as a Product Attribute**

Muhammad frames time as an unusual economic input in bourbon: unlike nearly every other industry where time is merely an inconvenience to be minimized, bourbon consumers actually value age as a product attribute in itself. The industry's sweet spot is six to ten years; beyond ten years, some consumers find the oakiness too heavy. For ultra-aged expressions like 20-plus-year Pappy Van Winkle, very little liquid remains in the barrel due to evaporation — making it a premium product by scarcity as much as by taste.

Trotsky notes that bourbon barrels lose significant volume during aging — sometimes half or more — and that evaporation losses are severe enough that 23-year barrels may have only a small amount of liquid left. The market for used barrels, typically sold to Scotch, Irish, and Indian whiskey producers, is currently soft: Scotch is itself slowing down and buying fewer barrels than before, leaving Kentucky distilleries with a secondary revenue stream that is weakening at the same moment their primary one is under pressure.

**The Boom, the Glut, and the Bust**

Patrick describes the period from 2012 to 2021 as one of aggressive industry expansion driven by genuine consumer demand and optimism about the India and China export markets. Distilleries borrowed money, built capacity, and put barrels away to age four to eight years — only to find that by the time the bourbon was ready, consumer tastes had shifted. Gen Z is drinking less brown spirit; health and wellness concerns are reducing overall alcohol consumption; GLP-1 drugs may be playing a role; and the ready-to-drink canned cocktail market is growing at 20-plus percent, pulling consumers toward lighter, more convenient options. There are now 800 to 1,000 bourbon SKUs on the market — choice fatigue is a real factor, and some price-sensitive consumers have hit a ceiling.

Patrick draws a direct parallel to the Scottish whisky industry's overproduction crisis in the 1980s, which ultimately consolidated the sector from more than 140 distilleries to about five major players. He expects a similar shakeout in Kentucky. The most vulnerable businesses are contract distillers (producing bulk spirit for others to blend and brand) and private equity–backed distilleries carrying heavy debt; the survivors will be those with established consumer brands. Patrick is advising distilleries to hold off on major infrastructure investment but to build international market awareness so they are positioned when tariff situations resolve.

**Distribution Inefficiencies**

The three-tier system — producers cannot have financial relationships with distributors, and distributors cannot have financial relationships with retailers — was designed after Prohibition to prevent organized crime from controlling the alcohol supply chain. Today it produces significant inefficiencies. Trotsky describes a scenario where a Blanton's bottle that sells for $74 at the Buffalo Trace gift shop (a change made possible only after a 2022 regulatory update allowing distilleries to sell directly from their own gift shops) costs $130 at a typical Kentucky retailer and $400 in California. Arbitrage across state lines is illegal.

Distributor allocation power creates additional distortions: a distributor may offer a retailer access to sought-after allocated bourbons only if the retailer also moves volume in less desirable categories, such as Fireball. Trotsky notes that the distribution market is highly concentrated — two major companies dominate — though retail appears more competitive.

**Tariffs and Export Challenges**

Muhammad has spent decades studying trade in agricultural commodities and says tariff retaliation against bourbon is particularly effective because, unlike broad categories of goods, premium spirits depend on brand loyalty and market share that is hard to recover once lost. When the EU imposed its 25% retaliatory tariff on American whiskey during the first Trump trade war, some companies cut prices rather than raise them to preserve market position. Canada's response was blunter: American products simply came off the shelves.

Muhammad observes that the Kentucky bourbon industry has done strong global marketing and built genuine brand equity worldwide, but lacks the trade infrastructure and global agreements that Scotch has assembled over decades. Scotch is a $40 billion global business; bourbon is roughly $10–11 billion. Small craft distillers, Patrick agrees, are least able to weather tariff disruptions — they lack the internal financial tools that large multinational spirits companies use to manage currency and trade risk.

**What Comes Next**

Patrick sees two realistic growth vectors for the industry's oversupply: RTDs and tourism. Sazerac's acquisition of Buzz Balls — brightly colored, distinctively shaped canned cocktails growing 20–25% annually — represents the corporate adaptation strategy. Patrick believes RTDs will eventually level off but are worth pursuing for distilleries that already have bottling and canning infrastructure. The Kentucky Bourbon Trail, meanwhile, has become a serious tourism draw, and Patrick sees lifestyle, luxury, and events as areas where innovation is already happening and where distilleries can create direct consumer relationships that bypass some of the distribution system's inefficiencies.

Trotsky frames the current situation in terms of creative destruction: the destruction is visible and painful; the creative response has not yet fully emerged. With 16 million barrels aging across Kentucky, there will be no shortage of well-aged bourbon in the near future — the question is whether the industry can find enough buyers willing to drink it.

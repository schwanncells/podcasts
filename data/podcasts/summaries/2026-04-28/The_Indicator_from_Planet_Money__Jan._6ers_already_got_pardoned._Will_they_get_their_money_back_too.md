# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Jan. 6ers already got pardoned. Will they get their money back too?"

**Host:** Veda Emanuel and Wailin Wong. **Guests:** Frank Bowman (law professor emeritus, University of Missouri; pardon power scholar); Cy Prather (law professor, University of Virginia; author on pardon history).

## TL;DR

- **~1,500 January 6th defendants pardoned in 2025** — Many had paid fines ranging from $500 to $2,000 toward Capitol repair, totaling over $400,000, and now seek refunds after receiving full pardons.
- **A Civil War precedent blocks refunds from Treasury** — An 1860s case involving Confederate soldier John Note established that once fines are in the U.S. Treasury, the Constitution requires Congressional appropriation to refund them—the president cannot unilaterally return Treasury money.
- **Appellate limbo creates an escape hatch** — Many January 6th defendants had appealed their convictions before Trump's pardons; because their convictions were vacated while appeals were pending, courts say they never completed the judicial process, so the government's right to keep the money is questionable.
- **The judgment fund unlocks refunds despite Treasury limits** — Congress created the judgment fund in 1956 to avoid repeated appropriations. Since January 6th defendants' convictions were voided, courts argue their fines were "improperly retained" and can be paid back from the judgment fund (permanent appropriation), bypassing the separation-of-powers block.
- **Refunds require appealing conviction at time of pardon** — Cy Prather notes that only defendants who had active appeals when pardoned qualify; others are out of luck, though some have filed a class action for $18 million in damages against Capitol police.
- **Congressional legislation to block refunds appears dead** — Some members introduced bills to prevent judgment fund payouts to January 6th defendants, but they have little chance of passing; a decision from the DC Appeals Court is expected this summer.

**What a Pardon Does—and Doesn't Do**

Frank Bowman explains that a presidential pardon is a powerful but limited tool. It wipes out the consequences of a conviction—primarily releasing someone from prison, but also nullifying financial penalties like fines. However, the pardon does not erase the fact of conviction itself; it simply eliminates the legal fallout. In the January 6th cases, many defendants paid substantial fines (ranging from $500 to $2,000), with over $400,000 collectively going to Capitol repair accounts in the U.S. Treasury. After receiving full pardons, these defendants have sued to recover their money.

**The Civil War Precedent and Treasury Constraints**

Cy Prather traces the legal obstacle back to the 1860s, when former Confederates who received pardons from Lincoln or Andrew Johnson sought to reclaim confiscated property. One such soldier, John Note, sued the U.S. Treasury and the case reached the Supreme Court. The Court acknowledged that pardons eliminate conviction consequences, but it also recognized a practical problem: who actually holds the money? When funds are in the Treasury, the Constitution stipulates that Treasury money must be appropriated by law—a separation-of-powers issue. Congress, not the president, controls Treasury appropriations. As a result, the Court sided with the government: the Confederate petitioner was denied refund, because the money was off-limits to the executive.

**The Appellate Limbo Advantage**

A key difference in the January 6th cases involves timing and jurisdiction. Many defendants had appealed their original convictions before Trump's 2025 pardons, but their appellate courts had not yet ruled. When Trump pardoned them while their cases were in legal limbo, their convictions were vacated—a legal term meaning voided, as if they had never existed. Prather explains that this creates a critical distinction: because these defendants never completed the judicial process (their appeals were interrupted by the pardon), the government's claim to retain their fines is weakened. If the conviction itself was only provisional or incomplete, the financial penalty loses its justification.

**The Judgment Fund as a Workaround**

Prather describes how Congress sidestepped this historical headache by creating the judgment fund in 1956. Rather than pass thousands of individual appropriations each time the U.S. lost a lawsuit, Congress created a standing pool of money, permanently appropriated, for paying judgments against the government. One judge handling the refund cases reasoned that since the January 6th defendants' convictions were voided, the fines were "improperly retained"—the only original justification (the conviction) no longer exists. Therefore, the government can use the judgment fund to refund the money, effectively sidestepping the Civil War-era separation-of-powers block. Prather considers this outcome likely, but notes that the DC Appeals Court could still overturn it.

**Conditions and Alternatives**

Prather emphasizes that the refund pathway has a specific condition: defendants must have been appealing their conviction at the time of the pardon. Those who pleaded guilty without appeal, or whose appellate deadline had already passed, will not qualify for refunds from the judgment fund. However, many January 6th defendants have filed a class action lawsuit seeking over $18 million in damages for injuries they claim were caused by Capitol police. A settlement or verdict would come from the judgment fund as well—another use of taxpayer money. Some members of Congress have introduced legislation to bar judgment fund payouts to January 6th defendants altogether, but Prather views such legislation as unlikely to pass.

**The Broader Principle**

Bowman and Prather discuss whether the Capitol attack—a crime of political violence and election subversion—might warrant different treatment than ordinary financial crimes. Prather expresses skepticism: "All bets are pretty much off if you're pardoned of a crime. If you can get your money back for committing financial fraud and for other sorts of offenses, it's not clear why riots or, you know, election subversion should be different." The episode closes with the note that a final court decision is expected sometime this summer, leaving the fines, cases, and refunds in continued limbo.

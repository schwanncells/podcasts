# The Ezra Klein Show — "What We Got Right — and Wrong — in 'Abundance'"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guests:** Derek Thompson (contributing writer, The Atlantic; co-author, *Abundance*), Mark Dunkelman (fellow, Searchlight Institute and Brown University; author, *Why Nothing Works*).

## TL;DR

- **The concept of abundance has achieved "0.1 percentile" cultural penetration** — governors Kathy Hochul and JB Pritzker now frame energy and housing solutions around supply-side policy, and the Democratic Party has largely adopted the vocabulary and framing.
- **Legislation is mixed: B+ grade on explicit bills, declining returns on California housing law** — California signed "Abundant and Affordable Homes Near Transit Act," but decades of housing bills in California haven't reliably increased supply or reduced rents.
- **Austin built dramatically and rents fell** — Austin constructed thousands of homes in the 2010s-2020s and saw average rents decline consistently, providing evidence that supply expansion works when deployed at scale.
- **Eric Adams pivoted from rent freeze messaging to supply expansion** — New York's mayor paired rent control rhetoric with developer-support policies and launched the "Neighborhood Builders Fasttrack" program to accelerate construction.
- **Power structures may matter more than policy design** — Dunkelman argues the "corporate power" critique progressives emphasize has validity; abundance thinking doesn't address which actors control what gets built, when, and where.
- **Populism vs. abundance liberalism is the deeper divide among progressives** — The disagreement isn't primarily about rent control or regulation but about whether corporate/institutional power or scarcity is the root problem to solve.

---

**The One-Year Check-In on Abundance**

Ezra Klein brought Derek Thompson and Mark Dunkelman together to assess what has and hasn't changed since Klein and Thompson published *Abundance* just over a year prior. The three discussed the book's reception across three registers: vibes (cultural messaging), legislation (bills passed), and outcomes (actual results on housing, energy, and infrastructure).

Thompson framed the vibes-level success as dramatically exceeding his expectations. The concept of abundance—the idea that progressive solutions should focus on expanding supply rather than restricting markets—has penetrated political discourse at the level of governors, senators, and congressional leaders. The Democratic Party, in particular, has absorbed the vocabulary and its core argument: look at problems, expand supply, solve them on the supply side. Democratic politicians no longer obscure this framework; they explicitly cite abundance when championing housing and energy policy.

Klein pushed back on this optimism, citing California's experience as cautionary. Despite dozens of housing bills framed as transformative, passed over a decade, California's rents and housing costs have not consistently fallen. Thompson acknowledged this discrepancy but pointed to Austin as a counterexample: Austin built an enormous number of homes in the 2010s and early 2020s, and average rents consistently declined. The difference, he suggested, may hinge on scale and political will to remove obstacles.

**California, Austin, and the Question of Policy Effectiveness**

Dunkelman added nuance to the California debate. He noted that laws often contain loopholes or face implementation barriers that hollow them of practical effect. The question is not merely whether a bill exists but whether it actually enables construction. California's housing legislation, measured by outcomes, has underperformed relative to the boldness of the rhetoric surrounding it. Austin, by contrast, deployed permitting reforms and zoning changes with far less resistance and achieved measurable results.

Klein introduced a framework from Dunkelman's book *Why Nothing Works*: understanding housing politics requires asking who decides what gets built, when, and where. This is fundamentally a question of power. Progressives focused on corporate power argue that institutional gatekeepers—developers, landlords, NIMBYs, regulators—have captured the system. Abundance thinking, they contend, assumes supply expansion will help if bureaucratic barriers fall, but ignores who controls supply chains and development decisions.

**Eric Adams, the Populist Pivot, and Policy Messaging**

The group discussed Eric Adams' shift in New York City. Adams campaigned partly on rent freezes—a populist, price-control frame—but has also pursued supply-side solutions and now leads a program called the "Neighborhood Builders Fasttrack" to accelerate development. This dual messaging appears contradictory but reflects a broader recognition among Democratic politicians that both rent control and supply acceleration are politically necessary. Klein noted that polling data suggests voters hear "rent freeze" far more than "accelerate development," suggesting messaging asymmetry in Adams' communications.

Thompson observed that this dynamic—pairing price controls with supply expansion—reveals a political truth: abundance thinking alone is insufficient to win elections. Politicians must address both the perception of fairness (controlling prices, standing up to corporate landlords) and the practical need to build more housing. The tension between these two messages remains unresolved in Democratic politics.

**Beauty, Regulation, and the Depth of the Housing Problem**

Klein and Thompson revisited an argument from their housing chapter: anger in the 1960s and 1970s that America was becoming uglier—the rise of "ticky-tacky" suburban sprawl and declining aesthetic quality in cities. Thompson suggested that allowing rapid, flexible construction could restore beauty to urban environments. Dunkelman expressed skepticism, arguing that beautifying cities requires more than deregulation; it demands proactive design standards, planning, and investment. Beauty does not emerge simply from removing constraints; it requires positive choices about what to build and where. This disagreement highlighted a limitation of pure abundance thinking: it can remove obstacles but does not guarantee outcomes beyond lowering prices and increasing quantity.

**Corporate Power and the Limits of Abundance Framework**

The deepest tension emerged around corporate power. Dunkelman and, to a degree, Klein pressed Thompson on whether abundance thinking adequately addresses the progressive concern that large corporations—landlords, tech billionaires, construction conglomerates—shape markets in their favor. Thompson acknowledged that those critical of corporate power have valid points: institutional actors do have outsized influence. However, he argued that the constraint progressives most underestimate is scarcity itself. Even in markets dominated by large firms, expanding supply creates options and competition, which benefits consumers.

Thompson noted that Reed Hoffman, a tech billionaire, has been circulating the *Abundance* book in Silicon Valley, which raised questions about whether the framework risks legitimizing the interests of wealthy builders and landlords. The conversation did not resolve whether supply expansion, even if successful, primarily benefits consumers or primarily benefits the developers and asset holders who profit from growth.

**Procedural Reform and the Populist Critique**

Klein introduced Nick Bagley's concept of "procedure fetish"—the idea that progressives obsess over process and delay as features rather than bugs. Thompson and others disagreed, arguing that progressives pursue procedural reforms not because they enjoy them but because they distrust concentrated power and want checks on it. However, Dunkelman suggested that some procedural complexity may reflect legitimate concerns about externalities and fairness. The group acknowledged a genuine divide: abundance thinking prioritizes speed and deregulation; populist progressivism prioritizes accountability and constraints on power.

**Looking Forward**

The three agreed that abundance thinking has successfully shifted Democratic rhetoric and some policy priorities. However, whether the framework will deliver outcomes at scale—cheaper housing, faster energy infrastructure, more livable cities—remains uncertain. California's mixed results suggest that legislative victory and actual supply expansion are not synonymous. The next phase of the debate, they suggested, involves whether abundance-inspired policies can overcome entrenched interests and implementation barriers to meaningfully expand housing and infrastructure supply.

# Throughline — "The fight that shook America"

**Host:** Rund Abdelfatah. **Guests:** Dave Zirin (award-winning sports journalist, host of Edge of Sports radio show; expert on sports and social activism); Dr. Harry Edwards (professor, UC Berkeley; author of *The Revolt of the Black Athlete*; leading scholar of Black athlete activism); Amira Rose Davis (professor, Penn State; co-host of feminist sports podcast Burn It All Down); Jules Boykoff (professor, Pacific University; author of books on sports history).

## TL;DR

- **Jack Johnson defeated Tommy Burns on December 26, 1908** in Sydney, Australia, in round 14, becoming the first Black heavyweight boxing champion despite an unspoken rule prohibiting Black fighters from championship bouts in the heavyweight division.
- **The fight was a racial spectacle** — local media predicted a "race war" and newspapers explicitly tied the outcome to racial hierarchy; Jack London and other writers called for a "great white hope" to beat Johnson and "remove the golden smile off of Jack Johnson's face."
- **Johnson's victories sparked race riots** — after he beat former champion Jim Jeffries in 1910, celebrations by Black Americans were met with violent white retaliation that killed countless people across multiple major US cities.
- **Johnson refused to accept racial constraints** — he famously dated white women openly, wore furs, drove nice cars, and traveled to watch white boxers' fights to taunt them into fighting him; this defiance made him "public enemy number one."
- **The Mann Act was weaponized against Johnson** — a federal law nominally about trafficking women across state lines was used to prosecute him for traveling with his white girlfriend (later wife); he was convicted by an all-white jury in 1913 and sentenced to one year in jail.
- **Johnson fled the country and went into exile** — after jumping bail, he lived outside the US for approximately 7 years, during which his career was destroyed; he never regained his dominance in boxing, and there would not be another Black heavyweight champion for 22 years.
- **Trump pardoned Johnson in 2018** — more than 72 years after Johnson died, President Donald Trump used his pardon power to clear Johnson's Mann Act conviction, gathering boxer Deontay Wilder and actor Sylvester Stallone for the announcement in the Oval Office.

**The Port City Boy Who Saw the World Early**

Jack Johnson was born in Galveston, Texas in 1878, the son of two formerly enslaved people. As a young man, he worked on the docks of this port community, where he encountered sailors and traders from the Middle East, sub-Saharan Africa, and Europe. This early exposure to the wider world shaped his fearlessness and his understanding that there was more to life than what Texas could offer. Journalists and historians note that Johnson, unlike many of his peers, possessed a global perspective before international travel was common.

As a teenager, Johnson took a job as a janitor at a gym, where he met German-born boxing trainer Herman Bernau and began his training. Within a few years, he started fighting in prize fights and quickly excelled. By 1903, he had become the colored heavyweight champion of the world—but this accomplishment was not enough. Johnson was determined to fight for the true heavyweight championship, held by white fighters.

**Breaking the Unspoken Rule**

There was a "gentleman's agreement" among boxing promoters and fighters that no Black boxer would be allowed to fight for a heavyweight championship. Johnson, however, devised a strategy to force the issue. He would appear ringside at championship fights between white boxers, buy a front-row seat, and spend the entire bout trash-talking the champion. This next-level trolling, as contemporaries describe it, was an extraordinarily bold move for a Black man in the early 20th century. He focused this harassment especially on the Canadian heavyweight champion Tommy Burns, traveling to Burns's fights around the world to continue the psychological campaign.

The strategy worked. In 1908, Burns agreed to fight Johnson for $30,000—a substantial sum at the time—with an Australian promoter putting up the purse. The fight was scheduled for December 26, 1908, in Sydney, Australia. The stakes, however, extended far beyond the title. A local Sydney newspaper ran an ominous headline: "This battle may in future be looked back upon as the first great battle of an inevitable race war. There is more in this fight to be considered than the mere title of pugilistic champion of the world."

**The Historic Fight and Media Reaction**

Nearly 20,000 people packed Sydney Stadium to witness the bout. From the opening round, Johnson's physical advantages were evident—he was significantly taller than Burns at 212 pounds, and he controlled the fight with ease. Johnson did not simply beat Burns; he dominated and humiliated him, calmly talking to him throughout, taunting the champion and making clear that this was no contest but rather what he called "a picnic." The abuse continued through the rounds, with Johnson landing punishing combinations while Burns appeared helpless.

In round 14, Johnson unleashed a barrage of left hooks and punches that left Burns unable to continue. At this moment, the police entered the ring and stopped the fight, awarding the heavyweight championship to Jack Johnson. The law enforcement intervention itself was revealing—they would not even allow the fight to conclude naturally.

The response in the American media was immediate and visceral. Jack London, the famous novelist, captured the outrage of white America, writing that "a great white hope should come along and remove the golden smile off of Jack Johnson's face" and that "the white man must be rescued." Newspapers across the country began calling for a "great white hope"—a white boxer capable of defeating Johnson and restoring white supremacy in the ring.

**The Racial Spectacle**

Journalists and scholars emphasize that Johnson's fights became more than sporting events; they were understood as racial battlegrounds. The media and public explicitly mapped racial meaning onto every bout. Amira Rose Davis notes that newspaper headlines reflected a fear that if Johnson won, Black Americans across the country would riot and revolt, would gain the dangerous idea that they could fight back, and would question their supposed racial inferiority. Each of Johnson's victories against white fighters carried implications far beyond boxing.

Johnson embraced his role as a symbol, though not necessarily willingly. He was a showman and a trash-talker, and he backed up his words in the ring. He went on to defeat a series of "white hopes," culminating in his 1910 victory over the great former champion Jim Jeffries. The New York Times coverage of the Jeffries fight stated plainly: "If the black man wins, thousands and thousands of his ignorant brothers will misinterpret his victory as justifying claims to much more than mere physical equality with their white neighbors."

The night after the Jeffries fight, Black Americans celebrated in the streets of many major American cities. White America's response was violence. Race riots erupted, and countless people died as white mobs attacked Black communities and celebrations.

**Johnson's Personal Rebellion**

Ironically, Johnson never asked to be a racial symbol. In his own philosophy, he said he found no better way to avoid racial prejudice than to act with people of other races "as if prejudice did not exist." But this strategy—a kind of radical colorblindness—was impossible in a society built on racial hierarchy. Prejudice absolutely existed, and Johnson's very existence became an affront to white supremacy.

More fundamentally, Johnson simply wanted to live his life to the fullest, exactly how he wanted. He was a prototype of the modern star athlete—wealthy, brash, smooth-talking, and tough. He drove expensive cars, wore fine furs, and most provocatively, he dated white women openly and flagrantly. He would walk into hotels in St. Louis and, when rejected for being Black, respond with withering insouciance: "It's not for me. It's for my lady and she's not my kind, she's your kind." He was then escorted out of town.

This refusal to defer or submit to racial norms made him increasingly dangerous in the eyes of white America and the government.

**Legal Persecution and Exile**

The federal government moved against Johnson with extraordinary force. The Mann Act, passed in 1910, made it illegal to transport women across state lines for "immoral purposes." In the context of the early 20th century, interracial relationships were classified as immoral. Johnson, as a prizefighter, naturally traveled state to state for fights, and he did so with his girlfriend, who was white and later became his wife.

A warrant was put out for his arrest, and Johnson was eventually brought to trial. An all-white jury convicted him in 1913, and he was sentenced to one year in jail. Rather than serve his sentence, Johnson jumped bail, fled to Canada (where he briefly posed as a member of a Black baseball team), and then lived in exile in Europe and South America for approximately 7 years.

Those seven years of exile devastated his boxing career. He lost his heavyweight title and never regained the dominance he had once possessed. There would not be another Black heavyweight champion for 22 years after Johnson's reign ended.

**Later Life and Pardon**

Johnson eventually returned to the United States and served his sentence. He lived the remainder of his life under the shadow of his Mann Act conviction. It was not until 2018, more than 72 years after Johnson's death, that President Donald Trump gathered members of the boxing community, including champion boxer Deontay Wilder and actor Sylvester Stallone, in the Oval Office to grant Johnson a presidential pardon for his Mann Act conviction.

Today, Johnson is often remembered not as he saw himself—as a man simply living his life—but as a pioneer of athlete activism. Though Johnson did not identify as an activist or racial symbol, his battles inside and outside the ring struck a significant blow against the ideology of white supremacy that dominated the turn of the 20th century. He became, whether intentionally or not, the father of the first wave of athlete activism, inspiring generations of Black athletes who followed to use their platforms to challenge racism and injustice.

# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Your American Dream is pending review"

**Host:** Julia Ritchie, Adrian Ma. **Guests:** Immigration attorney Mauricio Garcia (handles green card adjustment cases); anonymous green card applicants (chemistry teacher on track for tenure, engineer, tech worker, U.S. citizen with Brazilian mother).

## TL;DR

- **Trump administration policy: aliens must return home to apply for green cards** — announced "from now on, an alien who is in the U.S. temporarily and wants a green card must return to their home country to apply, except in extraordinary circumstances."
- **Policy backpedaled after business lobby pushback** — USCIS issued vague statement that policy "will have no noticeable impact on highly qualified applicants and skilled professionals who have followed the law."
- **Criteria for who stays unclear** — USCIS said "skilled professionals who provide economic benefits" won't be affected and mentioned prioritizing immigration that "strengthens America culturally, socially, and financially, while preventing mass third-world migration"; refused to clarify what "economic benefits" or "third world" means.
- **Applicants trapped in limbo report extreme anxiety** — people with pending green card applications (some on tenure track, married, with children) fear job loss, forced relocation, deportation of family members, and losing years of investment in their American lives.
- **Immigration lawyers expect significant litigation** — Mauricio Garcia says "almost every immigration attorney I know" expects the administration to lose lawsuits because the policy pushes "the bounds of the law" and will cause applicants to "lose significant immigration benefits."
- **Policy shift undermines immigrants' faith in U.S.** — one applicant noted "freedom is quite a big factor" in coming here but "freedom of expression, freedom of multiculturalism is kind of fading"; another said "the U.S. claims to be" the American dream but "is very happy to have my labor, but is not very happy to have me."

**The Administration's Policy Shift and Initial Backlash**

In early June, the Trump administration announced a sweeping green card policy requiring applicants currently in the U.S. on temporary visas to return to their home countries to apply for permanent residency—with only narrow exceptions. Within weeks, facing strong pushback from the business community, the administration walked back the announcement. USCIS released a statement saying the policy would have "no noticeable impact on highly qualified applicants and skilled professionals." However, the agency did not explain how it would distinguish between those who could stay and those forced to leave. When pressed on what "highly qualified" and "skilled professionals" mean, and what the phrase "preventing mass third-world migration" means, USCIS declined to provide further comment.

**The Human Cost: Green Card Applicants in Limbo**

The episode featured anonymous interviews with several applicants caught in the uncertainty. A chemistry teacher on track for tenure described the moment she heard the initial announcement: she almost had a panic attack and began calculating logistics like where she'd live if forced to leave, whether she'd lose her house and mortgage, and how a temporary departure would affect her daughter's schooling. A U.S. citizen originally from Brazil worried that her mother—who came on a temporary visa to help care for her newborn but has since fallen out of legal status because the green card application took too long—could face detention and deportation rather than approval of her application. An engineer expressed that while freedom initially drew him to the U.S., "freedom of expression, freedom of multiculturalism is kind of fading." A tech worker said it feels impossible to reconcile the American dream with a policy that welcomes his labor but rejects his presence.

**Legal Challenges Ahead**

Immigration attorney Mauricio Garcia, who handles green card adjustment cases, said the announcement sent his office into chaos: "every single client that we have pending with an adjustment of status was calling in, freaking out." He noted that while immigration policy does shift between administrations—citing Biden-era leniency on interviews—he believes the Trump administration's new green card policy overreaches legally. Garcia expects "significant litigation" because applicants will "lose significant immigration benefits" as a result, and the policy likely violates immigration law. He acknowledged that policy shifts are normal (Biden required fewer interviews; Trump tightened that) but argued this latest move crosses a line.

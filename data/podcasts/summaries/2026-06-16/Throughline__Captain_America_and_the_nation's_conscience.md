# Throughline — "Captain America and the nation's conscience"

**Host:** Randa Abdel Fattah. **Guests:** Danny Fingeroth (author, *A Marvelous Life: The Amazing Story of Stan Lee*); Steve Englehart (comic book writer, Captain America comics 1970s).

- **Captain America introduced in 1941 as symbol of American identity** — blonde-haired, blue-eyed white super soldier created on eve of WWII who fights only when necessary, armed only with shield, representing ideals of standing up to bullies and fighting for justice.

- **Fans debated Cap's values through published letters** — by late 1960s, Marvel printed reader letters debating "who Captain America should be and what he should stand for," with controversy centered on whether Cap represented past-oriented patriotism or could evolve with social movements (civil rights, women's rights, Vietnam War).

**Captain America's Origins and WWII Role**

Captain America was first introduced to comic readers in 1941, depicted as a blonde-haired, blue-eyed white super soldier created through an experimental serum given to a scrawny, unfit man named Steve Rogers. His costume is red, white, and blue with a giant white star on his chest — he essentially wears the American flag. Unlike other superheroes, he has only one superhuman power: strength. He carries no weapon, only a shield, and fights only when he must. His defining characteristic is that he knows what it is like to be the one getting sand kicked in his face; he was not born into power, making him someone on the side of the little guy.

During World War II, Captain America fought Nazi saboteurs and Japanese soldiers, epitomizing American power and identity. Beyond his physical abilities, Cap's moral clarity became his defining feature. In the Marvel universe, he is the character other heroes look to when they do not know what is the right thing to do. As a cultural figure, many people want to be Captain America or believe in him because he represents America as many want it to be: a place that stands up to bullies, knows right from wrong, and calls out injustice.

**The Post-War Identity Crisis**

After World War II, when the United States emerged as a global superpower, Captain America faced a crisis. Without a clear enemy worthy of the character, he became directionless. Danny Fingeroth, who wrote *A Marvelous Life: The Amazing Story of Stan Lee*, explains that "Captain America, without a big war the country is involved in, almost becomes directionless." Stan Lee, one of the most well-known comic book writers in history, struggled to find an appropriate enemy for Captain America, and the character essentially disappeared from comic books in the 1950s. Ten years later, in 1964, Stan Lee brought Captain America back, but the timing could not have been worse. All of a sudden, this American war hero found himself in the middle of the civil rights movement, the women's rights movement, and the Vietnam War. Cap became what one writer describes as a "Hamlet kind of character, just always with his hands stapled to his forehead in grief and anguish," saying "I don't belong in this age, in this year, no place for me."

This tension sparked what became known as the patriotism-centered controversy. By the late 1960s, Marvel was printing letters from fans inside the comic books where they debated who Captain America should be and what he should stand for. Some argued his roots belonged in the past, not the present. Others countered that only a dreamer could think the world is safe and peaceful. Stan Lee saw that his audience was changing and faced a business dilemma: take a stand on an issue and potentially lose half the audience, or stay silent.

**Steve Englehart's Reimagining During Watergate**

The next generation of Captain America writers took over and pushed beyond Stan Lee's cautious approach. Steve Englehart, a conscientious objector during the Vietnam War, became the writer of Captain America Comics in the 1970s. Englehart began to wonder if the writers before him went wrong by trying to equate Captain America with the American government or military. Instead, he thought: what if Cap stood for American ideals — "the stuff that transcends whatever America's doing at this particular time"?

When the Watergate scandal broke, Englehart became hooked on the story, as was most of America, and asked himself: "If I were Captain America, what would I do?" He wrote "The Secret Empire," a storyline that parodied the scandal. He recast Nixon's right-hand man Haldeman as a character called Harderman, an advertising guy, and created the "Committee to Regain America's Principles" (CRAP) to mirror Nixon's "Committee to Re-elect the President" (CREEP). Without saying it explicitly in the comic, the head of the secret empire is Richard Nixon.

In the story, Captain America discovers ads being run that claim he is a vigilante not to be trusted. He gets thrown in jail but breaks out and chases the head of the secret empire into the White House and the Oval Office. When Cap wins, he is broken. To capture that disillusionment, Englehart wrote that Steve Rogers stops being Captain America and instead becomes a superhero called Nomad because "he doesn't want to stand for the America that he just saw."

Less than a year later, Steve Rogers returned to being Captain America. The key insight was that he could stand for ideals even if the president was a crook. Englehart had forever changed the idea of what patriotism could mean to Captain America: that he could love his country and also not love what his government was doing.

**Cap's Evolving Role in Contemporary America**

The mutating identity that Englehart established became constant as new writers came in. The iterations that followed have often been a response to the moment they are born in. Cap has been more militant, fighting terrorism after 9/11. He was critical of Guantanamo Bay. There have been Black Captain Americas and a Native American Captain America. In the latest Captain America movie, Cap teams up with a superhero originally named Sabra, an agent for Israel's National Intelligence Agency, Mossad. After getting backlash, the studio decided to call her Ruth and had her work instead for the United States.

These debates about what Captain America should stand for, who he should fight for, and what is right or wrong are part of the ongoing conversation America continues to have about who it is and who it wants to be. They ask a fundamental question: if we were Captain America, what would we do?

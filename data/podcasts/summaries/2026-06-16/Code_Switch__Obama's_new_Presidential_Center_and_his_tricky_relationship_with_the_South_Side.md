# Code Switch — "Obama's new Presidential Center and his tricky relationship with the South Side"

**Hosts:** Gene Demby and B.A. Parker. **Guests:** Natalie Moore (Chicago journalist, editor, and Northwestern University journalism instructor; covered the Obama Center since before site selection) and Myra Kwaja (writer, educator, and multimedia producer; 10 years at the Invisible Institute, where she ran a youth journalism program at Hyde Park Academy — the school directly across the street from the Center).

## TL;DR

- **The Obama Presidential Center opens on Juneteenth** — the $850 million campus in Jackson Park includes a museum, public library branch, state-of-the-art playground, gardens, a lagoon, and a sledding hill Michelle Obama had built because she never got to sled as a kid growing up on the South Side.
- **Hyde Park Academy sits directly across the street** — the under-resourced high school recently saw students walk out after three students died in one month and community support services were cut; the playground students used was demolished to build the Center's basketball court.
- **Woodlawn home prices have jumped 4.6 times in 10 years** — median single-family home sale prices in the neighborhood, which residents and organizers feared would be displaced, now show million-dollar listings on Zillow.
- **Obama rejected a Community Benefits Agreement** in a 2017 community meeting, telling organizers "there's no community organization that speaks for all the community" — a jarring move, Kwaja says, from a man who built his political identity as a community organizer.
- **The city, not the University of Chicago, owns most of the vacant land** in East Woodlawn — a 10-year organizing campaign led by the late Maddie Butler argued displacement wasn't inevitable because there was enough vacant city-owned property for everyone.
- **A white-led group called Protect Our Parks repeatedly sued to block construction** on the grounds that parkland shouldn't be privatized — a framing Moore calls "a very intentional, curious choice" from a group that doesn't otherwise advocate for equity.
- **Preview visitors report crying, feeling "the promise of 2015"** — Moore says the museum opens not with the Obamas but with suffragists, labor organizers, and the Black Panthers, aiming for visitors to leave feeling like they can still do something; the word "Trump" never appears.

**The Center and its neighbors**

The Obama Presidential Center occupies Jackson Park on the South Side of Chicago, straddling wealthy Hyde Park on one side and lower-income Woodlawn on the other. Kwaja notes that Hyde Park Academy, a large under-resourced public school where she ran a youth journalism program for years, stands directly across Stony Island Avenue. Just last month, students there walked out to protest after three students died in one month and the school cut community support services. The playground they used was demolished when the Center built its basketball court, and Kwaja says she hopes the new state-of-the-art playground on the Center's campus will be genuinely accessible to those students.

The neighborhood carries deep historical weight. Woodlawn is where Lorraine Hansberry's father bought the house that inspired *A Raisin in the Sun*. It was also the endpoint of the Great Migration, and Moore describes Chicago's South Side as "the capital of Black America." Obama came to it as a young organizer working through questions of identity; he met Michelle Obama there; and a sitting U.S. president emerged from it. Both Kwaja and Moore grew up in or near the neighborhood and, while Obama was in the White House, lived on the same block as the Obama family home on Greenwood Avenue.

**The organizing campaign and the CBA fight**

From nearly the moment in 2017 the Jackson Park site was announced, housing advocates raised alarms about displacement. A decade-long campaign led by organizer Maddie Butler — who died before the Center opened — insisted the project could proceed without displacement because so much of East Woodlawn was composed of vacant lots owned by the city, not by the University of Chicago as many feared. "There is enough room for everybody," Butler argued. The campaign's consistent message: yes to the Obama Center, no to displacement.

The central flashpoint came when organizers asked Obama directly whether the Foundation would sign a Community Benefits Agreement providing housing protections. Obama declined. "Michelle and I feel very confident in our ability to make sure that we have a very inclusive process where everybody has their say," he said, before effectively telling organizers he knew what was best. Kwaja calls the moment jarring: "He would have been one of the people maybe on the other side of this in a different lifetime."

**Housing reality a decade in**

The displacement fears have materialized, at least in the data. The Illinois Answers Project found that median single-family home sale prices in Woodlawn increased 4.6 times over the past decade, with million-dollar listings now appearing on Zillow. Moore is careful not to overstate the dynamics — she pushes back on characterizations that the Obamas are trying to push Black people out, and notes that gentrification, as a term, "often does not happen in Black neighborhoods" in the classic white-displacement-of-color sense. But she acknowledges that citywide rents are high, the housing crisis is real, and "if you do feel left behind in this country or this city and neighborhood, I understand that more protections are needed." Moore says the Foundation largely deflected the housing concerns to the city, which actually owned most of the relevant land.

**Architecture and the "Obamalisk"**

Detractors have nicknamed the Center's tower the "Obamalisk." Architecture critics were divided before construction finished, and the building can look like either a beacon or a foreboding monolith depending on where you stand. Moore, who attended the press-day preview, says the overall campus is beautiful — winding paths, gardens, a lagoon, Lake Michigan visible on the other side — and draws a comparison to the Picasso statue in Daley Plaza, which Chicagoans hated when it was installed and now love. "I do think over time, we might see some different opinions on how this space looks."

**Policing and public access**

Both guests flag the question of who will actually be able to use the space. Natalie Moore notes that Chicago's lakefront parks already carry a heavy police presence, and Kwaja says the police presence in Hyde Park and the surrounding beaches has "dramatically intensified as the weather has gotten warmer." She describes a stark pattern of surveillance of groups of Black teenagers simply gathering. The Center is nominally open public space, but, as Moore puts it: "What I am interested in seeing is what does that public look like?"

**Political valence and the 2026 moment**

The Center opens on Juneteenth 2026 against a backdrop Moore describes plainly: "the vibes are trash." Democratic voters are angry at both Trump and Democrats in Washington, and the sharpest critiques of the Obama presidency are now coming from the left. Moore wrote after the press preview about what it means to have a museum dedicated to democracy "when democracy is falling apart." Yet preview visitors reportedly cried, describing the experience as feeling like "the promise of 2015" — a stark contrast to where things stand now.

The museum is deliberately structured to extend beyond Obama: it opens with the suffragist movement, the labor movement, and the Black Panthers before arriving at the Obama story, with the explicit aim of leaving visitors feeling like they can still act. The word "Trump" never appears. Kwaja adds a caution that has animated both guests throughout: any critical framing of the Center risks being weaponized by people who simply hate Obama, and the organizers' careful "yes Obama Center, no displacement" message exists precisely to prevent the two strands of opposition from being conflated.

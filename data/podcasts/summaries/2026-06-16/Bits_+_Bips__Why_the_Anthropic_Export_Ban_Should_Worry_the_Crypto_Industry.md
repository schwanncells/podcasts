# Bits + Bips — "Why the Anthropic Export Ban Should Worry the Crypto Industry"

**Host:** Austin Campbell (Zero Knowledge Group). **Guests:** Ram Alawalia (principal, Lumina; investor); Chris Perkins (partner, 250 Digital; national security background); Sam Enzer (partner, Cahill Next; crypto regulatory attorney).

## TL;DR

- **Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models disabled worldwide** — U.S. government issued export control directive citing national security concerns over a jailbreaking vulnerability; Anthropic refused to fix the exploit on Dario's decision, forcing complete model recall.
- **No public disclosure of government authority or substantive case** — The Commerce Department's letter and statutory/regulatory basis remain unreleased; speakers argue this contradicts due process and free speech precedent around code as protected speech.
- **Export controls now apply to APIs, setting precedent for future sweeps** — If API access from foreign nationals counts as "export," the government could theoretically regulate any U.S.-based server or technology company, creating a playbook for abuse.
- **Regulation breeds consolidation, locks out competitors** — Tighter AI/crypto controls force startups into expensive compliance regimes while VCs and large incumbents absorb costs; Sarbanes-Oxley already created this dynamic.
- **SpaceX IPO valued at $2.25 trillion with <5% public float** — Classic low-float/high-FDV token pattern: initial parabolic move followed by extended bleed as insiders unlock. Expected to underperform within 6–12 months as VC lockups expire.
- **SEC proposed rescinding Reg NMS Rule 611** — Would unlock tokenized equities on decentralized AMMs and DeFi; expected resistance from high-frequency trading firms (Citadel) and payment-for-order-flow brokers (Robinhood), but likely to pass by Q1 2027.

**The Anthropic Export Ban: Substance and Process**

The U.S. government issued an export control directive at 5:21 p.m. Eastern, blocking foreign nationals (including Anthropic's own international employees) from accessing Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models, citing a demonstrated jailbreak as a national security concern. Anthropic responded that the technique exploited only "a small number of previously known minor vulnerabilities" also present in OpenAI's GPT-5.5 and other publicly available models. The company declined to fix the exploit or de-deploy the model, prioritizing "the continued offering of the consumer model over safety," according to David Sachs, speaking for the administration. The administration's framing: Fable is Mythos (a powerful model) with guardrails, and if those guardrails fail, advanced cyber capabilities are exposed to bad actors.

Sam Enzer, a crypto attorney with experience during the SEC's regulatory wars, frames the core tension. While acknowledging the administration's pro-tech stance, he points out that the government has broad power to ban software dissemination—a power that, under First Amendment precedent (particularly the PGP encryption wars of the 1990s), cannot constitutionally extend beyond explicit national security justification paired with due process. "The letter that was reportedly sent by the Commerce Department to Anthropic has not been publicly released," Enzer notes. "We don't know what the concerns were, what the statutory or regulatory authority was." This absence of transparency mirrors the secrecy problems in FISA courts and represents a major red line: the government can now invoke "national security" and "export controls" to disable any commercial software deployed to hundreds of millions, without disclosure.

**The Export Control Playbook and Precedent Risk**

The mechanism here is export regulation, traditionally used to control physical goods. But the government is applying it to APIs—meaning any U.S. server accessible to foreign nationals could theoretically be regulated as an export. Enzer articulates the core question: "If I have a US server with an API and someone in China accesses it, that that's an export? If so, does that mean the export power can be used against any US-based server, API, or technology?" This is not hypothetical. The speakers worry the precedent creates a playbook for future administrations to invoke export controls against any frontier technology: AI, crypto, decentralized finance. Chris Perkins, with national security experience, acknowledges that "national security will always trump responsible innovation, no matter what." Yet he warns of political capture: Congress is already considering using national security as a Trojan horse to kill the Clarity Act (pro-crypto legislation) via back-door regulatory pressure rather than direct votes.

Enzer's core remedy: transparent rulemaking. If the government has legitimate national security concerns, it must communicate them via notice-and-comment rulemaking—the "bare bones of democracy." Instead, the Trump administration rescinded the Biden-era AI diffusion framework in January 2025 but has not replaced it with a new framework. The result: "What is the framework here? What is the precedent being set?" No one outside the government knows.

**Decentralization and the Long Game**

Austin Campbell notes the parallel to the original encryption wars of the 1990s, when Gene Hoffman (PGP) carried a printed code book to circumvent export controls, using the First Amendment as cover. The speakers see echoes: Anthropic marketed Mythos as dangerously powerful ("too hot to touch"), and now the government is using that marketing against them. Campbell predicts government focus on AI will only intensify as midterms approach, and AI (like crypto before it) will become a political scapegoat.

The panel agrees that absent a limiting principle, the response should be a pivot toward decentralized AI. Perkins notes that "you always have somebody centralized no matter what, like whether it's that front end or something else, they're going to be subject to regulation." The future may be the "AI mullet"—decentralized backend, some centralized element at the front—but that's unproven territory. Campbell and Perkins highlight that even decentralized protocols can't escape regulation if any component is centralized. The real defense is First Amendment protection and market-driven security fixes rather than government mandates.

**SpaceX IPO: Low Float, High Risk**

SpaceX went public on June 12, 2026 (ticker SPCX), priced at $135 for a $1.77 trillion valuation, raising $75 billion. The stock closed day one up 19% at $161.11 and briefly hit $2.25 trillion intraday. The bull case centers on SpaceX's monopoly on large-scale launch capacity and Starlink's strategic importance. The bear case (articulated by Ram Alawalia, an investor) is straightforward: **95–100 times revenue is outrageous.** The company generates ~$15 billion in annual revenue; at $1.77 trillion, "that is not pricing a company. That is pricing a thesis."

More damaging for near-term buyers: the IPO exhibits classic low-float, high-fully-diluted-valuation dynamics. Less than 5% of shares are publicly traded; the rest are locked up in insider hands, including venture capitalists and company employees who will begin selling after lockup expiration in 6 months. Every mega-cap IPO in history has experienced 50% drawdowns in the same year it went public. Alawalia cites Tesla and Amazon: both had multi-year waits before meaningful gains. Campbell compares SpaceX to a low-float crypto token—a parabolic initial move followed by a slow bleed as float increases.

Chris Perkins acknowledges Elon Musk as a visionary ("Thomas Edison of our era") and notes that SpaceX's success at fundraising is itself a masterclass in narrative. But narrative is not valuation. The opportunity is opposite: sell expensive assets to fund SpaceX purchases, then buy back the megacap stocks (Microsoft, Meta, NVIDIA) that were liquidated to pay for the IPO. These traded at discounts last week not seen since the 2020 financial crisis; with earnings growth at 24%+ and an S&P breakout to 8,000+, the asymmetric bet is in the majors, not SpaceX.

Perkins also raises a structural question: why do companies stay private until they achieve trillion-dollar valuations? Retail investors miss all the growth. The solution, he argues, is two-fold: (1) roll back Sarbanes-Oxley requirements that make going public prohibitively expensive, and (2) tax the crap out of share disposals at valuations above the lowest S&P 500 member, incentivizing earlier IPOs and punishing prolonged private-market valuations. Musk deserves credit for broadly distributing equity to SpaceX employees—they benefit from the IPO upside—but the system is broken if only insiders and late-stage VCs capture generational wealth.

**Reg NMS Rescission: Unlocking DeFi Equities**

The SEC, under chair Paul Atkins, voted to propose rescinding Regulation National Market System (Reg NMS), Rule 611—a 2005 rule requiring that any equity trade execute at the best national bid-offer, even if the trader doesn't request it. This rule has been the primary obstacle to tokenized equities and decentralized AMMs. Atkins' reasoning: after 20 years, Rule 611's unintended consequences have "hindered long-term growth." On-chain AMMs cannot guarantee the best national bid-offer and have been locked out of trading tokenized equities entirely.

Chris Perkins calls this "a humongous deal." Tokenization is "electronification 2.0"—it enables 24-7 global access to equities. Remove Rule 611, and you can build 24-7 decentralized equity markets with DeFi infrastructure. But the battle ahead will be titanic. High-frequency trading firms like Citadel profit from the current fragmented structure; the firms will fight hard in comment letters. Perkins predicts: "The people that really are winning in this market structure, I think they're going to pivot and they're going to win in the new market structure." Sophisticated players are already positioned to exploit arbitrage in a more fragmented (i.e., less tightly regulated) market.

Campbell notes that Robinhood and other payment-for-order-flow brokers will be hurt: thinner bid-ask spreads (the goal of Reg NMS rescission) mean less economics for brokers who profit from the spread difference. Perkins adds that AI agents will be the real winners—they excel at optimization across fragmented, decentralized marketplaces.

The timeline: comment period will generate fierce debate, but Atkins' SEC likely has the votes. Finalization is expected by Q1 2027. Austin Campbell closes with dark humor: "The U.S. government simultaneously hates and loves AI and cannot speak with all these things."

# Bits + Bips — "Why Crypto Is Holding Up as the AI Trade Starts to Crack"

**Host:** Austin Campbell (adjunct scholar, Zero Knowledge Group); Chris Perkins (Franklin Crypto). **Guests:** John D'Agostino (head of strategy, Coinbase Institutional); Nick Van Eck (CEO and co-founder, Agora — stablecoin issuer).

## TL;DR

- **Bitcoin finished the week flat while the chip index lost 11.4% and South Korea's KOSPI had its worst week since 2008** — D'Agostino attributes Bitcoin's resilience to thinner liquidity creating price gaps on the upside, not genuine non-correlation; equity-adjacent crypto names like Strategy and Coinbase still fell ~10% and ~5% respectively.
- **GENIUS Act pulled stablecoin enterprise demand forward by 3–5 years** — Van Eck calls it "the most important piece of banking legislation in my lifetime," passed six months into a new presidency; $300B in stablecoins outstanding, but enterprise adoption remains a "drop in the bucket" vs. $1.6 quadrillion in annual global B2B payments.
- **Tokenized U.S. equities are seeing near-insatiable demand** — Coinbase's UAE offering is generating roughly $20M/week since launch; D'Agostino argues tokenized equities could bring billions of people globally into U.S. capital markets with as little as $5.
- **AI frontier lab economics are cracking at the debt level** — Anthropic is reportedly sub-investment grade (junk); CoreWeave fell 25% last week with bonds at distressed levels; D'Agostino notes projected 50-gigawatt data center buildout in 2027 would consume ~96% of all U.S. annual tech investment at even half Jensen Huang's $50B/gigawatt estimate.
- **September CPI came in at 3.7% YoY, PPI posted its biggest jump since March 2022** — Brent crude hit $110; 10-year Treasury reached 5.33%; France saw 2,400 arrests and half a million marchers over cost-of-living anger.
- **Van Eck dubs the debt transfer to younger generations "the generational pump and dump"** — U.S. debt grew from near-zero to $40T over the prior generation's lifetime while interest rates fell for 40 years; Social Security (~22%), interest on debt (~14%), Medicare/Medicaid (~26%), and military (~13%) consume nearly all federal spending with almost no discretionary room.
- **Google's ~250 previously unsolved math proofs accelerated the post-quantum cryptography timeline** — D'Agostino's source at Abu Dhabi Investment Authority called it exciting, not alarming: "We knew this was coming. We thought we had more time."

**Bitcoin's Relative Resilience and the Limits of Non-Correlation**

D'Agostino cautions against reading too much into Bitcoin's flat week. He frames it as a liquidity phenomenon: Bitcoin's market is smaller than AI equities, so it takes less capital to hold price levels, and thin liquidity creates gaps that have benefited crypto on the upside many times before — and hurt it on the downside. He is explicit that correlation tends to "hop to one at the worst possible time" and expects Bitcoin to eventually trade risk-on in other environments. Citrini's published thesis — that Bitcoin and ETH won't make new all-time highs on this cycle, with a 15-token basket as his preferred expression — drew pushback from Milk Road: "Bitcoin and ETH don't derive value from Citrini's assumptions."

D'Agostino and Van Eck both note additional structural supports: Bitcoin ETF distribution is widening as more RIAs get comfortable selling the product; regulatory clarity post-GENIUS has extended beyond Bitcoin into other asset classes. Van Eck argues the market is in an "apathy stage," past peak bearishness but not yet attracting new capital. He expects the unwinding AI trade to redirect liquidity toward crypto over the next year, citing memory-sector names already 54% off all-time highs year to date while still up over 100%.

**Stablecoins: Enterprise Adoption Is Accelerating, But Still Tiny**

Van Eck describes the GENIUS Act as a watershed — a genuine surprise that passed six months into a new presidential term after four years of regulatory hostility. Enterprises across telecom, global mobility, and hospitality are now racing to adopt stablecoins for cross-border value transfer. He places the current moment in context, however: $300B in stablecoins outstanding sounds large, but annual global B2B payments run at $1.6 quadrillion, and some enterprises Agora is working with do that figure in annual revenue alone. The stablecoin market today remains predominantly correlated to crypto trading; Tether remains the largest stablecoin precisely because institutional use is still marginal. Van Eck's benchmark for maturity: when USDC or comparable institutional-grade stablecoins overtake Tether, crypto trading will have become a minority of market volume.

**Tokenized Equities as a Gateway and Geopolitical Asset**

D'Agostino frames tokenized equities as a "wedge attack" for getting non-crypto-native users comfortable holding value on chain. He argues the "one-hour thesis" — if a technically literate person can't learn a new system in under an hour with some confidence, widespread adoption is unlikely — makes tokenized equities a more accessible entry point than Bitcoin. He describes insatiable global demand for U.S. large-cap tech stocks among the billions who cannot open brokerage accounts, and presents it explicitly as a geopolitical tool: "I want everyone in the world with $5 of excess savings to bet on the economic miracle that is the U.S. capital markets." Coinbase's UAE launch is generating roughly $20M/week and growing; D'Agostino acknowledges Franklin competitors have a larger head start.

**The AI Trade Cracks: Frontier Lab Economics Under Pressure**

Campbell attributes the AI selloff to a cascade beginning with Anthropic's revenue disappointment, which prompted reassessment of frontier model economics, hitting the most leveraged names hardest — CoreWeave, memory producers — while leaving Google and Meta relatively unaffected given their cash-flow businesses. D'Agostino adds the arithmetic problem: one bank's projection of 50 gigawatts of data center capacity in 2027 implies, at even half Huang's $50B/gigawatt, spending equivalent to 96% of all U.S. annual tech investment. Van Eck notes frontier labs "have absolutely no moat" and are "in the red by tens of billions of dollars a year." D'Agostino cites one company — possibly Character — spending $16 for every $1 of revenue before going defunct. The open-weight model trend, he argues, is "great for consumers and businesses and really bad for the financial profile of these companies."

Campbell distinguishes three discrete questions that markets tend to conflate: Is AI valuable? Are current frontier labs valuable? Are they valued correctly right now? Airlines, he notes, are an obviously valuable invention that has filed for bankruptcy repeatedly. D'Agostino and Perkins both characterize the AI moment as a "productive bubble" — one whose detritus will "elevate humanity and elevate business to an entirely new level" — while acknowledging the near-term bloodbath. Companies that were 97–98% off their dot-com peaks eventually reclaimed them, D'Agostino notes, but it took 15 years.

**Inflation: Elevated and Politically Destabilizing**

September CPI was 3.7% year-over-year (0.6% month-over-month), up from 3.4%; PPI posted its biggest monthly jump since March 2022 at 1.3% versus 0.5% expected. Gasoline was up 9.1%. The 10-year Treasury hit 5.33% midweek before settling around 5.14%. D'Agostino, a former commodities trader, argues the long-term inflation trajectory "has been exacerbated" but that short-term cooling cycles — typically driven by oil price corrections — will keep recurring. He notes distillates lag crude by two to three weeks, meaning the pump pain from $110 Brent has not yet fully arrived. Trump's move to waive key Russia sanctions was characterized by analyst Priyanka Sachdeva as "another desperate attempt to keep oil prices in check ahead of U.S. midterms."

Perkins flags social stability as the larger concern: France saw 2,400 arrests, half a million marchers, and historical buildings destroyed by fire; a cost-of-living march in Milan turned violent. He frames this as fraying the social contract, with extremist movements gaining on both flanks. He also notes ongoing turmoil in Saudi Arabia as an additional geopolitical wildcard.

**The Generational Divide and the Fiscal Trap**

Van Eck introduces "the generational pump and dump" — the prior generation grew up with effectively zero federal debt and experienced 40 years of falling interest rates, benefiting on both ends while leaving the next generation with $40T in debt and rising rates. He argues young people would have "no problem cutting social programs for elderly people" because this is "the first time in American history where there is a generation that so clearly left off generations following it worse off — and they're incredibly selfish about it."

Campbell lays out the arithmetic: Social Security runs at roughly 22% of federal spending, interest on the debt around 14%, Medicare and Medicaid combined around 26–28%, and the military around 13%. That is nearly all of the budget, with almost no discretionary items — zero-ing the military would not solve the problem. The Social Security math is inescapable: benefits promised exceed what was paid in, so some combination of massive benefit cuts, massive tax hikes, or continued deficit spending is inevitable. Van Eck sees bond vigilantes as the only realistic forcing mechanism: "I don't think there's enough political will. It's only going to be the cost of servicing the debt exploding that's going to force both political parties to recognize we have a serious problem." Perkins concludes: "The only real path out of this thing is growth."

**Quantum Cryptography: Accelerated Timeline, Not Panic**

Google's release of approximately 250 previously unsolved math proofs prompted brief discussion of quantum-resistant cryptography. D'Agostino relays a conversation with Alex Lipton, chief data scientist at Abu Dhabi Investment Authority, who dismissed the alarmism: the work accelerates the timeline toward Q-day but does not change the destination. Post-quantum cryptography preparations were already underway; practitioners simply expected more time. Lipton compared the broader scientific unlocks to decoding the genome. Campbell's mathematician contact made a more expansive point: a working quantum computer capable of breaking all encrypted communications would also enable space travel at extreme speeds and probable cancer cures — the upsides would far outweigh the cryptographic disruption risk.

# The Economics of Everyday Things — "26. Graffiti"

**Host:** Zachary Crockett. **Guests:** Repos (Philadelphia graffiti artist); Tommy Conway (director, Community Life Improvement Program / CLIP, Philadelphia, running the program since its founding in the 1980s); Jane Golden (executive director, Mural Arts Philadelphia, the largest public art program in the country).

## TL;DR

- **Philadelphia is graffiti's birthplace** — artist Cornbread began tagging walls in the 1960s for individual recognition, shifting the practice away from its gang-marking origins.
- **The U.S. spends roughly $12 billion annually on graffiti cleanup** — Philadelphia alone runs $3 million/year through CLIP; San Francisco spends $20 million; Austin $500,000.
- **CLIP removes graffiti from ~185,000 surfaces per year** — about 500 per day — and uses same-day erasure as its primary deterrent.
- **Mural Arts Philadelphia has installed ~4,000 murals** since the 1980s, with artists paid $3,000–$10,000 per project — far less than the $40,000–$60,000 Repos says he earns on the private market.
- **Post-pandemic graffiti surged** — bored residents during COVID created a new wave of vandalism that took CLIP two years to bring back under control.
- **Street artists operate by an informal code** — no cars, churches, or personal property; abandoned spaces and dedicated walls like Graffiti Pier are fair game.
- **The mural program's motives are contested** — Repos questions whether murals in certain neighborhoods serve beautification or gentrification, calling it a "racial kind of thing."

---

**Graffiti's Philadelphia Origins**

Most experts trace modern graffiti to Philadelphia, specifically to Darl McCrae, who began spraying walls under the tag "Cornbread" in the 1960s. His motivation was individual recognition rather than gang affiliation, a distinction that seeded a new tradition. The legend grew when a newspaper wrongly reported his death in a gang shooting — Cornbread responded by tagging his name on an elephant at the Philadelphia Zoo to prove he was alive. Repos, a working Philadelphia artist, explains that every city developed its own stylistic dialect: Philly's is called "wickets," originated by an artist named Notorious Bick, characterized by elongated tags that signal local identity at a glance.

**The Cost of Removal**

Tommy Conway has run CLIP since Philadelphia established it in the 1980s as part of a Zero Tolerance push that started on Broad Street and expanded outward. The program now employs 14 in-house crews, operates on a $3 million annual budget, and removes graffiti from roughly 185,000 surfaces each year — about 500 per day. Removal is free to property owners who call the city's non-emergency line. Conway frames fast turnaround as the core deterrent: "When they put it up there we try and get it down within a couple days." He argues that unchecked graffiti signals disorder and accelerates decay — leading to more graffiti, then litter and a sense of hopelessness. Nationally, total cleanup costs are estimated at around $12 billion per year.

**What Gets Spared**

CLIP removes nearly everything reported, but makes narrow exceptions: memorial graffiti is left up for a couple of weeks until after a burial; tags too high on a building are deprioritized when roof access is the bigger safety concern. One junkyard on Fifth Street — covered in elaborate colorful pieces that the surrounding community wanted kept — was left standing. Last year CLIP fielded around 30,000 removal requests.

**The Artist's Code**

Repos describes a set of informal rules governing where artists paint: no personal property, no cars, no churches. The preferred canvases are abandoned structures. In Philadelphia, Graffiti Pier — the underside of a vacant railway spur on the Delaware River — serves as an open-air practice ground, now popular with tourists and Instagram photographers. The city has taken note of its draw.

**Mural Arts and the Limits of Institutionalization**

In the early 1980s, the city brought in Jane Golden to redirect graffiti writers toward city-funded murals. Golden describes recruiting artists by meeting them behind rec centers and corner stores, working to make the program feel non-punitive rather than a co-option of rebellion. She faced resistance from two directions: graffiti writers skeptical of the formal art world's terms, and the formal art world dismissing street murals as not real public art. The program eventually overcame both. Philadelphia now has around 4,000 murals, and USA Today named it the best city in America for street art in 2025. A 2015 Yale School of Medicine study found positive mental health effects on both artists and neighbors in areas where mural projects were installed.

**Money and the Gentrification Question**

Mural Arts budgets $25,000 to $50,000 per mural; artists receive $3,000 to $10,000 of that. Repos says he took home about $1,200 for one mural with the program. On his own, working with businesses like Foot Locker or private clients, he can earn $40,000 to $60,000 per wall. He participates in both worlds but questions whether the program's placement decisions reflect genuine beautification or serve gentrification — noting that the racial dimension matters to many graffiti writers who observe where murals get commissioned and who benefits.

**The Post-COVID Reversal**

After years of progress, graffiti in Philadelphia surged during the pandemic. Conway theorizes that lockdowns created a new category of vandal — bored residents with time and little oversight. CLIP spent roughly two years restoring its previous gains. Conway's motivating line for his crews: "We have more paint than they do."

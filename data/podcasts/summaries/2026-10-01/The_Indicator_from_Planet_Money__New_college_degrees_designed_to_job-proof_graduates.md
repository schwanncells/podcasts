# The Indicator from Planet Money — "New college degrees designed to job-proof graduates"

**Host:** Ricky Mulvey, Waylon Wong. **Guests:** Eddie Hernandez (professor in human anatomy and physiology, UT San Antonio; designer of the university's new health sciences degree); Jason Fox (chief academic officer, Savannah College of Art and Design; formerly taught graphic design at SCAD).

**Tailored Health Sciences Degrees**

UT San Antonio launched a health sciences major in 2026 designed specifically for students pursuing medical school, dentistry, or other healthcare careers. Eddie Hernandez, a professor in human anatomy and physiology, explained that while the university's existing biology degree was "very longstanding," it felt broad and did not reflect the specific needs of the student population. The new health sciences degree maintains the prerequisites required for medical school while adding specialized coursework that gives students practical advantages in competitive professional programs. The program includes a clinical anatomy lab, advanced physiology classes, and hands-on training in suturing and casting, skills that make students more competitive in graduate school applications.

Case Brown, a senior at UT San Antonio, switched from biology to health sciences after learning about the degree. Hernandez approached him and told him the new major checked all his medical school prerequisites while offering skills that would enhance his resume and applications. Brown signed up immediately. Hernandez notes that around 500 undergraduates are currently enrolled in the health sciences program, about 15% of the entire department of biology and the environment. The program offers six specialty tracks, including medicine, dentistry, and physical therapy.

**Education as Job Training**

College leadership increasingly views education as a pathway to employment rather than a place for exploration. Parents and students expect a monetary return on investment, particularly as the cost of higher education has risen. Jason Fox, chief academic officer at SCAD, puts it bluntly: "Parents and students are looking for a pathway to what's next. When you offer generalized degrees, especially in a specialty university, it begs the question: OK, what am I going to do next?"

This shift has consequences for traditional liberal arts. The Trump administration enacted a policy cutting off federal loans for degrees with low earnings potential. The effect on humanities is stark—bachelor's degrees awarded in humanities have fallen 30% since 2012, according to the American Academy of Arts and Sciences. Even colleges with art and design in their names must make hard choices. SCAD stopped accepting new students in sculpture this year. Students can still take sculpture classes, but the major was eliminated because there was insufficient market demand or because the college was not delivering on the promise of employment. Fox frames these decisions not as purely economic but as honest assessments: either the college was not preparing students for available jobs, or no realistic job market existed.

**The Rise of AI Majors**

Colleges are rushing to add artificial intelligence programs in response to industry demand. According to a tracker from Northeastern University, around 80 AI majors are now offered in the U.S., ranging from big state schools like Boise State to small private colleges like Harding University in Arkansas. SCAD launched an applied AI major in September 2025 after realizing the technology was already transforming creative disciplines like animation and game design. Fox met with a team from NVIDIA and saw "the light bulb come on." NVIDIA became a corporate partner and donated graphics cards that would otherwise cost the college $10,000 to $15,000 each. Close to 30 students have enrolled.

SCAD requires AI majors to choose a minor in another discipline—photography, painting, or similar fields—linking technical training to creative practice. The school says the program prepares students for careers in designing AI products and machines. Fox pointed to alumni working on digital twins—digital versions of real people that can hold conversations, such as a digital George Washington or a deceased grandparent nearing end of life. When asked if a digital twin of himself existed, Fox replied, "There is not."

**Backlash and Concerns**

The integration of AI into college curricula has generated pushback. Earlier this year, students at SCAD circulated a petition asking the college to eliminate a new class on using AI in filmmaking. The concerns centered on environmental impacts and intellectual property rights. Fox emphasizes that no SCAD students are required to use AI, and the college is not mandating the technology.

**A Counterpoint: Digital Humanities**

Not all colleges are abandoning liberal arts entirely. UCLA launched a new Department of Digital Humanities, with the stated goal of using digital tools to explore what it means to be human. Waylon Wong notes that this kind of scholarship can take years to develop and reflect what the academic process is about—allowing students to grapple with fundamental questions rather than simply training them for immediate employment. The existence of the UCLA program suggests that some institutions are trying to preserve humanistic inquiry alongside job-focused practical training.

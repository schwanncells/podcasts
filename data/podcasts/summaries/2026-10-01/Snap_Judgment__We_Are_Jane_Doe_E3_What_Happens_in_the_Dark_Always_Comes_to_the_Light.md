# Snap Judgment — "We Are Jane Doe E3: What Happens in the Dark Always Comes to the Light"

**Host:** Snap Judgment; investigation by Madi Bolaños and Anna Sussman. **Guests:** Alexandria Prout (assault survivor, formerly incarcerated); Antoinette Yancey (survivor, currently incarcerated); Katrina Cameron (survivor); Christina Wind (survivor); Elizabeth Lozano (survivor); Assistant U.S. Attorney Alexandra Young (DOJ investigator); Suki Lewis (editor, On Our Watch).

## TL;DR

- **Gregory Rodriguez convicted on 64 counts, sentenced to 224 years** — the former Correctional Officer at California's Central California Women's Facility operated a 27-year predatory system exploiting inmates with job offers and contraband in exchange for sexual access.
- **13 women testified as Jane Does at trial; DNA evidence sealed the conviction** — semen found on a green chair in Rodriguez's office matched his DNA; camera footage and medical records corroborated assault claims.
- **August 2, 2024: 159 women beaten, pepper-gassed in chow hall lockdown** — corrections officers confined the entire unit without notice, beat detainees with batons, and deployed tear gas; federal investigators concluded this was retaliation for women reporting sexual abuse.
- **Federal investigation found "unbroken pattern" of sexual abuse dating to at least 2000** — U.S. Department of Justice identified deliberate indifference by CDCR leadership and systemic failure to prevent or adequately investigate staff sexual misconduct.
- **Rodriguez's misconduct was reported in 2014 but dismissed** — CDCR investigated the early allegation and closed the case, allowing him to continue assaulting women for another eight years.
- **CDCR disciplined 34 officers; zero were criminally charged** — most received temporary pay cuts; only 3 were fired; the chief deputy warden who approved Operation Breakdown was not disciplined and was later promoted to warden at another prison.
- **Retaliation continues; survivors avoid reporting** — women stated that officers remain at the prison post-incident, some victims are now avoiding filing PREA reports, and one survivor who reported Rodriguez continues to face targeted harassment inside.

**Gregory Rodriguez's 27-Year Predatory System**

Rodriguez worked at the Central California Women's Facility for 27 years, building a methodical system of sexual exploitation. He maintained a list of female prisoners he was interested in, looked them up in the prison information system, and called them to his office under the guise of job offers. In exchange for extra phone calls, commissary items like vodka, or other perks, he would sexually assault them. His access to the prison's blind spots—hallways without camera coverage, a closet where he had constructed a makeshift bed, spaces behind curtains in mop stations—was deliberate and systematic. Some assaults were quick and opportunistic; others involved him cultivating what he portrayed as relationships with victims. Trial testimony and evidence revealed he knew exactly where cameras did not reach and how to move women to those locations.

**The Trial and 13 Jane Doe Witnesses**

Thirteen women testified at Rodriguez's trial, their identities protected as Jane Does: Nicole, Erica, Andrea, Sonia, Sarah, Brandy, Rosa, Nancy, Serena, Paige, Brianne, Jennifer, and Alexandria. All were incarcerated at the time of assault. Alexandria Prout, the first witness called, prepared herself mentally by listening to music, wearing clothes she felt confident in, and steeling herself to be "stoic" on the stand. She made eye contact with Rodriguez during her testimony, identifying him in the courtroom while recounting her assault. The jury heard from each woman in turn, and the evidence was overwhelming. Semen recovered from a green chair in a building where Rodriguez had access matched his DNA. Camera footage and hospital ducats—medical records of injuries—corroborated their accounts. A striking detail: someone had reported Rodriguez's misconduct as far back as 2014. CDCR investigated at the time and dismissed the allegation, leaving him free to assault women for another eight years before his arrest. The jury found him guilty on 64 counts, and the female judge sentenced him to 224 years in prison without leniency.

**August 2, 2024: Mass Use of Force and Alleged Retaliation**

Two months before Rodriguez's trial, on August 2, 2024, officers at the Central California Women's Facility conducted what they called "Operation Breakdown." The stated rationale was to address contraband and disruption on D-yard. According to a leaked interview with Chief Deputy Warden Cynthia Rojas, she had met with the acting captain and associate warden on August 1st to discuss ongoing problems: women smoking, being disruptive. Rojas authorized a mass search the next day, though she said she planned to be off-site. What unfolded was a lockdown of 159 women in a chow hall without warning, followed by beatings and pepper-gassing. Officers spoke casually on body-worn cameras about "fun, fun, fun" during the operation. The contraband found was minimal: some vapes, cell phones, one or two weapons. No significant quantities of drugs. Women were left unable to breathe, believing they would die. One survivor, Elizabeth Lozano, described the experience as dehumanizing: "If you could openly spray people as if they're rats or roaches, they're not human. You completely disregarded them as human."

**Two Investigations: Inside and Outside Pressure**

Two separate investigations began in late 2024. The CDCR's internal affairs investigation, led by the prison agency itself, aimed ostensibly to identify who was responsible for August 2nd and hold them accountable. The federal investigation, led by the U.S. Department of Justice, came from the outside with a broader mandate: to determine whether Rodriguez's abuse was an isolated incident or part of a systemic pattern, and whether August 2nd was connected to a larger problem of sexual abuse at the facility.

The internal investigation interviewed Warden Rojas for about an hour and fifteen minutes. She acknowledged using profane language ("we need to gain control of that fucking unit") but denied making statements about reminding "bitches" who was in charge, claiming she did not recall such language. When pressed by the investigator about discrepancies between her account and witness statements, she maintained, "I don't recall." The investigator never asked whether August 2nd was designed as retaliation. Notably, Rojas was not disciplined for planning and approving Operation Breakdown; she was later promoted to warden at another prison.

**Federal Findings: Systemic Failure and Retaliation**

The federal investigation reached a different conclusion. Assistant U.S. Attorney Alexandra Young stated that women interviewed at the facility described unnecessary cell searches and other retaliation close in time after reporting staff sexual abuse. Based on interviews and investigation files reviewed, the DOJ concluded "the evidence indicates it was retaliation for women reporting staff sexual abuse or misconduct." The report went further, finding an "unbroken pattern of staff sexual misconduct" at the Central California Women's Facility dating back to at least 2000. The DOJ concluded that CDCR leadership "knew women faced a substantial risk of sexual abuse, but failed to adequately take steps to prevent it." This constituted "deliberate indifference" in violation of the Eighth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Antoinette Yancey, reading excerpts of the federal report while still incarcerated, expressed validation: "I do feel that the federal government making this statement is a start because the crimes held against women within prisons are serious and they need to be taken as serious."

**Discipline Without Criminal Accountability**

CDCR reported that 34 officers were disciplined in connection with August 2nd—the largest disciplinary action the agency claimed to have taken. Most received temporary pay cuts. Only three were fired. Critically, not one officer was criminally charged. Christina Wind, who was beaten and pepper-gassed, was sent to solitary confinement for ten days as a result. When asked if CDCR's discipline felt like accountability, she said bluntly: "Absolutely not. A pay cut, transferred so you could abuse somewhere else."

The federal DOJ cannot fire officers or press criminal charges in this civil investigation, but it did demand that CDCR take corrective steps: eliminate blind spots without camera coverage, punish officers who harass and silence reporters, and create truly confidential reporting channels. It threatened to sue the state if CDCR does not comply.

**The Chilling Effect: Silence Over Safety**

The impact on survivors remains devastating. One victim of Rodriguez, still incarcerated, told reporters that she now regrets reporting his abuse. She said, "If I could go back, I would not have reported the sexual assault because it's too dangerous for me in here to have reported that." Moving forward, she stated: "There have been sexual assaults. And if there are any more, I will not report them." This sentiment—sacrificing safety for survival—echoes across the facility. Sergeant Isidro Arroyo, whom Antoinette Yancey had accused of threatening to stop PREA reports, was transferred after the incident but has since returned to the yard. His reappearance is being monitored as a sign of whether the system will truly change.

**Reporting the Unreportable: Journalism Inside a Black Box**

The investigation itself faced extraordinary barriers. CDCR refused to provide body camera footage, full surveillance tapes, or complete internal affairs interviews—all public documents—to the reporters. Only limited materials were released. The journalists were able to visit the prison but were forbidden from speaking to any incarcerated women. When women shouted out from cells, "They treat us terrible in here. They treat us like crap," the reporters were instructed not to respond. A trauma expert quoted in the episode described prisons as "closed systems," comparing them to isolated families where "there's greater risk of harm." The larger issue: public institutions funded by taxpayers operate with near-total opacity. The leaked internal affairs interview with Rojas that made it into the episode was one of the few cracks in the black box.

Despite CDCR's stated cooperation and planned reforms, survivors and advocates remain skeptical. As one formerly incarcerated advocate put it, removing the warden is "like a Band-Aid on a gaping wound." The pattern of accountability through transfers rather than criminal prosecution—moving officials between institutions—remains the norm.

**What Comes to Light**

The episode's title, borrowed from a closing statement by Alexandria Prout, reflects a hard-won victory: Rodriguez's conviction and the federal findings of systemic abuse. Yet the broader question remains unresolved: whether genuine accountability and reform will follow, or whether the prison's closed system will continue to shield abusers and silence victims. As of the episode's recording, that answer lay in the hands of federal pressure, CDCR's willingness to comply, and the continued courage of women still inside, refusing to be silenced.

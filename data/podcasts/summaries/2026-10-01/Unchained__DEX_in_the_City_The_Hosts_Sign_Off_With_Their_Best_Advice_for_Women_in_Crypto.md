# Unchained — "DEX in the City: The Hosts Sign Off With Their Best Advice for Women in Crypto"

**Host:** KK (Catherine; fluent in TradFi and conversant in deep tech at Chainlink Labs). **Guests:** Jesse (Web3 prosecutor turned Web3 protector at Rivet Capital; formerly DOJ prosecuting crypto cases); V (former SEC enforcement division attorney; worked on market structure and financial systems).

## TL;DR

- **Don't enter "crypto"—enter to solve a specific problem** — Think about whether you care about financial access, privacy, payments, governance, or market structure. Without a problem-driven anchor, you'll lose perspective through market cycles.
- **Learn the technology deeply, not just your narrow role** — For non-engineers, this means understanding smart contracts, how assets move, who controls them, and where risks live. "People will make assumptions about you as a woman no matter what role you're in."
- **Get comfortable feeling "other"** — Women in crypto are still outnumbered. The space is male-dominated and that dynamic won't disappear; learning to operate without being derailed by it is essential.
- **You need to be "better" — legally, ethically, and professionally** — One host describes this as a "hot take": women have to be the adult in the room and maintain immaculate integrity in ways male peers often don't. This applies across industries but is amplified in crypto.
- **Have a tough skin for sexism without internalizing it** — Expect inappropriate comments, assumptions, and questioning of your presence. Recognize that most of this reflects immaturity or bias in the industry, not a personal failing.
- **Build community both inside and outside crypto** — Find your people within the space (collaborators, aligned values, friends), but also maintain friendships, hobbies, and identity outside it. This balance is critical for surviving volatility.
- **Maintain intellectual independence and contrarian views** — Don't conform just because the dominant voices disagree with you. Crypto as a nascent industry benefits from debate; being the only voice on an unpopular position doesn't mean you're wrong.
- **If you lose your values, you lose yourself** — The loudest people online aren't representative. Find your purpose in helping vulnerable people, building safe systems, or whatever drew you in. Stick to what's true to you.

**Framing: Why This Episode and Why It Matters**

This is the final episode of DEX in the City, the podcast's sign-off after more than a year. The hosts decided not to cover crypto news in their last episode but instead to share hard-won advice for anyone entering or struggling in crypto, with particular focus on women. They note that the situation for women in crypto did not measurably improve over the year despite the show's efforts to amplify female voices. The hosts emphasize that crypto is not just "crypto bros" and that the industry needs all kinds of diversity—age, race, socioeconomic, gender—to build a better financial system. The show was created specifically to amplify women's voices in a space that claims to need everyone's input but often doesn't show it.

**Focus on Problems, Not on "Crypto"**

V argues that newcomers should not think of entering crypto as entering a career category. Instead, identify a specific problem you want to solve: improving financial access, enhancing privacy, creating better payment systems, exploring new forms of governance, or—in V's case—improving market structure through better regulation and financial systems design. V came to crypto through prior work on SEC enforcement investigations and the conviction that blockchain could address structural weaknesses in traditional finance. This problem-driven approach keeps perspective through the industry's notorious volatility. Without it, when the market crashes or sentiment shifts, you have no anchor to explain why you're still there.

**Learning the Technology Deeply**

V makes clear that all roles in crypto require deep technical understanding, not just domain expertise in your narrow function. A marketer needs to understand smart contracts, asset movement, and risk. A lawyer must grasp how every regulatory component (securities law, CFTC oversight, international considerations, money transmission rules) connects to what's actually being built. V stresses: "You might not need to learn to code, but you should sit with your smart contract engineers and understand what they're doing." This knowledge serves two purposes: it makes you more effective in your role, and it counters assumptions people make about women in tech—assumptions that dissolve when you demonstrate serious technical competence.

**The "Other" Factor and Reframing Gender Dynamics**

Jesse highlights that women in crypto must get comfortable being "other." She recounts an anecdote from a women-focused conference where a man complained of being the only guy in the room and receiving compliments on his outfit. Jesse's response: "Well, at least you're in a conference where you're the only man in the room and everyone's being nice to you. That's not always how it works when it's reversed." The point is not self-pity but situational awareness—you will be outnumbered, noticed differently, and sometimes treated differently, and that reality requires psychological preparation.

KK adds a more contentious point: women in crypto often need to be "better" in ways male colleagues do not. She compares it to her earlier life—in college, she was a "goody two shoes" because she wanted to run for office and felt she needed to be immaculate with no skeletons. In crypto, she carries a similar ethic: being the adult in the room, the one with unimpeachable judgment and credentials, because she is a woman. She notes this is a "hot take" and acknowledges that other women reject this framework, preferring to openly own motherhood, gender presentation, or other facets of identity. KK's point is not that this is objectively right but that she made this choice and it shaped her approach to the industry.

**Handling Sexism and Maintaining Perspective**

KK provides concrete examples of sexism she has faced: a troll telling her to "get a pumpkin spice latte" (coded sexism), a man asking her why she works given her husband's job (a question never asked of men). She stresses that recognizing this pattern doesn't mean internalizing it. Much of the poor behavior in crypto reflects "a lack of maturity that exists within certain pockets of this industry." The lesson: understand that a lot of the hostility or questioning "is not about you. It's about standing your ground for a multitude of reasons"—advocating for your position, your client, your company, your interpretation. Sometimes it's disagreement. Sometimes it's immaturity. But crypto, like any nascent, fast-growing industry, "is not for the faint of heart."

**Building Dual Community**

All three hosts emphasize community but split the advice. Jesse and V both stress the need for people outside crypto—spouses, friends, hobby groups, anyone who can remind you what normal life looks like and tell you to "get a grip" when you're spiraling about a market move. Jesse finds meditation, dinosaurs, and AI learning calming. V recently started a weekly Mahjong group. V frames this as essential perspective-keeping: "someone who could not care less about crypto" helps you remember that the industry, while important, is still a small piece of global finance.

But KK balances this with an equally strong point: you also need your people inside crypto. She describes the General Counsel community as deeply collaborative, intellectually honest, and aligned on values. They have dedicated chat channels where they "talk shit" in the nerdiest way—intellectually debating positions, calling out bad ideas, commiserating through crises. These people understand the unique pressures of crypto without needing a 20-minute explainer. The combination—deep friendships inside crypto with aligned values, plus a life outside—is what sustains people through the volatility.

**Managing Volatility and Finding Purpose in Rough Times**

All three hosts describe how they managed the brutal 2022-2023 crypto winter. KK drew on memory of the 2008 financial crisis, giving her historical perspective. Jesse leaned heavily on the crypto community—calling friends on speed dial—but also deliberately stepped away to learn AI, discovering it used similar legal reasoning patterns to crypto and sparking new intellectual interest. V faced a particularly hard moment after the FTX collapse when she became emotional in a Senate meeting and realized she had attached part of her identity to the belief that crypto was worth defending. Her insight: you must separate belief in the technology's potential and your own reasons for being in crypto from the actions of bad actors in the industry. Learning from failures does not mean the whole thing was wrong.

All three stress: find things outside crypto. Whether it's nature (KK's "power walks"), learning new technology (Jesse's AI videos), hobbies (V's Mahjong), or community (their friend network), you need places where crypto doesn't dominate your mental space. This is not optional—it's survival equipment.

**Intellectual Independence and Not Losing Yourself**

Jesse's most powerful advice is direct: "Don't lose yourself." Many of her positions are contrarian in crypto—she speaks constantly about illicit finance, protecting victims, and supporting vulnerable people, knowing these topics don't get the airtime of pure technical advancement or financial speculation. She has lost opportunities, been excluded from rooms, and disagreed with influential people because of these positions. But she found her best friends, her closest collaborators, and her purpose by sticking to what felt true to her and learning as much as possible to support that mission. She notes: "For everyone you disagree with, or everyone that you think isn't trying to build something safe, there are so many people who are doing such amazing things."

V reinforces this with a broader point about conformism in crypto: the industry developed a strong conformist culture early on, when "we didn't even know if we would survive as an industry," fostering solidarity and a sense of "us against the world." That's understandable but not permanent. Crypto as a nascent industry benefits from disagreement and intellectual independence. Don't be afraid of having an unpopular point of view. No one has all the answers, and the hard questions genuinely need debate.

KK adds a practical observation: the loudest people on social media are not representative. Every time one of them posts a strong take and gets attacked by the same five loud voices, they receive 5–10 private messages from people saying "I agree with you" or "that was interesting." The silent majority is often on your side; the algorithm just doesn't show it.

**Memorable Crypto Moments and Lessons**

KK recounts attending Paris Blockchain's VIP dinner at Versailles—something she notes would likely never have happened in big law. She also describes the jarring transition from a King and Spalding (traditional, conservative law firm) partner retreat to her first crypto company offsite in a rented 1980s mansion in Fort Lauderdale with fur on the floors. As a GC, she was mentally running through risk factors (people drinking together in one house, etc.), but it was a lesson in the sheer culture shift from traditional finance to crypto.

V shares the anecdote of a team member asking her, as General Counsel, whether it was okay to attend a crypto development conference in North Korea—a question V finds hilarious and illustrative of the bizarre edge cases a crypto GC encounters.

Jesse describes prosecuting terrorist financing cases involving Bitcoin when "nobody cared" in government—she had to beg the FBI to help. Years later, when terrorism financing in crypto finally got mainstream attention, she received an avalanche of reach-outs from people who hadn't cared at the time. The lesson: the industry and the world's understanding of it have grown substantially, and being in the space through that growth was meaningful.

**Crediting Mentors and Female Leaders**

KK shouts out Arie Redboard, Ben Sherwin (a "girl dad" who recruited her to Chainlink), Kyle Armstrong, Rebecca Reddigs, Kathy Younes, and Lindsay Haswell as women who embraced her early in crypto. She also credits Kathy Clay at S&P (not in crypto but a brilliant mentor) for understanding her value and mentoring her seriously. KK emphasizes: "I'm not going to mentor somebody just because they're a woman. I'm going to mentor a smart, ambitious woman because I want her in this space." The plea to marketing and PR teams: put female leaders on panels and show them to press.

Jesse thanks unnamed mentors at the DOJ who worked tirelessly and ethically, even as crypto was dismissed. She advocates for understanding that government workers are often doing hard, important jobs even when we disagree with policy.

V recommends the podcast "Women in Tech" hosted by Abishka Devora, which covers career advice and founder stories beyond just technical content. She also highlights "How I AI," a technical podcast hosted by a woman offering practical AI tool tutorials.

**The Long View**

The hosts close by reflecting on what crypto has become. KK draws a parenting metaphor: watching crypto grow is like watching children grow—mixed emotions, pride in helping them develop, occasional frustration, and excitement for what's next. What the hosts are most proud of is showing that crypto is not monolithic—that diversity, especially of voice and value system, is possible and necessary. Being a woman in crypto is still hard. Being a woman with a contrarian position is harder. But the work of building with integrity and insisting on ethical consideration for vulnerable people has meaning, and there are many good people doing it alongside you.

The show ends with both hosts and listeners knowing that "DEX in the City" will live on the internet forever, and the conversation—though not weekly anymore—will continue among the three of them and the community they've built.

# The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart — "A More United Nations with PM Pedro Sánchez of Spain"

**Host:** Jon Stewart. **Guest:** Pedro Sánchez (President of the Government of Spain, advocating for UN reform and multilateralism at the UN General Assembly).

## TL;DR

- **Sánchez condemns "coordinated demolition" of multilateralism** — he frames rising unilateralism as a three-pillared attack built on "selfishness" (zero-sum thinking), "ignorance" (belief that powerful nations don't need others), and "cruelty" (indifference to civilian suffering and environmental destruction).
- **Security Council veto power must be abolished** — Sánchez calls for veto elimination and fairer regional representation, arguing "no country should be judge in its own case, no matter how powerful they are."
- **Spain doubled development aid during a global cutback** — while 60% of world development spending was slashed in two years, Spain increased both bilateral cooperation by 100% and multilateral system contributions by 60%, and refused military buildup despite pressure.
- **Call for a Latin American woman as UN Secretary-General** — after nine male secretaries-general in 80 years and three decades excluding the third-most-populous continent from the role, Sánchez frames female leadership as settling an "historic debt" and rejecting backslide on equality.
- **Spain opposed all wars of aggression and rejected embargo-busting** — he lists Ukraine, Lebanon, Iran, and Gaza conflicts; emphasizes arms embargo on Netanyahu's Israel and refusal to allow military deployment via Spanish territory.
- **Multilateralism is the "best tool" for managing diversity and interdependence** — Sánchez positions UN reform (not abandonment) as essential for managing a multipolar, globalized world, pointing to the UN Charter itself as the blueprint.
- **Critics of the UN aren't describing failure; they're attempting it** — Sánchez distinguishes between legitimate criticism and deliberate destabilization, arguing some actors "cut contributions" and "obstruct" to render the organization "useless."

**The Assault on Multilateralism and Its Three Pillars**

Sánchez opens his address to the UN General Assembly with alarm about an 81-year erosion of the UN's founding principles. He identifies a "planned and coordinated demolition attempt" against multilateralism that he characterizes as based on three fundamental misguided pillars. First is selfishness—the zero-sum belief that one nation's gain requires another's loss. Second is ignorance, which assumes that powerful countries need not cooperate with others to prosper. Third, which he calls "the worst," is cruelty—an indifference to starving children, bombed hospitals, environmental destruction, and human trafficking. Sánchez frames these forces as having grown bolder over eight years: seizing power, initiating illegal wars, massacring civilians, treating human beings as "livestock," and systematically poisoning the UN itself.

**Hope Amid Systemic Decline**

Despite witnessing these destructive forces, Sánchez maintains confidence in humanity. He cites millions mobilized to defend nature and civil rights, thousands of activists and public servants risking careers and lives to counter reaction, scientists achieving breakthroughs, and governments choosing cooperation, social justice, and environmental sustainability. He concludes that humanity is neither intrinsically good nor bad—each day, year, and crisis requires choosing how to act. This framing positions the UN not as a failed institution but as an essential decision-making stage in an era demanding human agency.

**Distinguishing Criticism from Sabotage**

A key argument separates legitimate critique from deliberate destruction. Sánchez acknowledges his own past calls for the UN to address internal contradictions and failures. However, he draws a line: the "majority of voices insisting on criticizing the United Nations are not describing its failure. What they're doing is trying to cause its failure." This distinction matters because it shapes his response—not retreat from multilateralism but renewal and strengthening.

**Core UN Reforms: Security Council Overhaul**

Sánchez anchors his reform agenda in the UN Charter itself, arguing its text holds all necessary guidance. The primary structural reform is Security Council transformation: ending the permanent members' veto right and ensuring fair regional representation. He argues that allowing any country to be "judge in its own case" violates both the spirit and letter of the charter, regardless of military power. He also calls for strengthening the International Criminal Court to investigate and prosecute rule-breakers, noting that humanity took centuries to replace "might is right" with rule of law—a principle that must not be abandoned. Additional reforms include beefing up the UN's main bodies, reorganizing agencies with more resources and executive capacity, rebuilding nuclear non-proliferation architecture, and regulating tech oligarchs controlling social networks and AI development, framing these as protecting children's futures over corporate profit.

**Spain's Multilateralist Stance Under Pressure**

Sánchez positions Spain as a counterexample to rising unilateralism. Over eight years his government has opposed all wars of aggression (Ukraine, Lebanon, Iran), refused military spending hikes to 5% of GDP, and rejected diplomatic shortcuts. In response to pressure—particularly surrounding irregular migration into the Spanish city of Ceuta, where "inequality, desperation and disinformation" fueled one of recent years' most acute migration crises—Spain has refused to abandon decency or international law. Instead, citizens and officials have managed the crisis "without compromising on decency, upholding the law and respecting human rights." On aid, Sánchez emphasizes that while 60% of global development spending was cut in two years, Spain doubled bilateral cooperation and raised multilateral contributions by 60%. On Israel-Gaza, he was direct: Spain imposed an arms embargo on Netanyahu's government and explicitly condemned the "genocide that Netanyahu is still perpetrating in Gaza," while also blocking use of Spanish territory for military operations against international law.

**The Case for Female Latin American UN Leadership**

Sánchez makes an extended argument for appointing a female Latin American as the next UN Secretary-General. He begins with a basic arithmetic: nine men have served as Secretary-General in the UN's 80 years; no woman has ever held the role. For three decades, the third most-populated continent (Latin America) has been excluded from the position. Sánchez frames this as an "historic debt" the UN must settle and positions female leadership as non-optional in an era where feminism faces attack and thousands of women suffer violence. He invokes Clara Campo Amore, a Spanish parliamentarian who pioneered arguing that security comes through social progress rather than force, modeling the empathy and peace-building he wants to see in UN leadership. Sánchez argues feminism is "one of the main forces of transformation and progress that the world has experienced in the last century" and the UN must allow it to "guide this institution in much more of a clear and robust way." He concludes that reform's success depends not only on appointing female leadership but on "brave governments prepared to do things right, however high the price to pay may be"—a description he positions Spain as exemplifying.

**Closing Framing: Generational Accountability**

Sánchez ends by invoking future judgment. In a few years, future generations will study this "decisive decade" and ask what role each of us played. They will ask what we did when countries broke rules and others "looked the other way," when defending beliefs "started to cost us dearly," and when pressure mounted to abandon principles. Sánchez commits, on behalf of Spain, to show that Spain did not give up, that it gave "everything to leave them a more peaceful, more sustainable, and of course, a fairer world."

# Embedded — "Where is Austin Tice?: The American journalist"

**Host:** Josh Baker (investigative journalist, BBC Radio 4 and NPR's Embedded). **Guests:** Sara Obeidat (investigative journalist, co-producer); David Enders (journalist who reported in Syria with Austin in 2012; 25-year veteran covering conflicts including Iraq).

## TL;DR

- **Syrian intelligence files confirm Austin Tice was held by the National Defence Forces (NDF)** — a paramilitary militia loyal to President Assad, not in the regime's official prison system, providing a layer of deniability while maintaining control.
- **Tice arrived in Syria in summer 2012 as a freelance journalist with former Marine combat experience** — within two months, his Washington Post story appeared on the front page, launching his career as a foreign correspondent.
- **The Assad regime actively tracked Tice's whereabouts** — Syrian intelligence memos show the regime requesting his location, tracking his mother Deborah's movements and meetings with the CIA, and even offering hospital staff equipment in exchange for his capture.
- **A hospital director reported the regime demanded Austin's surrender** — after the hospital refused and security forces demanded they sedate Tice for abduction, the facility was burned to the ground, possibly in retaliation.
- **Despite the regime's fall, Tice's fate remains unknown** — intelligence files contain conflicting information, some pages claiming he was "liquidated" while others suggest he may still be alive, leaving his family without closure more than a decade later.

**Syrian Intelligence Files and Austin's Detention**

Josh Baker and Sara Obeidat discovered secret archives created by Syrian intelligence spanning years of handwritten notes, formal reports, memos, and coded messages investigating Austin Tice. The files reveal that Syrian intelligence was searching for Austin rather than holding him in their own system. A memo mentions Tice being held with the National Defence Forces, a paramilitary group that operated outside Syria's conventional prison system but remained loyal to the Assad regime. This arrangement allowed the regime to control Austin while maintaining plausible deniability. When Baker and Obeidat visit the NDF compound on a mountain outside Damascus, they find a small prison and confirm through multiple sources that Austin was indeed held there, though no trace of him remains at the time of their investigation.

The intelligence files also document Syrian security services monitoring efforts to find Austin from outside parties. They tracked Deborah Tice, Austin's mother, when she arrived in Lebanon and met with the U.S. ambassador, gathering details about her phone number, location, and contacts. One memo summarizes a meeting between the CIA director and Lebanese security leadership, with Syrian intelligence having intercepted the conversation—their notes indicate the Americans were seeking proof of Austin's survival, even mentioning someone offering to provide his DNA.

**Austin Tice's Journey to Syria**

Austin Tice arrived in Syria in the summer of 2012 as the civil war escalated following months of peaceful Arab Spring protests against the Assad regime. Unlike staff reporters from major news organizations, Tice was a freelancer—a young journalist who had put his law studies on hold to pursue foreign correspondence. He brought military experience from his service as a U.S. Marine in Iraq and Afghanistan, giving him a knowledge of combat and survival that most journalists lack. David Enders, an experienced journalist also covering Syria for McClatchy newspapers, initially viewed the competition warily ("who is this guy?"), but they connected and began traveling together through rebel-held areas.

Tice worked as embedded with the Free Syrian Army (FSA) and rebel fighters, dependent on them for transport, accommodation, and security. Video footage captured by local activists shows Tice photographing anti-government graffiti painted by teenagers, filming peaceful demonstrations, and reporting from the streets. He is visibly distinctive—six feet four inches tall, with visible tattoos and a shaven head, almost always wearing the same faded green shirt and backward baseball cap, his camera hanging constantly at hand. Enders recalls that Tice brought little with him ("only one change of clothes"), traveled light, and was "very risk-acceptant," positioning himself at the front lines to capture stories other journalists could not access.

**The Risks of Military Background and Inexperience**

David Enders observed troubling gaps in Tice's judgment during their weeks together in 2012. While Tice's Marine experience was invaluable in combat situations—Enders notes it was "very impressive" and "quite frankly, it was, at times, very terrifying"—his military identity became a liability in the Syrian context. One fighter told Tice directly: "he's an American soldier he has blood on his hands," referencing the U.S. invasion of Iraq. Enders warned Tice about this perception and discouraged him from teaching Syrian fighters how to shoot or offering military advice about tactics, which crossed into contributing to violence.

Enders was so concerned about Tice's risky behavior that he discussed with a Syrian journalist the possibility of "having Austin tied up and dragged back to Turkey," acknowledging logistically it would have been difficult but reflecting how dire his safety concerns had become. Enders attributes this to the psychological toll of prolonged exposure to combat—after four or five weeks in a war zone, judgment erodes under stress, exhaustion, and hunger. Tice seemed "clinically insane" in his decision-making and lacked awareness of the cultural and historical context he was reporting in. Unlike Enders, who operated within the larger structure of a news organization, Tice was entirely on his own and learning journalism in real time.

**Rapid Success and Increased Visibility**

After just two months in Syria, Tice achieved a major breakthrough. His story appeared on the front page of the Washington Post, a prestigious national newspaper, launching him as a recognized correspondent. Enders emailed congratulations, and Tice was excited—Enders joked that Tice would win a Pulitzer, and Tice took it seriously. This success, combined with Tice's use of social media to document his reporting almost in real time, increased his visibility significantly. Baker traced nearly every step of Tice's movements through Syria by reviewing his social media posts, which shared photos, place names, and locations in sequence.

This visibility proved professionally valuable—it led to appearances on major news broadcasts and attracted attention from major news organizations seeking first-hand accounts from inside Syria, a country that rarely granted press visas. However, it also created a security liability. Tice's posts revealed his path toward Damascus, the capital and epicenter of regime control. While visibility helps freelancers get work, it simultaneously broadcasts their movements to hostile actors—in this case, to Syrian intelligence services monitoring foreign journalists in the country.

**The Hospital Incident at Al-Tal**

Baker and Obeidat travel to Al-Tal, a quiet, conservative town about thirty minutes from Damascus, to interview Dr. Hassan, the director of a hospital where Tice reported from. Dr. Hassan describes the moment security services became aware of Tice's presence. The regime reached out to the hospital with an explicit request: hand over the American journalist. They offered incentives—equipment, supplies, whatever the hospital needed. When the hospital refused, the regime escalated, asking the staff to sedate Tice so they could abduct him from the facility. After the hospital declined a second time, regime forces attacked and burned the hospital to the ground.

Dr. Hassan states definitively: "Yeah. 100%. Yeah, because it was like, why would you not cooperate with us? Therefore, you're going to be a target." Baker fact-checks this account and finds confirmation that the hospital was indeed set on fire during the period in question. However, he notes uncertainty about motive—while Dr. Hassan's account is consistent and supported by photographs of the burned structure, the Assad regime routinely targeted medical facilities throughout the war, so the targeting may not have been a direct retaliation for protecting Tice. Nevertheless, the account raises an important question: if the regime was actively planning to capture Austin at the hospital, this changes the understanding of his abduction and who bears responsibility for what happened to him.

**The Remaining Mystery**

The fall of the Assad regime in 2024 was expected to be the moment when Austin's fate would finally be revealed. His family and supporters anticipated he would emerge from one of the prisons the regime had run. Instead, more than a decade after his disappearance in summer 2012, there is no sign of him in any liberated facility. Deborah Tice, Austin's mother, has shifted from a situation with "an excuse almost for not hearing anything"—when the regime was in power and information was a black box—to a state of profound ambiguity. Baker describes her as grasping at every "minute" possibility for answers, desperate to find out what happened to her son.

The intelligence files, while extensive, offer contradictory clues rather than clear answers. Some pages state Austin was "liquidated," suggesting his death, while others seem to indicate he may still be alive. Baker's investigation has established that Austin was definitely held by the NDF militia on a mountainside outside Damascus, but his ultimate fate remains unknown. Baker plans to retrace Austin's path through Syria to find people who might know what really happened to him.

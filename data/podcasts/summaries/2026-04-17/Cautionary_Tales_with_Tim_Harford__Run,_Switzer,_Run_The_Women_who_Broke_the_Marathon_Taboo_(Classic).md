# Cautionary Tales with Tim Harford — "Run, Switzer, Run: The Women who Broke the Marathon Taboo (Classic)"

**Host:** Tim Harford.

## TL;DR

- **Jasmine Paris won the 2019 Spine Race (268 miles) while breastfeeding** — she demolished the men's course record by more than 12 hours, completing the brutal January ultra-marathon across northern England's Pennine Way in roughly 83 hours with only 3 hours of sleep, stopping to pump milk at rest stops.
- **Bobby Gibb secretly ran the 1966 Boston Marathon in 3:21:40 without official entry** — she snuck onto the course disguised in a hoodie and brother's shorts, and a rejection letter the previous year claimed women were "not physiologically capable of running a marathon."
- **Catherine Switzer officially entered Boston in 1967 and was physically attacked at mile 4** — a race official tried to rip her number off; her boyfriend body-checked him; iconic press photos captured the assault and galvanized women's rights campaigns.
- **The 1928 Olympics 800m race was falsely reported as a disaster** — newspapers claimed five of eleven women collapsed; in fact, only 9 women competed, 6 finished, and the top three all broke world records; the IOC used the manufactured scandal to ban women from that distance for 30 years.
- **Women run faster than men in ultra-marathons (195+ miles)** — the International Association of Ultra Runners' 2020 report showed faster average women's times, but this reflects an elite female field versus a much larger pool of average male competitors; "the average female competitor, one of a tiny handful of unbelievably badass women."
- **Women's performance gap in distance running is closing rapidly** — in 1954 women's mile record was one minute slower than men's; by 1992 the gap had narrowed to just 26 seconds; women's times have improved dramatically once restrictions were lifted and cultural barriers fell.
- **Athletic establishments falsely claimed marathons would damage women's fertility and reproductive capacity** — teenage runners were told a mile a day might make them infertile or "turn them into a man"; these were the justifications for banning women from marathons until the 1970s.

**The Legend and Reality of the Marathon**

Tim Harford opens by contrasting two foundational marathon stories—the legend of Philippides running 26 miles from Marathon to Athens in 490 BCE and collapsing dead, and the dramatic 1908 London Olympics finish of Durando Pietri, an Italian pastry chef who fell repeatedly in front of the finish line, supported by onlookers, before collapsing as disqualified. Arthur Conan Doyle, present at the event, described the scene as "horrible and yet fascinating, this struggle between a set purpose and an utterly exhausted frame." The marathon became enshrined as a test so grueling it could kill a man—and therefore, the logic of the era went, could never be safe for women.

**Women's Exclusion From the Olympics and Early Propaganda**

Women were barred from the first Olympics entirely. When the International Olympic Committee finally agreed to admit female athletes in 1928, the longest women's race was only 800 meters. The 800m final became a turning point—though not in the way newspapers reported. Contemporary accounts, including from the New York Evening Post, claimed five of eleven women had collapsed and one required medical attention, painting the race as a "disgrace" and proof that women lacked the physiology for distance running. The Chicago Tribune and syndicated journalists opined that 200 meters was "surely the maximum distance a woman could attempt without premature aging and damage to her reproductive capacity."

The reality was starkly different. Only nine women competed, not eleven; six finished (a completion rate better than the 1928 Spine Race). The top finisher, Lena Radkeerbatchawer, broke the world record, as did the silver and bronze medalists and the three runners behind them—the most remarkable women's middle-distance field in history. Rather than celebrating this achievement, the IOC weaponized the fabricated scandal to ban the 800m from women's competition for the next 30 years.

**Bobby Gibb's Unofficial Victory**

In 1966, the Boston Athletic Association rejected Bobby Gibb's application to run the Boston Marathon with a letter flatly stating that "women are not physiologically capable of running a marathon." Gibb crumpled the letter and trained extensively—regularly running 40-mile distances near Boston, using time-based navigation since she had no way to measure distance formally.

On race day, Gibb crouched in a bush near the start and, once half the male runners had departed, stepped onto the course in disguise: a hoodie and her brother's shorts. She was nervous, unsure if she'd even be arrested, but male runners quickly reassured her: "We won't let them." As she shed her hoodie partway through the race in a strappy vest top, the news spread. Wellesley College students roared their approval. She maintained a sub-3-hour pace for much of the race—fast enough to challenge for Olympic gold—but her feet, shredded by brand-new men's running shoes (no women's shoes existed in the sports market at the time), forced her to slow in the final miles. She finished in 3 hours, 21 minutes, and 40 seconds, with two-thirds of the male field still behind her, no official number, and no official timestamp.

**Catherine Switzer and the Iconic Boston Marathon Attack**

Catherine Switzer, encouraged by her father to run ("Cheerleaders cheer for other people. You want people to cheer for you"), became a journalism student at Syracuse and trained with the men's cross-country team. Coach Arnie Briggs, a 50-year-old mailman and Boston Marathon veteran, eventually agreed to help her qualify. Three weeks before the marathon, they ran 31 miles in training; Arnie turned gray and passed out, while Katherine felt great. She signed up using her initials—"Kay V. Switzer"—alongside Arnie and her boyfriend Tom, a college football player.

At mile 4, a press truck pulled alongside to photograph "that dame" running the marathon. Switzer recalled: "a man with an overcoat and felt hat" reached out to grab her hand, then she heard "the scraping noise of leather shoes coming up fast behind me." A large official with "the most vicious face" grabbed her shoulder and flung her back, screaming "Get the hell out of my race and give me those numbers." In terror—Switzer "realized she had wet her pants in fear"—she sprinted away, but Big Tom, in his bright orange Syracuse sweatshirt, body-checked the official into a crumpled heap. Arnie yelled "Run like hell," and they sprinted away with the press truck in pursuit, cameras clicking.

What made the scene historically pivotal was that it was captured on film. The image of Switzer being attacked by an official became, according to Switzer, "one of the most galvanizing photos in women's rights history." Catherine, harassed by photographers, with a cold hand (her glove ripped off), wet pants, and abandoned by Tom partway through, pressed on in cold anger. She crossed the finish line with bleeding feet and a 4-hour time, determined to prove no one could stop her. Tom eventually caught up, walking and defeated. Bobby Gibb, running unofficially ahead of Switzer, had already crossed without drama and been turned away from the post-race dining room ("Sorry, men only").

**The Pivotal Impact and Domino Effect**

Neither Gibb nor Switzer set out to change the world, but between them they did. The photographs provided concrete leverage to campaigners pressing authorities to change the rules. In 1972, Nina Cusick became the first woman to officially win Boston, while Bobby Gibb was retrospectively recognized as a three-time Boston Marathon winner. Gibb's later reflection captured the principle: "Men weren't allowed to have feelings and women weren't allowed to have a brain. What if a man wants to knit, is he any less of a man? No? What if a woman wants to drive a truck? Is she any less of a woman? No? All people can be who they want to be."

**The Closing Performance Gap**

As women gained access to competition, their times improved rapidly. In 1954, the women's mile record was one minute slower than the men's. Days after Roger Banister broke the four-minute mile in 1954, Diane Leather broke the five-minute mile not far away. By 1992, the gender gap had narrowed to just 26 seconds. The improvement reflects not any physiological shift but rather that women, once allowed to train and compete seriously, proved competitive across all distances.

An eye-catching finding from the 2020 International Association of Ultra Runners report claimed that in races longer than 195 miles (roughly 7.5 marathons), women run faster than men on average. This is not due to women's superior physiology at extreme distances but rather a selection effect: only a tiny, elite cohort of women attempt ultra-marathons, while a much larger and more average pool of men participate. As Harford explains, "what they're saying is that the average female competitor, one of a tiny handful of unbelievably badass women, is faster than the average male competitor who comes from a much larger pool."

Over extreme distances, the determinants shift from lung capacity and muscle mass—where men have a clear advantage—to "luck, on resilience, and on a tolerance for pain," evening the field.

**Jasmine Paris and the 2019 Spine Race**

Jasmine Paris, a champion distance runner, entered the 2019 Spine Race despite never having raced over such a long distance. The race spans 268 miles up England's Pennine Way in January darkness (16 hours per day), with competitors carrying bedding and food, no enforced rest breaks, and terrain that forces many to drop out.

Paris approached the race while still breastfeeding her one-year-old daughter; she planned to pump milk at rest stops. By the final checkpoint, with 42 miles remaining, she had been running for three days with only three hours of sleep, hallucinating visions of trees in yoga poses and a bright pink pig. She pressed forward despite extreme fatigue, extending her lead over course record holder Eugenie Rossello Sole.

As Paris limped toward the finish line on Wednesday evening with tendonitis in both legs, Rossello Sole, exhausted and dangerously cold, pushed his emergency button to quit the race with just four miles remaining. Paris obliterated the men's course record by more than 12 hours. Ian Keith, the previous course record holder, did not finish until the following morning—15 hours after Paris.

After the solitude of the Pennine Way, Paris crossed a finish line surrounded by cameras and a clingy reunion with her daughter Rowan. She showered, ate the fish and chips she'd been dreaming of, and breastfed Rowan. Her PhD thesis was due in ten weeks.

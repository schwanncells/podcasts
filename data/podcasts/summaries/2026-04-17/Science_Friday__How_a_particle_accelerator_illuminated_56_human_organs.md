# Science Friday — "How a particle accelerator illuminated 56 human organs"

**Host:** Ira Flatow. **Guest:** Dr. Claire Walsh (Associate Professor, University College London; Director of the Human Organ Atlas Hub; researches 3D imaging techniques for human organs).

## TL;DR

- **56 human organs scanned using synchrotron radiation** — the Human Organ Atlas, a public database built at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility in Grenoble, France, lets anyone explore detailed scans of kidneys, lungs, hearts, brains, and more via an interactive portal similar to Google Earth.
- **HIP-CT resolution is 1 micrometer, 1,000 times finer than hospital CT** — while hospital CT scanners achieve 1-millimeter resolution, hierarchical phase contrast tomography (HIP-CT) reaches 1 micrometer (roughly 1/100th the diameter of a human hair) by exploiting phase-shift interference patterns in synchrotron X-rays rather than just absorption.
- **Project launched in 2021 to study COVID-19 lung damage** — during the pandemic, Hanover Medical Centre collaborators contacted UCL researchers to understand lung injury in COVID-19 patients, spurring the development of HIP-CT for human imaging.
- **Datasets are terabytes in size, now made publicly accessible** — a major challenge was storing and sharing massive data files while allowing researchers and students worldwide to interact with them meaningfully and explore organs in three dimensions.
- **AI can map blood vessel networks across multiple kidneys to identify structural patterns** — AI-assisted segmentation extracts blood vessel architecture from datasets containing multiple kidneys, revealing how structure changes across age, sex, and disease states like diabetes.
- **Brain white matter can now be visualized as individual axons and their wiring patterns** — HIP-CT reveals individual axons (long neuronal projections connecting distant brain regions) and their orientations, helping researchers map the brain's "wiring diagram."
- **Surgeons can prepare for congenital heart disease repair using exact anatomical maps** — HIP-CT scans show where structures unexpectedly relocate in congenital conditions, allowing surgeons to plan procedures with high precision.
- **Whole-body cadaver scanning within 5 years is the goal** — currently individual organs are scanned, but the team plans to scan intact human cadavers to preserve the connections and interactions between organs, revealing how systems work holistically rather than in isolation.

**Origins During the Pandemic and the Human Organ Atlas**

The Human Organ Atlas grew out of an urgent clinical need. In 2021, during the COVID-19 pandemic, medical collaborators at Hanover Medical Centre reached out to Professor Peter Lee at University College London seeking a way to better understand how the virus damaged lungs in critically ill patients. Lee connected them with Paul Taffero at the European Synchrotron Radiation Facility (ESRF) in Grenoble, France. Together, they adapted synchrotron imaging technology—previously used for crystallography and materials science—to scan human organs at unprecedented detail. The team quickly realized the technique had potential far beyond COVID lungs, spurring the creation of a comprehensive public database of 56 human organs available to researchers and students worldwide.

**How Synchrotrons Differ From Particle Smashers**

Unlike the famous Large Hadron Collider at CERN, which smashes particles together at extreme energies, synchrotrons like the ESRF operate differently. They accelerate electrons around a ring roughly 800 meters in circumference at near light-speed. As the electrons bend along the circular path, they emit synchrotron radiation—extremely bright, focused X-rays in the form of a thin beam. This radiation is fundamentally different from the X-rays produced by hospital equipment: it is far more "brilliant," meaning it is intensely focused and orders of magnitude brighter. Synchrotrons perform many roles at research facilities, including crystallography and imaging of materials like car parts, airplane components, and batteries; human organ imaging is now one of their applications.

**HIP-CT: A Supercharged CT Scan**

HIP-CT (hierarchical phase contrast tomography) uses the same basic principle as hospital CT scanning: an X-ray beam passes through an organ, which is rotated slowly while images are captured from many angles. The critical difference lies in what the technique measures. Hospital CT relies on absorption—bone absorbs X-rays strongly and appears bright, while soft tissue is more transparent and appears dark. HIP-CT exploits phase contrast instead. As X-ray waves pass through tissues of different densities, the waves shift relative to one another, creating interference patterns. By measuring these patterns, HIP-CT can detect minute differences in tissue density invisible to standard CT. The result is extraordinary resolution: 1 micrometer, compared to 1 millimeter for hospital CT—a thousand-fold improvement. To put this in perspective, one micrometer is roughly one-hundredth the diameter of a human hair.

**Managing Terabyte Datasets and Public Access**

The high resolution produces enormous data files; a single organ scan can be several terabytes. A major engineering challenge was making these massive datasets available to researchers and students worldwide without requiring them to download entire files locally. The team built an interactive viewing portal modeled on Google Earth, allowing anyone to explore organs in three dimensions, zoom into regions of interest, and access progressively higher resolution as they zoom in. This web-based approach democratizes access to anatomy that was once visible only to those performing physical dissections.

**AI Applications: Mapping Blood Vessels and Brain Wiring**

AI-assisted segmentation unlocks powerful research possibilities. One focus is extracting blood vessel networks from kidneys across multiple donors. By analyzing the vasculature across multiple kidneys, researchers can compare how blood vessel architecture differs by age, sex, and disease—for example, how diabetes alters kidney structure. Another project maps white matter in the brain, which consists of axons, long cellular projections connecting distant brain regions. At HIP-CT resolution, individual axons become visible, and their orientations can be mapped, effectively revealing a wiring diagram of the human brain. This level of detail has never before been systematically available.

**Surgical Planning and Congenital Heart Disease**

One immediate clinical application is surgical planning for congenital heart disease. In these conditions, cardiac structures that are normally in one location can be displaced or malformed. A surgeon preparing to repair such a heart needs to know exactly where structures are located. High-resolution HIP-CT scans provide that anatomical precision, enabling surgeons to plan procedures with confidence and likely improving outcomes and surgical efficiency.

**Limitations and Future Directions**

HIP-CT has constraints. It works only on cadaveric tissue; the X-ray dose would be too high for living patients. The technique is also static—organs are fixed in place. Dynamic conditions where blood flows or other fluid dynamics play a central role, such as blood cancers affecting white blood cell function, remain invisible. Dr. Walsh notes that many human ailments involve dynamic rather than structural changes, limiting HIP-CT's current scope in those cases.

The next frontier is whole-body cadaver scanning. Currently, individual organs are scanned separately, which severs their natural connections. The heart and lungs, for instance, function together as an integrated system; scanning them in isolation loses this crucial connectivity. The team aims within five years to scan an intact cadaver, preserving all inter-organ relationships. This holistic view would provide unprecedented insight into how the body's systems work together and what goes wrong in disease.

**The Kidney as an Unsung Hero**

When asked about her favorite organ, Dr. Walsh highlighted the kidney. Despite being underrecognized, it is a beautiful structure with distinct functional compartments, and kidney disease represents an enormous health burden worldwide. HIP-CT offers a powerful tool for understanding kidney dysfunction and may help mitigate problems like transplant rejection and chronic kidney disease—conditions affecting millions globally.

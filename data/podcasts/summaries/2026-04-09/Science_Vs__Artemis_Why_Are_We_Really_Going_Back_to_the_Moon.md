# Science Vs — "Artemis: Why Are We Really Going Back to the Moon?"

**Host:** Wendy Zuckerman. **Guests:** Ángel Abud-Madrid (director, Center for Space Resources, Colorado School of Mines; researcher on lunar resource extraction); Nicole Zellner (professor of physics, Albion College; Mars exploration advocate); Greg Hallinan (professor of astronomy, Caltech; designer of Farsight lunar telescope).

## TL;DR

- **Artemis II orbited the moon with four astronauts in April 2026**, traveling further into space than humans ever have before and transmitting stunning images that sparked renewed interest in lunar exploration.
- **Rare earth elements on the moon are not economically viable to mine** — while moon rocks contain terbium and other rare earths used in phones and solar panels, they are too diluted in moon dust to extract cost-effectively compared to Earth sources, which are not actually rare despite the name.
- **Helium-3 fusion fuel is a future fantasy, not a near-term possibility** — the moon contains more helium-3 than Earth, and 220 pounds could power a city like Dallas for a year, but nuclear fusion reactors that use it do not yet exist and scientists are still working on controlling fusion after decades of effort.
- **The moon serves as a training ground for Mars, not a launch pad** — NASA plans to land humans on the moon within two years and build a lunar base to solve practical challenges like handling corrosive moon dust and finding water sources before attempting the six-month journey to Mars.
- **The Farsight Array, a radio telescope on the moon's far side, could reveal the cosmic Dark Ages** — by detecting hydrogen radio waves from 14 billion years ago, it could show how the universe evolved from a dark, featureless state to one filled with stars and galaxies, answering fundamental questions about cosmic origins.
- **The Farsight Array could also detect magnetospheres on exoplanets** — radio telescopes can identify magnetic fields that may be necessary for life, potentially discovering the first evidence of life outside Earth's solar system.
- **The moon would serve as a refueling station for deep space missions** — water on the moon could be split into hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel, allowing spacecraft to launch from the moon's weaker gravity rather than Earth's.
- **Most lunar resource claims rest on political competition, not scientific necessity** — while the original Apollo program was driven by US-Soviet rivalry, the renewed interest follows China's lunar rover landing and reflects countries realizing "there's something in it for us."

**The Artemis II Mission and Renewed Lunar Interest**

Artemis II launched in April 2026, carrying four astronauts around the moon—further into space than any human had ever traveled. The mission transmitted striking images of lunar craters and the far side of the moon, along with views of Earth that sparked visceral reactions from observers worldwide. Astronauts reported seeing specific features like Copernicus and Rainier Gamma craters. The mission also provided practical lessons: the spacecraft's toilet malfunctioned, requiring astronauts to use bags for waste, and crew members found the sleeping quarters uncomfortably cold and wished for different equipment. These details matter because they represent the first real-world data about surviving extended lunar missions.

The mission's visibility coincided with a global shift in lunar interest. China's lunar rover landed on the far side of the moon, India launched toward the moon, and Israel, South Korea, and Japan all pursued lunar programs. According to Abud-Madrid, there are now 73 space agencies engaged in lunar activities, with 20 more being planned. This represents a dramatic increase from previous decades when, following the original Apollo era, lunar interest had waned.

**The Lunar Gold Rush: Rare Earth Elements**

One argument for returning to the moon centers on rare earth elements—elements like terbium, neodymium, and dysprosium that are critical to modern technology. They appear in phones (where terbium creates green displays), renewable energy systems, and electric cars. Apollo astronauts brought back moon rocks containing these elements, leading some to propose mining the moon as an alternative to Earth-based extraction, which pollutes soil and waterways and is concentrated in China.

However, Abud-Madrid directly challenges the economics. Despite their name, rare earth elements are not actually rare on Earth—the concentration of mining in China reflects current geopolitics, not scarcity. More critically, the moon's rare earth hotspot, located on the lunar surface's right eye, contains elements so diluted in moon dirt that extraction would be impractical. Abud-Madrid offers an analogy: extracting rare earths on Earth is like collecting rainwater from Seattle or New Orleans; on the moon, it would be like trying to collect droplets from Death Valley, "probably one of the driest places on earth." He concludes that attempting lunar mining "is not worth it at this point" based on available information, and resources would be better spent developing safer Earth-based mining and improving recycling of existing electronics.

**Helium-3: The Clean Nuclear Fantasy**

Another oft-cited reason for lunar exploration is helium-3, a isotope of helium that some have called "the best reason to return to the moon in the 21st century." The appeal is theoretically compelling: 220 pounds of helium-3 could generate enough energy to power a city like Dallas for a full year, with no radioactive waste—only harmless helium as a byproduct. The moon contains far more helium-3 than Earth because the solar wind deposited it over billions of years.

The critical catch: the nuclear reactors required to use helium-3 do not exist. Current nuclear power relies on fission—splitting atoms to release energy. Helium-3 requires fusion—combining atoms to generate energy. Scientists have been working on controlling fusion for decades without success. Until fusion is solved, helium-3 remains scientifically interesting but practically useless. Abud-Madrid suggests that if a government wanted to fund a helium-3 moon mission, that money would be better spent elsewhere "at the moment." Some countries may view staking a claim to lunar helium-3 as a long-term bet in case fusion becomes viable, but it is not a justification for immediate action.

**The Moon as a Training Ground for Mars**

While direct missions to Mars are technically feasible (SpaceX plans to attempt this), NASA's strategy centers on using the moon as a proving ground. Nicole Zellner compares it to climbing Mount Everest—you train on smaller peaks first. Mars is six months away from Earth; the moon is three days away. On Mars, if an astronaut forgets a wrench, resupply is impossible. The moon offers a low-stakes laboratory for testing systems and solving real problems.

NASA's plans are detailed and practical. Within two years, they aim to land humans on the moon. Longer-term, they plan a lunar base to address specific challenges. One major problem is moon dust—tiny, abrasive particles created by billions of years of asteroid impacts. Apollo astronauts were blinded and irritated by it; some reported tasting it. A lunar base must solve dust infiltration during airlock cycles. Another critical challenge is water. Astronauts will initially receive water from an orbiting spacecraft (similar to the International Space Station), but the goal is to locate and extract lunar water sources so settlers can become self-sufficient.

**Water as Fuel and a Gateway to the Solar System**

A secondary but important use for lunar water involves rocket fuel. Water consists of hydrogen and oxygen. Hydrogen is a rocket fuel currently used in conventional rockets. If astronauts can split water molecules on the moon, they can extract hydrogen for fueling spacecraft. This offers a strategic advantage: launching from the moon's weaker gravity is more energy-efficient than launching from Earth's strong gravity well. In essence, the moon becomes a gas station for deep-space exploration—a refueling depot that makes longer missions to Mars and beyond more feasible. This technology is well-understood; no breakthroughs are required to implement it.

**The Farsight Lunar Telescope: Solving Cosmic Mysteries**

The most compelling scientific argument for returning to the moon comes from Greg Hallinan, who proposes placing a radio telescope on the moon's far side. Named (awkwardly, by his own admission) the Farsight Array for Radio Science Investigations of the Dark Ages and Exoplanets, the telescope would unfold across an area 10 kilometers across—over six miles.

The motivation is profound. Fourteen billion years ago, the entire universe was a featureless soup of hydrogen and helium following the Big Bang. At some point, stars and galaxies formed from this darkness, but the transition remains one of astronomy's greatest mysteries. Optical telescopes cannot observe this "Dark Ages" period because no visible light existed. However, the hydrogen from that era emitted radio waves that persist today. By detecting and analyzing these ancient radio signals, astronomers could map the universe's evolution from its dark state to the present, answering fundamental questions about how structure emerged from chaos.

Earth-based radio telescopes cannot detect these signals because the atmosphere blocks them entirely—"like looking through a brick wall," Hallinan explains. The moon's far side offers a quiet location where radio signals can be received without interference from Earth's transmitters or atmosphere. Hallinan envisions the result as a "movie" showing the universe's birth and evolution.

**Detecting Life: Magnetospheres on Exoplanets**

Farsight also has a secondary scientific objective: detecting magnetic fields (magnetospheres) around exoplanets. Earth's magnetic field protects life from solar radiation; scientists believe similar fields may be necessary for life on other worlds. Radio telescopes can detect these magnetospheres from exoplanets. If Farsight identified an exoplanet with a magnetosphere, it would constitute evidence—though not proof—that the conditions for life might exist beyond Earth's solar system. Hallinan describes this as his "ultimate dream." He notes that discovering life elsewhere would be "such a profound moment" and would fundamentally alter humanity's understanding of its place in the cosmos.

**Political Theater and Genuine Scientific Benefit**

Wendy Zuckerman notes the irony that while lunar politics echo Cold War rivalries (President Trump posted that "we are winning in space"), the actual science emerging from the renewed lunar push may be genuinely transformative. Zellner acknowledges that the Apollo program was "the mother of all pissing contests" but argues that "it's possible for national prestige projects to be fundamentally good in their application." The original moon landings transformed humanity's perspective on itself and the cosmos. Historical data shows that space race spinoffs yielded practical benefits: smaller computers, GPS, and memory foam all emerged from Apollo-era research.

The episode ends with reflection on the visceral human response to images of Earth from space—a "big, lonely blue marble" that appears fragile from orbit. Astronauts and scientists emphasized that distance from Earth reveals its beauty and uniqueness: "this oasis, this beautiful place that we get to exist together" in "a whole bunch of nothing." While the renewed lunar race may be rooted in geopolitical competition, the scientific discoveries and philosophical insights emerging from it offer humanity something rare: a shared project of exploration that serves purposes beyond dominance.

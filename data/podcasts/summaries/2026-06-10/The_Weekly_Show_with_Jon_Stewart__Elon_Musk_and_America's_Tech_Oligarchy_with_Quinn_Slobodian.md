# The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart — "Elon Musk and America's Tech Oligarchy"

**Host:** Jon Stewart. **Guests:** Quinn Slobodian (professor of international history, Boston University; co-author of *Muskism* and *Hayek's Bastards*; historian of neoliberalism and tech capitalism).

## TL;DR

- **"Muskism" as parallax to Fordism** — Ford secured worker consent through wage raises, trade union recognition, and social services; Musk captures capital markets with no social compact, leaving wages stagnant and people's lives worse, as digital tech companies now drive the entire U.S. economy.
- **SpaceX was born from post-9/11 military doctrine** — Rumsfeld proposed "network-centric warfare" needing satellites; Musk undercut Boeing by 90% on launch costs, making SpaceX indispensable to the DOD, starting 2002.
- **Tesla got Obama's $500M clean-tech loan (2009), then fracking reversed U.S. climate policy** — America pivoted from electric autonomy to domestic oil dominance, leaving China alone on the renewables path.
- **Tech oligarchs use multi-layered voting control** — Dual-class share structures (Zuckerberg, Musk) give founders 10–20x votes per share; Musk will hold 85% of shareholder votes needed to dislodge him after SpaceX's Friday IPO ($1.75 trillion valuation).
- **DOGE framed as efficiency but enforced ideology** — Cut was ideological (DEI, foreign aid, animal disease research) not technocratic; integrated databases across agencies to enable mass deportations; no fiscal savings achieved.
- **"Suicidal empathy" reframes diversity as exploitable bugs** — Musk borrows from Gadhafi-adjacent psychologists; sees empathy, immigration, anti-racism as flaws requiring override for system optimization; views NPCs (non-player characters) and illegal immigrants as data to be removed.
- **Silicon Valley broke the Fordist bargain and faces grassroots pushback** — Proposal for sovereign wealth funds (Bernie Sanders, Trump interest) emerges as "revolution insurance," but tech leaders scrambling after overestimating a "supine population" now face anti-AI data center polling that's unprecedented in American consensus.
- **AI companies prioritize avoiding regulation over any other concern** — When Lena Khan proposed antitrust at FTC, tech class openly preferred a "fascist" over capital gains taxation or antitrust; SpaceX IPO designed to make company "too big to fail" and lock in subsidies permanently.

**The Rise of Muskism vs. Fordism**

Slobodian frames the central tension: capitalism always reorganizes society around new technology and government relations, but the digital era concentrates disproportionate power in a small number of firms. These firms now mediate all everyday interaction—cloud storage, streaming, payments, spreadsheets—creating asymmetrical dependency on individuals like Musk who can unilaterally cut off state capacity. The Ukraine Starlink incident exemplifies this: Musk disabled satellite service during a Ukrainian offensive into Crimea on his whim. Fordism, by contrast, secured social consent through a compromise: workers got wage raises, union recognition, and social services in exchange for productivity. The outcome was a virtuous cycle: healthy workers meant functional factories, justifying post-war social programs like Social Security and Medicare. Today's digital capitalism inverts this. Wages stagnate, precarity rises, and tech leaders offer no reciprocal stability to justify their control.

**Government Seeding and Public-Private Partnership**

The federal government did not invent Fordism directly but created conditions for it: favorable laws, mass production, and military demand (Keynesian defense spending). The Internet itself emerged from military R&D before being commercialized. SpaceX exemplifies this lineage. After 9/11, Rumsfeld outlined a new Pentagon doctrine: replace large land armies with mobile special forces guided by satellite-enabled "network-centric warfare." Musk founded SpaceX in 2002, the first full year of the war on terror, and undercut Boeing's launch costs by over 90% within two decades, becoming indispensable to U.S. military operations. Tesla received a nearly $500 million loan from Obama's Department of Energy in 2009 as part of a green-tech push to reduce Middle Eastern oil dependency. But fracking was discovered a few years later, reversing that trajectory: America became a petroleum giant and abandoned renewables, while China stayed on the electric path and now dominates EV and lithium-ion battery markets. The state bore the upfront risk; tech capitalists captured the upside.

**The Hacked Public Corporation and Techno-Kings**

Slobodian identifies a critical shift: Zuckerberg and Musk redesigned corporate governance using dual-class shares. Founders' shares carry 10–20x voting power per share relative to public shareholders, stripping away the check-and-balance that public ownership was supposed to provide. Ford never went public and hated markets, fearing "coastal manipulators" diluted his workshop-of-the-Midwest authority. Modern founders get the opposite: global capital via IPO without accountability. Musk will retain 85% of the votes needed to remove him as SpaceX CEO after this Friday's IPO (valued at $1.75 trillion, the largest in history). He can tweet at 3 a.m., move stock prices and crypto markets within seconds, and give unlimited campaign funds under current law. This historically unprecedented concentration of private power dwarfs even Henry Ford's reach.

**DOGE: Ideology Dressed as Efficiency**

Stewart and Slobodian examine the Department of Government Efficiency. Musk framed it as technocratic, but the actual cuts were ideological: DEI programs, foreign aid to developing nations (like USAID), and animal disease research (screwworm containment). No genuine fiscal savings materialized. However, one genuine achievement: DOGE integrated databases across federal agencies that had been deliberately kept separate, enabling more efficient location and targeting of populations for deportation. Slobodian argues this reveals the difference between neoliberal austerity (which at least maintains the fiction of neutral, agentless market forces) and Muskism (which openly embraces centralized command, survival-of-the-fittest missions, and the need for citizens to "find your place in the command structure").

**The Computational Worldview and "Suicidal Empathy"**

Musk's thought originates in computing. He first programmed on a Commodore as a teenager, connected to the Internet before leaving South Africa, and has since viewed the world as a system running on code. When things conflict with his interests, he treats them as bugs or viruses needing fixing. At DOGE, he described his role as reprogramming the Matrix, excising bugs, viruses, bots, vampires, and NPCs. In this frame, illegal immigrants, perceived Democratic voters, multiculturalism, and anti-racism are exploitable flaws in the system's operating code. Slobodian traces this back to "suicidal empathy," a concept Musk borrows from Gadhafi-adjacent psychologists: empathy, immigration, and diversity are bugs in human software that must be overridden for system optimization. This language echoes Nazi descriptions of Jews as "pests" and "vermin," though Slobodian notes the computational metaphor obscures the dehumanization. Musk even toggled between playing dungeon-crawling video games (Path of Exile, Diablo 4) and executing DOGE while at work, using the same speed-and-kill-count logic.

**Effective Altruism, Long-Termism, and Mars Futures**

A related ideology animating tech leaders is effective altruism and long-termism. Musk and Sam Altman anchored these ideas at OpenAI's founding in 2015. Long-termism argues that optimizing for the next few generations is myopic; instead, priority should go to breakthrough technologies enabling human life to propagate across sextillions of digital copies on interplanetary mainframes. Once that frame applies, present-day humanitarian instincts (feeding the hungry, curing malaria) become obsolete. Slobodian notes Bill Gates still offers "malaria nets for a software monopoly" as a social gesture; Musk skips even the gesture. This explains why AI data center buildouts are framed as civilization-saving missions, not as profitable ventures. The implied bargain: submit to digital infrastructure domination or face extinction.

**The Sovereignty Question and Silicon Valley's China Envy**

Stewart pivots to structural resilience. Silicon Valley's ideology increasingly envies China's ability to move fast—setting goals, clearing regulations, consolidating winners. But Slobodian argues tech leaders misdiagnose China's success. China doesn't work because it eliminates democratic veto points; it works because it disciplines capital. The Chinese state sets investment targets, refuses wasteful behavior from the financial class (share buybacks, short-term chasing), and maintains control of the investment function. America's problem is not NIMBY objections; it's rampant financialism. The SpaceX IPO, giving 30% to retail investors, exemplifies this. Musk needs a cult following hanging on every tweet to drive valuations, whereas traditional investors demand fundamentals. American capitalism privatizes profits and socializes losses—the opposite of disciplined capital allocation. Musk becoming too big to fail locks in indefinite subsidies and removes downside risk.

**Grassroots Resistance and the Failure of Political Solutions**

Slobodian notes there is no political constituency for resistance because the entire American economy has bet on generative AI. Both parties court Silicon Valley. The Biden interlude attempted diversification (green manufacturing, biotech, higher education), but even that was undercut by hawkish anti-China industrial policy. Governors like Rahm Emanuel and Gavin Newsom will quickly make nice with tech oligarchs because no alternative appears available. Resistance is coming from the grassroots instead: poll numbers on AI data centers show unprecedented American consensus against them (people mostly don't fear job loss but despise being lied to by generative AI, losing shared reality). Sanders and even Trump have floated sovereign wealth fund proposals—taking an equity stake in AI companies and distributing returns. Slobodian credits Sanders for opening this conversation but notes the danger: if Americans become equity holders in AI, they become invested in its success, eliminating the remaining check (skepticism) and turning everyone into little AI barons betting on a single technology.

**Cyborg Conservatism and Emancipatory Networks**

Slobodian invokes Donna Haraway's 1980s essay "The Cyborg Manifesto." Haraway argued that computing emerged from military-industrial needs but contained liberatory potential: the Internet dissolved traditional gender and racial hierarchies (the slogan "on the internet, nobody knows you're a dog" crystallizes this). What Musk is attempting is "cyborg conservatism": keep all the technology but strip the emancipation. Hierarchies are reinforced, surveillance is consolidated, and control is restored to programmers. The programmer-vs-user-vs-NPC frame is existential. From 2020–2021, networks enabled the largest street protests in U.S. history, trans rights movements, and #MeToo—horizontal communication that terrified oligarchs. Musk's move to buy and control Twitter was a direct response: seize the technology that enabled uncontrolled solidarity before it destabilizes power again.

**Conclusion and Optimism Through Cycles**

Stewart closes by noting that history isn't one-way domination. Technology has cycles: emancipatory use triggers oligarch panic, which triggers counter-power, which opens new possibilities. The book's publication and public conversation signal the backlash has begun. Slobodian emphasizes none of this is inevitable. Forces exist to push back. The American design problem is not that oligarchs are evil but that we have chosen to de-risk capital completely while imposing all downside on labor and nature. Recalibrating requires disciplining capital: less profit for oligarchs, more public control of investment, restoration of Fordist-era checks (unions, social services, shared sacrifice). It's possible; it requires political will and grassroots pressure to override the locked-in preferences of a bipartisan elite that has bet everything on tech monopoly.

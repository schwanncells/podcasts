# Embedded — "Where is Austin Tice?: We believe he's alive"

**Host:** Kelly McEvers. **Guests:** Josh Baker (investigative journalist, BBC Radio 4 and NPR; spent nearly a decade working in Syria; experienced in locating missing persons); Sara Obidat (Jordanian investigative journalist; researched Syrian prison system for years).

## TL;DR

- **American journalist Austin Tice vanished in Syria on August 11, 2012** — he was on a summer reporting trip as a Georgetown law student and has not been seen or heard from in 14 years.
- **Deborah Tice, Austin's mother, has attracted attention from every U.S. president, the Pope, and world leaders** — she has led an unwavering campaign to locate her son, refusing to accept that he has been forgotten.
- **A Syrian intelligence source told investigators Austin was seen alive eight months ago on a hunger strike** — the source claims Austin is held at Palestine Branch, a notorious Syrian intelligence facility, and is treated better than other prisoners because he is a high-value hostage.
- **Assad's regime offers no strategic reason to kill Austin and every reason to keep him alive** — sources argue he functions as leverage against the U.S. government, making his survival more likely than death.
- **The Syrian regime fell in December 2024, suddenly making intelligence facilities accessible for the first time** — Assad fled to Russia and control of Syria dissolved into chaos and rebel hands.
- **Josh Baker traveled to Syria within 48 hours of the regime's collapse** — he met with contacts who provided a purple folder containing GPS coordinates and details of every location where Austin is possibly known to have been held.

**Austin Tice's Disappearance and Background**

Austin Tice was a U.S. Marine veteran, photojournalist, and Georgetown law student who traveled to Syria in summer 2012 to pursue his dream of becoming a war correspondent. On August 11, 2012, his 31st birthday, he called his mother Deborah for what would be the last time they'd speak, a FaceTime call where "we had the best FaceTime." Days later, while Deborah was on a camping trip with no cell service, her husband Mark delivered devastating news: Austin had been missing for almost a week. When Deborah emerged from the trip, she heard Austin's own voice on NPR—his recorded interview aired even as authorities were searching for him. She was simultaneously hearing that her son was missing and hearing him speak from Syria, what she describes as "dystopia."

**Deborah's Twelve-Year Search**

Deborah Tice has never wavered in her belief that Austin is alive. In the twelve years since his disappearance, she has built a relentless campaign to keep his case in public view. She has attended press conferences flanked by Austin's siblings, displayed his possessions—including his Converse All-Stars with his phone number written inside—and secured messages from world leaders. U.S. presidents have each paid attention to his case. At a National Press Club gathering marking the twelve-year anniversary, Deborah spoke about Austin's determination as a child and his unwavering goal to become an international reporter for NPR. She tells a story of young Austin calling Georgetown University repeatedly, threatening to apply each semester until they accepted him. That same determination, she believes, sustains him still. "I know that my son is somewhere alive," she says. "I haven't seen my son since 2012. That's the last time I hugged him."

**Intelligence Breakthrough: Salam's Information**

Josh Baker and Sara Obidat met with a Syrian contact named Salam, a former Syrian army officer who defected during the uprising and now gathers intelligence on Assad's security services. Salam's network includes sources inside Assad's own security apparatus. When asked about the possibility of Austin surviving twelve years in captivity, Salam explained the regime's calculation: "The Assad security regime, they are smart enough to know that he's a golden egg. They are very aware of how much the Western communities cares about their citizens and how big pressure they can put on the Western governments." He said the instructions would be "to keep him alive, to protect him, to treat him better than other prisoners." Salam revealed a startling piece of information: eight months before the investigation (around April 2024), a doctor was called to see an American journalist who looked like Austin because he was on a hunger strike. This would represent the most recent alleged sighting of Austin alive. Salam indicated Austin had been moved to Palestine Branch (also known as Branch 235), a known Syrian intelligence facility, where he was "treated differently from the other prisoners and not in the normal prisons."

**The Fall of Assad's Regime**

In early December 2024, rebel forces launched a lightning offensive across Syria, seizing territory from Assad's forces for the first time in years. The second-largest city, Aleppo, fell to rebels. The capital Damascus was breached. President Bashar al-Assad fled to Russia, and his regime collapsed. President Biden addressed the fall of the regime publicly, acknowledging Austin Tice directly: "We are mindful that there are Americans in Syria, including those who reside there, as well as Austin Tice, who was taken captain more than 12 years ago. We remain committed to returning him to his family." When pressed on whether there was proof Austin was alive, Biden stated: "We believe he's alive. We think we can get him back, but we have no direct evidence of that yet." The collapse opened unprecedented access: the intelligence facilities that had been tightly guarded for years were suddenly vulnerable to investigation.

**Into Syria: The Window Closes**

Just 48 hours after the regime fell, Josh Baker and Sara Obidat crossed into Syria from Lebanon by road (airports were abandoned). The journey revealed a country in chaos—abandoned checkpoints with smashed windows, darkened highways, and armed fighters controlling the roads. Checkpoints that had once been impenetrable were now unmanned. Baker noted the surreal nature of entering a country so quickly: "I've never entered this country so quickly. I've never entered any country so quickly. No one knows that I'm here. No one's checked my passport. Welcome to the new Syria." They arrived in Damascus and checked into a hotel. Before leaving for the journey, Baker had received a crucial contact from a source with intimate knowledge of Austin's case. The meeting took place outside a coffee shop in an undisclosed location, where the source handed him a purple folder with a simple instruction: "Find Austin."

**The Purple Folder and Ongoing Search**

When Baker and Obidat had time to examine the folder in Obidat's hotel room, they discovered it contained a list with GPS coordinates of every location where Austin was possibly known to have been held. For years, Western intelligence agencies had identified these sites but could not access them because they were under tight Syrian military guard. Now, with the regime gone, those coordinates represented a roadmap to potential answers. Baker describes the moment: "For years, Western intelligence agencies have been identifying sites where Austin might be held. But they couldn't go to them because they were tightly guarded by Syrian forces. Now that the regime is gone, we can search them. If Austin's being held in a Syrian prison, this might be the best chance in more than a decade that anyone has had to find him."

# Embedded — "Where is Austin Tice?: A black box"

**Host:** Josh Baker (NPR/BBC Radio 4). **Guests:** Sara Obeidat (investigative journalist; co-host of the series; experienced in Syrian detention and security matters).

## TL;DR

- **Assad regime fell on December 8, 2024**, ending 50 years of rule after Syrian news anchor announced the regime's collapse on live television.
- **Over 100,000 people have disappeared into Syria's prison system** since the civil war began; Austin Tice, an American journalist, vanished more than a decade ago and remains missing.
- **US government offers $10 million reward** for information leading to Austin's recovery and return, signaling Austin's case as a national priority.
- **Palestine Branch prison contained one-way mirrors, hidden interrogation rooms, and cells with torture evidence**—walls marked with messages in English, blood, urine-based invisible ink, and bleakness ("don't trust anyone, even your own brother").
- **Regime hospital morgue held dozens of bodies** in refrigerated units and contained files documenting deaths by starvation, bruising, and violence despite cause-of-death listed as "heart attack."
- **US Special Presidential Envoy Roger Carstens admits the US doesn't know if Austin is alive**, who took him, or what happened to him—describing the situation as a "black box" of conflicting intelligence reports.
- **Investigative journalists discover hidden intelligence facility** containing Austin Tice's secret Syrian intelligence files—"hundreds of pages long"—never meant to be seen by outside world.

---

**The Regime's Fall and the Hunt Begins**

On December 8, 2024, the Syrian regime under Bashar al-Assad collapsed after 50 years in power. A Syrian news anchor appeared on television and announced the moment formally, marking a historic turning point. Josh Baker arrived in Damascus shortly after, describing the city as suspended in chaos—shops shuttered, streets empty, surveillance drones overhead, Israeli airstrikes striking weapons stockpiles. Among the pictures of missing people plastered on walls are thousands who vanished into the Syrian prison system. Austin Tice, an American journalist who disappeared over a decade ago while working in Syria, is one of them. Baker had been given a purple folder by Western intelligence agencies containing coordinates for detention facilities where Austin might have been held. With the regime gone, for the first time, the team could access these sites.

**Palestine Branch Prison: A Machinery of Torture**

Baker and Obeidat entered Palestine Branch, a notorious military intelligence facility (Syrian Military Intelligence 235) that began as a security apparatus for Palestinian matters but expanded its reach into every corner of Syrian society—surveillance, interrogation, detention. The building was a concrete structure surrounded by high walls and barbed wire. Inside, they found an interrogation room with a camera and a one-way mirror overlooking a second room with comfortable chairs where observers watched prisoners being questioned. The interrogation room itself was pitch black when the mirror door closed, creating psychological terror. The cells were partially flooded, reeked of urine, and walls were covered with scratched messages in multiple languages, including English. Using an ultraviolet light (exploiting a technique where urine serves as invisible ink), Baker discovered even more messages. One read, "I passed from here in March." Another carried the grim warning: "don't trust anyone, even your own brother"—evidence of the regime's pervasive surveillance where families informing on each other was commonplace. No marks or signs definitively indicated Austin had been held there, though his father had given Baker a list of phrases and markers to search for.

**False Hope and a Mother's Conviction**

The US government's $10 million reward sparked hundreds of tips, many false. Social media erupted when a journalist posted video of a man claimed to be Austin Tice. It turned out to be Travis Timmerman, an American from Missouri who had been held for seven months on a religious pilgrimage. The mix-up illustrated how difficult it is to track who is or isn't imprisoned in Syria. Austin's mother, Deborah Tice, appeared on Meet the Press. Though disappointed by the false alarm, she remained convinced Austin would be found "at any moment" and drew hope from a man held in silence for 34 years who was reunited with his family. "We're just really excited about being a reunited family," she said. Baker noted the contrast between Deborah's certainty and the actual uncertainty surrounding Austin's fate.

**A Mountain Mission Under Fire**

The team received a tip that Austin might be held in a cell several stories underground under a mountain outside Damascus, at a Syrian-Iranian weapons depot that Israeli forces were bombing. The location was inaccessible because of the ongoing bombardment. By coincidence, the team was only 10 minutes away and decided to drive up the mountain road. Gunshots rang out, forcing them to turn around. Armed men in combat fatigues stopped the car. They apologized for being "jumpy," explaining they had lost many people the previous day during Israeli strikes. When asked if they had searched the facility, they confirmed they had and found nobody there. The rumor proved false.

**The Regime Hospital: A Catalogue of Suffering**

The team searched a former regime hospital where injured prisoners had been sent. It felt untouched—tea still sat at the reception desk, a doctor's stethoscope hung at a window. Sarah Obeidat led Baker down narrow stairs to the basement where detainees were held. The air grew thick with dust. They found a line of blood dragged hundreds of meters along the floor—someone's body had been pulled out of the facility. In a sealed room with refrigerated units (used as makeshift morgues), they discovered dozens of bodies stacked on top of each other. Files filled floor-to-ceiling shelves. These were not medical records of the living but archives of the dead—photographs of thin, starved bodies bearing bruises, cuts, and signs of violence. Some files listed the cause of death as "heart attack," but injuries told a different story. One file was labeled only "body parts"; another appeared to contain remains of a child. At the end of a corridor, the team found a section marked "Foreigners." As they reached for those files, their armed escort became extremely nervous after receiving a phone call and ordered everyone out immediately. The rebels now in control were beginning to restrict media access to detention sites, sensitive because families were demanding answers about the missing while those in power tried to control both the sites and the narrative.

**The US Government's Uncertainty**

Roger Carstens, the US Special Presidential Envoy for Hostage Affairs, met with Baker at the Four Seasons hotel in Damascus twelve days after Assad's fall. Carstens explained that the US government was trying to find "any information, whether they be interviews or documents" to help determine where Austin is, what happened to him, and his current status. When Baker pressed whether, given all the information the US has access to, he was beginning to worry Austin is no longer alive, Carstens replied carefully: "There's no proof that Austin is deceased. And so I have to assume that he's alive and he's still waiting for us to find him." However, when asked if there was "conflicting information" about whether Austin is alive, Carstens said: "Well, if I answer that fully, I'd go to jail. But I think picture someone disappearing in Syria that to an extent is a black box in terms of gathering information." He explained that sources provide "information that is partially true, false, true," making verification extremely difficult. The Syrian regime had never confirmed having Austin, never offered proof of life, and always denied ever holding him. Strikingly, despite years of public statements suggesting the US could bring Austin home, the US government appeared not to know definitively who took him, what happened to him, or even if he is still alive.

**The Discovery**

A sensitive contact informed Baker that a Syrian commander on the ground had agreed to guide them to a location containing "crucial information about Austin Tice." The team had roughly half an hour before sunset to reach an intelligence facility and exit. They raced from room to room, following FaceTime instructions from the commander. At a locked gate with a five-story drop beyond it, the commander instructed them to find another way. Baker found a narrow ledge, took a deep breath, and climbed around the gate, legs dangling over the abyss. On the other side was a massive locked safe. The commander's messages directed them to find keys in a general's office. After searching frantically—"maybe, yeah, maybe"—they located the key and opened the safe. Inside were Austin Tice's secret Syrian intelligence files: "hundreds of pages long," material that was never meant to be seen by the outside world. The team photographed the files to preserve evidence without removing them.

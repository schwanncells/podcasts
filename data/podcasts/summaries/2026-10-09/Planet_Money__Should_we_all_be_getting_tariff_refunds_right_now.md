# Planet Money — "Should we all be getting tariff refunds right now?"

**Host:** Kenny Malone. **Guests:** Amy Choi (Planet Money listener and Playdate buyer); Cable Sasser (CEO and co-founder of Panic, the Portland company that makes the Playdate handheld console); Maureen Thorson (trade attorney, representing major corporate importers, described as one of the country's top trade lawyers).

## TL;DR

- **$122 billion in IEPA tariff refunds paid to companies by early September** — but consumers who bore the cost through higher prices are not directly eligible; only the corporate importers who paid at the border can file for refunds.
- **Playdate maker Panic automatically refunded $14.02 tariff charges** to every customer who paid the explicit line-item fee, totaling ~$100,000 — reportedly the only company known to have done so.
- **The Supreme Court struck down Trump's IEPA-based tariffs** — the International Emergency Economic Powers Act authorized broad emergency powers but the Court ruled imposing tariffs was not among them; the U.S. government had collected $166–170 billion under those tariffs.
- **Delivery companies (UPS, FedEx, DHL) are building refund systems** for customers who paid tariffs directly on packages arriving at the border — some automatic, some requiring customer action.
- **Companies that raised prices rather than itemizing a tariff charge cannot easily return refunds** — untangling how much of any price increase was tariffs vs. supply chain costs vs. general inflation is practically impossible.
- **The gaming industry illustrates the full spectrum of corporate responses:** Panic refunded everything; Nintendo offered a 30% game sale for two weeks; Sony and Microsoft kept their tariff refunds, arguing customers accepted the higher prices voluntarily.
- **Panic CEO Cable Sasser acknowledged the "devil on his shoulder"** — the $100,000 refund represented a full employee salary at his 25-person company, but the team deliberated and chose to return it.

**The IEPA Tariff Refund Landscape**

The Supreme Court ruled that President Trump's use of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act to impose tariffs was an overreach — IEPA allows broad emergency powers, but tariffs are not among them. The U.S. government had collected between $166 billion and $170 billion in IEPA tariffs. As of early September, $122 billion had been paid back to corporate importers through a U.S. Customs data portal. Trade attorney Maureen Thorson, who represents some of the nation's largest importers, described the refund process for companies as surprisingly streamlined: importers submit a spreadsheet of customs entry numbers, the agency validates them, and payments follow. "It's sort of like filing your expenses at your company," she said.

The structural catch for consumers is that tariffs are paid by the importer of record — the company, not the end buyer. Companies then passed those costs to consumers through higher prices or explicit line-item charges at checkout. That means the legal right to refunds rests with corporations, and consumers are left to hope companies choose to pass the money back.

**How Companies Passed Tariffs to Consumers**

Thorson identified two distinct scenarios. In the first, a delivery company like UPS or FedEx paid the tariff at the border on a package shipped directly from abroad, then billed the recipient before releasing the item. For these transactions, the delivery carriers are building refund systems — some automatic, some requiring customer action. Planet Money producer James Sneed had this experience and went online to check his refund, only to receive an error message.

The second, far more common scenario involves companies that imported goods, paid tariffs, and then either raised retail prices or added a tariff surcharge. For price increases, Thorson notes the attribution problem is severe: "How do you tell that the price of these paper towels on the store shelf is actually like, hey, these went up by a dollar over the past year and precisely one quarter of that is tariffs." Practically speaking, most of these refund dollars will not reach consumers who paid higher prices. The money goes to shareholders through dividends or gets reinvested — not back to the buyers who absorbed the original cost.

**The Playdate Story**

Panic — a 25-person Portland company best known for helping launch Untitled Goose Game — spent about seven years developing the Playdate, a $229 yellow handheld console with a built-in crank, manufactured in Malaysia. When Trump's tariffs added 10–20% to the ~$120 per-unit manufacturing cost, Panic initially absorbed the losses. After eating roughly $100,000 in tariffs, CEO Cable Sasser decided to pass the cost to customers as a discrete line item at checkout. Each buyer saw a tariff charge (Amy Choi's was $14.02) alongside a lengthy personal explanation Sasser wrote himself — "Just so you know, we hate this. We know money is tight for everyone... Our deepest dream is that we can delete this line item and this page someday soon." Choi, like most buyers, did not click through to read it.

When the IEPA tariffs were struck down and Panic received roughly $200,000 in government refunds — about $100,000 of which corresponded to charges passed to customers — Sasser described the internal deliberation honestly. "On the one shoulder, I got the devil going, hey, maybe you should keep those. Maybe the people won't even remembers." That $100,000 was the equivalent of a full employee salary. After a team discussion, Panic issued automatic refunds to every affected customer with no action required. Choi received an email with the subject line "Playdate tariff update" and the full $14.02 returned to her original card. "I was really touched," she said. "As consumers, we're sort of being conditioned to expect less and less from companies."

**The Gaming Industry Spectrum**

Panic's approach sits at one extreme of a range visible across the gaming industry. Sony and Microsoft both raised PlayStation and Xbox prices during the tariff period, citing "a challenging macroeconomic environment," without breaking increases out as discrete tariff charges. Both have since confirmed they will not refund customers, arguing buyers accepted the higher prices at time of purchase. Nintendo took a middle path: acknowledging its tariff refund windfall, the company ran a two-week 30% sale on Nintendo games — directing value back to the consumer base without the logistical complexity of tracing individual transactions across multiple retail channels. The sale has since ended.

Thorson noted that for companies which raised prices rather than itemizing tariffs, direct refunds face genuine administrative barriers: identifying who overpaid by how much could cost more than the per-customer refund amount. Panic's situation was uniquely tractable because every tariff charge was a discrete, traceable line item in a direct-to-consumer checkout system. An employee named Kyle wrote the refund code.

**What Consumers Can Do**

For consumers who paid tariff charges explicitly through UPS, FedEx, or DHL, checking directly with the carrier is the most viable path to a refund. For those who paid higher retail prices — the far more common situation — meaningful recovery is unlikely without legal action or a company voluntarily choosing to pass refunds back. Planet Money solicited listener tariff refund stories broadly and found the Playdate/Panic case was the only known example of a company proactively refunding consumers.

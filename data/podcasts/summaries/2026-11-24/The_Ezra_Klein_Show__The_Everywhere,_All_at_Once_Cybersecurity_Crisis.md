# The Ezra Klein Show — "The 'Everywhere, All at Once' Cybersecurity Crisis"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Nicole Perloth (cybersecurity reporter, former New York Times; author of *This Is How They Tell Me the World Ends*; founder and managing partner, Silver Buckshot Ventures; host, *To Catch a Thief* podcast).

## TL;DR

- **AI-enabled hacking has entered "machine speed"** — Anthropic's Mythos model (released April 2026) was so effective at finding vulnerabilities that it was restricted to 40 key firms; open-source tools like Artex now let any attacker automate the entire attack chain, eliminating the skill bottleneck that once limited hacking to dozens or thousands of elite actors.
- **Recent outages (FAA, Outlook, Chase, Apple iCloud) were self-inflicted** — companies rushed to patch vulnerabilities discovered by AI, breaking systems in the process; incident response firms report 50% surge in breaches and are turning away victims even in nation-state cases due to capacity overload.
- **PHP (70% of web) and Java vulnerabilities discovered by AI** — once vulnerabilities exist in foundational software, attackers can exploit them "at machine speed" across millions of systems; the asymmetry between finding vulnerabilities (now automated) and patching them (still manual and risky) is the core crisis.
- **Government and water infrastructure most vulnerable** — many critical systems run unsupported software that can't receive patches; water treatment facilities rarely have in-house IT staff and rely on remote contractor access; the FAA still uses decades-old routers; procurement and "organizational latency" make modernization glacially slow.
- **The "digital deficit" has come due** — decades of "move fast and break things" philosophy embedded faulty software everywhere; companies and governments now face a crisis: deploy patches so fast they break systems (as recent outages show), or leave vulnerabilities open to constant AI-powered attack.
- **Haves vs. have-nots in cyber** — banks with existential risk perception have "blank check" budgets and hire intelligence-agency personnel; smaller firms and government agencies lack resources and personnel, leaving supply chains and critical infrastructure exposed; smaller companies must choose between investing in AI products or cybersecurity.
- **Deepfakes and social engineering are the hardest problem** — AI-generated voice clones are calling CFOs claiming to be the CEO; organizations already experiencing audio deepfake-based extortion; phishing is becoming nearly undetectable; best defense is code words with family/colleagues and treating authentication at the highest level of institutional paranoia.
- **Low-trust internet is returning** — after 30 years of assuming emails, videos, and websites were authentic, people must now assume everything could be AI-generated deepfake or spoofed; the transition will be chaotic, but on the far side, AI may help write more secure code if we survive the transition.

**How AI Changed Hacking From Scarce Skill to Commodified Threat**

For decades, hacking was limited by how many skilled people existed. Finding zero-day vulnerabilities in complex systems required expert knowledge and took thousands of person-hours. Perloth explains that Anthropic's Mythos model, released in April 2026, was so effective at discovering and exploiting vulnerabilities that the company only distributed it to roughly 40 key firms through Project Glasswing, fearing broader release would cause the internet to "break." Now, open-source tools like Artex — originally designed as an enterprise penetration testing framework — allow any attacker to automate most or all steps of the attack chain: finding vulnerabilities, exploiting them, escalating privileges, exfiltrating data. The FBI issued a flash advisory in October warning that attackers including state-sponsored groups from China are using these tools widely. The fundamental shift is not just speed but the elimination of skill as a barrier: anyone can now "push the attack button" and let the multi-agent system handle the work while they sleep.

**The Outage Crisis: Caught Between Competing Pressures**

The near-simultaneous outages last week—FAA groundstop, Microsoft Outlook, Chase, Apple iCloud—were not cyberattacks but rather what Perloth calls "self-owns." Companies were attempting to do the right thing by rolling out software patches to fix vulnerabilities discovered by AI. The problem is that patching is fundamentally harder and slower than finding vulnerabilities. Finding is now automated and near-instantaneous; patching requires careful coordination across complex, interdependent systems and carries real risk of breaking service. Yet companies are under enormous pressure to patch faster than ever because attackers can now exploit new vulnerabilities in hours. Incident response firms report their workload is up 50% from previous peak months, and some are turning away victims—even those facing nation-state attacks—because they don't have capacity. This creates an impossible triangle: attackers move at machine speed, defenders must patch faster than ever to keep up, but faster patching inevitably breaks things.

**Foundational Software as a Single Point of Failure**

PHP powers roughly 70% of the world's websites. Java is nearly ubiquitous in enterprise systems. When AI tools discovered critical vulnerabilities in these core pieces of software, it exposed millions of systems simultaneously. Perloth notes that the "digital deficit" has come due: decades of "move fast and break things" philosophy resulted in faulty, vulnerable software embedded everywhere—in critical infrastructure, healthcare, finance, education. For years, the assumption was that security would come later via patches and software updates. Now attackers can find and exploit these latent vulnerabilities at machine speed, forcing a reckoning. The asymmetry is stark: vulnerability discovery is now nearly free and instant; patching is expensive, slow, and risky, especially in older systems where changes can cascade into failures.

**Government and Critical Infrastructure: The Highest-Risk Domain**

Government systems present the worst-case scenario. Many still run software that is no longer supported, meaning vulnerabilities cannot even receive patches if discovered. The FAA, for instance, still operates decades-old routers. Procurement rules are Byzantine; modernization efforts routinely fail to completion within budget or timeline. Water treatment facilities are particularly vulnerable: they typically lack in-house IT staff and rely on remote contractor access to monitor chemical controls and chemical levels, creating an opening for attackers. No one designed these systems expecting Iranian hackers to probe them. Perloth emphasizes that so much of the problem comes down not to technical barriers but to political will, budgets, and procurement complexity. Even when funding exists, modernizing a critical system means risking an outage—which in a bureaucratic logic is worse for the person who broke it than for the person who fails to defend it. That inverted incentive structure paralyzes action.

**The Haves and Have-Nots: Resource Concentration in Cyber Defense**

Banks and large financial services understand that a successful cyberattack is an existential threat; if J.P. Morgan's customer accounts were wiped out, the institution would lose all trust. These companies have blank-check cyber budgets and hire people from intelligence agencies to hunt threats all day long. Everyone else—companies outside the Fortune 500, government agencies, water utilities, smaller banks—lacks comparable resources. There is no ROI on cybersecurity; a dollar spent on cyber defense is a dollar not spent on a new AI product. This creates a stark have/have-not divide. Perloth notes this is "the biggest blinking red light" and a "national imperative," but no mechanism exists to force smaller firms to invest or to incentivize larger firms to share talent.

**AI-Generated Deepfakes and Social Engineering: The Softest Attack Surface**

Perloth describes the hardest problem in cybersecurity: not code vulnerabilities but human psychology. AI can now generate voice clones so convincing that people are receiving calls claiming a loved one has been kidnapped and wiring money. Within Fortune 500 companies, CFOs have been tricked into authorizing wire payments or revealing credentials by voices claiming to be the CEO—recorded via deepfake. This is happening now, and it is happening repeatedly. The best defense is a code word used between family members or colleagues so that in a moment of panic, you can verify identity. Phishing emails are also improving; AI can personalize them, adjust language, and mimic trusted sources in ways that early clumsy attacks never could. The technical parts of cybersecurity—multi-factor authentication, unique passwords, endpoint detection—are solvable. The human part—believing a voice that sounds exactly like someone you know, clicking a link that looks totally legitimate—is where systems break.

**The Paradox of Low-Trust Digital Life Amid Continuing Reliance**

For the first 30 years of the internet, people gradually learned to trust it. You gave your credit card to Amazon because Amazon seemed stable. You assumed an email from your bank was actually from your bank. You believed a video was real. That era of high-trust internet is ending. Ezra Klein notes a strange paradox: people are simultaneously deeply skeptical of institutions and deeply trusting in practice. They know the internet is unsafe but don't have the time or energy to operate with full paranoia—not because they are stupid, but because the cognitive load of treating every email, video, and voice call as potentially fake is prohibitive. Perloth describes this as "disorienting"; human authenticity will become "the most valuable commodity." She observes that deepfake detection was her first venture investment because the proliferation of AI-generated disinformation is so concerning. Klein worries that most people will operate in a state of knowing something is wrong while continuing to trust anyway, unable to change behavior despite awareness of risk.

**Individual and Organizational Defense: Practical Steps**

Perloth offers concrete steps for individuals: print bank statements occasionally; store copies of passport and driver's license; use unique passwords (via a password manager, though Perloth herself avoids password managers for the most critical accounts and uses her own system); enable multi-factor authentication everywhere possible, preferably with a passkey fingerprint rather than SMS; never click links from unknown senders; verify email addresses. For organizations, the key is identifying the most precious asset and hardening it obsessively: fallback systems, backups, regular exercises simulating worst-case scenarios. When Perloth covered major attacks at the New York Times, her strategy for protecting sources was to avoid digital communication entirely, meeting physically in the same location on the same day quarterly, never via smart car or Uber, only pen and paper. AI also offers some defensive hope: new solutions can scan an organization's entire digital infrastructure to identify gaps (missing MFA, undeprovisioned employees) and can prioritize patches against real-world threat intelligence. Colonial Pipeline was breached because a former employee wasn't kicked off the network and MFA wasn't properly installed—gaps AI tools excel at finding.

**The Possibility of Transition Toward Greater Security**

Klein asks whether this crisis is fundamentally a period of adjustment—painful, but eventually leading to more secure systems—or a new, unstable normal. Perloth is cautiously optimistic but realistic about the obstacles. The transition will be "very painful." It assumes companies and governments are willing to rip and replace every archaic system, that people exist who know how to do so without cascading failures, and that constant AI-enabled attacks won't cripple the process. She notes that "we consistently underestimate the softer challenges"—political will, budgets, leadership, finding the right people. But she also believes that eventually we will use AI to write more secure code, eliminate buffer overflows, and emerge into a better place. She advocates for regulation mandating security testing before deployment, creative incentive programs like allowing companies to lend cybersecurity staff to under-resourced critical infrastructure in exchange for tax credits or student debt payoff, and leadership that understands these solutions are fundamentally about aligning incentives. The alternative—allowing faulty code to continue being embedded in critical systems—is not viable. Klein worries that the generation of instant gratification is not built for an era of outages and flickering systems, but Perloth argues there is no alternative: code with vulnerabilities must stop being rolled out, particularly to critical infrastructure.

**Three Book Recommendations**

Perloth recommends: *Red Notice* by Bill Browder (a hedge fund manager's journey to becoming an accidental activist after his lawyer was tortured to death in Russia; a reminder of who the actual adversaries are), *Hatchet* by Gary Paulson (a novel about resilience, which Perloth reads with her eight-year-old; she views resilience as the core skill needed now), and *Flash Boys* by Michael Lewis (a book about how technology has outpaced human ability to understand it, yet heroes have emerged to fix broken systems; a call for more people willing to ask whether deploying AI into every corner of life is wise).

# Throughline — "Yuri Kochiyama's lifetime of activism"

**Host:** Randa Abdel-Fattah (host, America in Pursuit / Throughline, NPR). **Guests:** Yuri Kochiyama (archival recordings; Japanese American civil rights activist, 1921–2014); unnamed academic expert (provides historical context throughout).

## TL;DR

- **Yuri Kochiyama held Malcolm X's head in her lap as he died** — on February 21, 1965, at the Audubon Ballroom in Harlem, she ran to him after the shooting and cradled him; a photographer captured the image that became her most enduring historical association.
- **Her father died days after FBI detention following Pearl Harbor** — he was taken in his bathrobe and slippers on December 7, 1941, held for weeks despite having just had ulcer surgery, released, and died the next day.
- **120,000 Japanese Americans were detained during WWII; Kochiyama spent two years in a camp** — she later rejected the term "relocation centers" in favor of "concentration camps," noting barbed wire, sentries, and restricted freedoms distinguished them from voluntary moves.
- **Her radicalization began at Chock Full O'Nuts in New York** — working alongside Black colleagues from the South, she learned for the first time that Black soldiers in uniform could not enter establishments on the main drag in Mississippi, saying "I was shocked."
- **She was among 600+ arrested during the 1963 CORE job-site blockade** — the Congress on Racial Equality organized to demand jobs for Black and Puerto Rican construction workers at a Harlem medical site; Kochiyama physically blocked construction trucks.
- **Malcolm X invited her to study at his OAAU Liberation School after she wrote him a letter** — her letter argued that the blame for integration's failure rested with non-Black Americans, not with Black people seeking autonomous spaces; Malcolm responded by welcoming her.
- **She organized continuously until her death at 93 in 2014** — her work spanned Japanese American reparations, support for the Young Lords, lobbying for political prisoners, and post-9/11 opposition to racial profiling of Muslim Americans.

**The Image at the Audubon Ballroom**

The episode opens with Kochiyama's own voice describing February 21, 1965. She was in the audience at the Audubon Ballroom when a distraction in the crowd preceded gunfire and Malcolm X fell. She ran to him and placed his head in her lap. A photographer captured the moment: Kochiyama in all black, kneeling, hunched over Malcolm, his eyes closed, his white shirt stained with blood. That image became the frame through which most people first encounter her name — though the episode's purpose is to explain how she came to be there at all.

**Pearl Harbor, the FBI, and the Death of Her Father**

Hours after Japan bombed Pearl Harbor on December 7, 1941, FBI agents arrived at the Nakahara family home. Her father, recently returned from ulcer surgery and still recovering, was taken from his bed in his bathrobe and slippers without explanation. He was among 2,000 to 3,000 Japanese American immigrant men detained that day. Five weeks passed before Kochiyama was allowed to visit him. He was released a week later — and died the following day, likely from being moved before he had healed. Kochiyama was 20 years old.

**Internment and the Awakening of Social Consciousness**

Kochiyama, her family, and roughly 120,000 Japanese Americans were forced into incarceration camps for the duration of the war; she spent two years inside. In the episode she reflects on the term "relocation centers" used at the time, which she came to reject in favor of "concentration camps" — distinguishing them from Nazi death camps but noting the barbed wire, sentries, and constrained freedoms that made the label accurate. The academic expert observes that Kochiyama began to develop a sense that "problems in the United States had social and structural origins" through conversations with more politicized Japanese Americans in the camps. In her own words: "You're taught what a good country America is, and it does so much good to the world and all that crap. I believed it wholeheartedly. And suddenly, for the first time, I felt something different."

**New York, Chock Full O'Nuts, and the Education in Racism**

After the war, Kochiyama married Bill Kochiyama — a World War II veteran she had met at one of the internment camps — and moved to New York, where there was no established Japanese American activist community to plug into. Her first job was as a waitress at Chock Full O'Nuts, a famous New York City restaurant, where she worked alongside Black colleagues, including two men from the South. She described mentioning the USO where she had worked in Mississippi, where no Black soldiers ever came in: they told her that on the main drag, Black soldiers in uniform were barred from entering any establishment. "I was shocked," she said. That conversation started what became years of self-education about segregation, racism, and the history of Black America.

**Harlem, CORE, and the First Arrest**

The Kochiyama family moved to Harlem in 1960 with six children, carrying their possessions across the city on the subway. The academic expert describes Harlem as exactly the environment Kochiyama had been building toward: the activism around her was rooted in the Black freedom movement, and she moved into it directly. She organized around better schools for Harlem children and labor rights at a hospital construction site. In the summer of 1963, the Congress on Racial Equality (CORE) organized a campaign demanding that Black and Puerto Rican workers be hired on a new medical building whose contractor was engaging in discriminatory hiring. Protesters physically blocked construction trucks from entering. Kochiyama was among the more than 600 people arrested.

**Malcolm X, the Letter, and the OAAU**

At the courthouse hearings that followed the summer 1963 arrests, Malcolm X walked in. Kochiyama wanted to shake his hand but hesitated, wondering whether it was appropriate for a non-Black person. She went ahead anyway and told him she admired his work but didn't agree with everything — specifically, "your harsh stand on integration." He said he didn't have time to discuss it but offered to schedule a meeting through his secretary. She followed up with a letter arguing that the barriers to integration lay with non-Black Americans who exhibited racism, not with Black people who sought autonomous spaces. Malcolm responded by inviting her to attend the newly formed Organization of Afro-American Unity's Liberation School. She joined, studied, and later said: "I feel that Malcolm did more than anyone else to let me see what's really happening in the world and why."

**Malcolm's Internationalism and Kochiyama's Expanding Framework**

Through the OAAU, Kochiyama came to understand Black liberation as part of a global struggle against colonialism and empire. The academic expert highlights a 1964 gathering at the Kochiyama apartment where Malcolm hosted Hiroshima atomic bomb survivors. He told them: "You have been bombed and you bear the scars of the bombs. And we too, as black Americans in the United States, also bear the scars of bombs." That practice of connecting struggles across nationalities and histories became central to Kochiyama's own organizing philosophy for the rest of her life.

**Decades of Organizing, to the End**

Kochiyama continued organizing after Malcolm X's assassination in 1965 and did not stop until shortly before her death at 93 in 2014. Her work included Asian Americans for Action, the campaign for reparations for Japanese American incarceration, solidarity with the Young Lords fighting for Puerto Rican self-determination, lobbying for the release of political prisoners, and speaking out against the racial profiling of Muslim Americans following September 11. The ethos she returned to throughout her life: "My people's liberation is intricately linked to your people's liberation. I cannot be free if you're not free."

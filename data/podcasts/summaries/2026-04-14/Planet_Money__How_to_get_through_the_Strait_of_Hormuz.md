**The Blockade and Its Global Economic Impact**

On February 28th, the U.S. declared war on Iran. Almost immediately, Iran leveraged its geographic control of the Strait of Hormuz—the narrow waterway connecting the Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman and beyond—by attacking ships attempting transit. Iran went further, insinuating that the Omani side of the Strait contained mines, forcing all captains to navigate close to Iran's coastline, creating an effective choke point. This blockade cut off approximately 20% of the world's oil supply, along with critical commodities like helium and fertilizer.

For most people, the war's impact felt distant—visible only at the gas pump. For Christian St. Clair, a production manager at Fantagraphics Books, the impact was painfully concrete: two books he'd been working on, printed in India, were scheduled to be shipped to the U.S. on the One Majesty, a distinctive bright pink container ship. The books were supposed to arrive in early April for a launch party.

**Iran's Toll System: $1 Per Barrel in Cryptocurrency**

Despite announcing a blockade, Iran was not preventing all passage. Rather, President Trump announced a ceasefire on Tuesday with Iran agreeing to "completely open up the strait," but the next day, ships received contradictory messages: "Transiting in the Strait of Hormuz is closed. If any vessels try to transit without permission, they will be destroyed." The word "permission" suggested a mechanism through which some ships could pass.

Nick Fountain spoke with Hamid Hosseini, a well-connected spokesperson for Iran's oil, gas, and petroleum products exporters union, who explained how the system works. According to Hamid, a ship owner must contact the Iranian Revolutionary Guard and provide details: ship name, flag country, destination, cargo description, and crew information. The Iranian authorities check that the captain and crew are not from the U.S., Israel, or other adversaries, and that the ship and cargo have no affiliation with those countries.

If approved, the ship must pay a toll: one dollar per barrel of oil. For a large crude carrier (VLCC) carrying 2 million barrels, this means a $2 million toll. Iran does not accept U.S. dollars for payment. Instead, Hamid said his Indian oil trader contact was instructed to pay in cryptocurrency, with a five-second deadline to complete the transaction. This arrangement serves Iran's interests: crypto avoids U.S. sanctions and enables rapid fund movement. Hamid's contact successfully paid and the ship reached India.

**The Law of General Average**

When the One Majesty was attacked in the Strait—sustaining a small hole but remaining afloat—it was damaged, not destroyed. Ryan Peterson of Flexport explained that ship damage typically invokes the Law of General Average, a maritime principle stating that if a disaster occurs at sea, all cargo owners share in the cost of repairs and losses equally, regardless of whether their specific shipment was damaged. The idea is to prevent captains from sacrificing cargo selectively to save the ship. This meant Christian and Fantagraphics' insurance company could be liable for a portion of the One Majesty's repair costs.

**Freedom of Navigation and the Global Order**

Peterson emphasized a larger concern. For over 70 years, the U.S. Navy has guaranteed the free flow of goods through the world's waterways, a service so taken for granted that most people assume it is natural and permanent. However, Iran's blockade and toll system represent a fundamental challenge to that order. If Iran can dictate the terms of passage, it signals that "the U.S. Navy is no longer the big boss of the seas," with profound geopolitical implications. The modern global economy depends entirely on freedom of navigation; a collapse of that principle would transform international commerce and reshape the balance of power. As Peterson put it, the toll amounts matter less than the symbolic question: "The U.S. Navy can no longer guarantee freedom of navigation anymore."

**Christian's Books and the Unexpected Resolution**

When Fountain shared Christian's situation with Hamid, Hamid offered to help broker a deal, suggesting that if the ship's destination were not the U.S., passage might be possible. However, the books were headed to New York City, making Iran cooperation unlikely unless a formal U.S.-Iran agreement restored open transit.

But before Fountain could relay this disappointing news, Christian called back with unexpected good news: the books had never been on the One Majesty at all. They had been printed and shipped to India as planned, but were stored there awaiting the next leg of the journey. Only just before the One Majesty was attacked, the books were loaded onto a different container ship—also bright pink, apparently the shipping company's standard color—which set sail without incident. If the current schedule holds, the books are expected to arrive in the U.S. in mid-July, only about a month late. Christian joked that his books "have exhausted" all the things that could go wrong in global shipping, expressing cautious confidence in their imminent delivery.

**The Negotiations and Four Possible Futures**

As of the episode's recording, negotiations between the U.S. and Iran had broken down, with the Strait's future as a central sticking point. Fountain identified four possible outcomes: an open waterway with no tolls (the pre-war status quo), a toll booth controlled by Iran, a toll booth controlled by the U.S., or a revenue-sharing arrangement between the two powers. Each outcome would reshape global commerce, the cost of goods, and the fundamental order of international relations. The episode ends with this uncertainty unresolved—the fate of the Strait, and by extension the modern global economy, hanging in the balance.

# Code Switch — "From the Confederacy to the White House: How Southern beauty traditions went MAGA"

**Hosts:** B.A. Parker and Gene Demby. **Guest:** Elizabeth Bronwyn Boyd (author of *Southern Beauty: Race, Ritual, and Memory in the Modern South*; scholar of Southern femininity and racial performance).

## TL;DR

- **"Mar-a-Lago face" is a loyalty signal, not just aesthetics** — dramatic makeup, long flowing hair, hyper-feminine dress, and conformist male grooming (Marco Rubio wore shoes gifted by Trump that were too large) are ways of branding oneself as loyal to the Trump administration, "almost like a line in your resume."
- **Boyd traces MAGA gender performance directly to Confederate pageantry** — antebellum Southern elites held jousting tournaments, May Day festivals, and 12th night parties in which crowning a white woman as queen was the culminating moment; she was both "the motif of the White South and its rationale."
- **The United Daughters of the Confederacy weaponized femininity for the Lost Cause** — after the Civil War, Confederate veterans reunions and monument unveilings always elevated the white lady figure because, in the ideology of the time, preserving that ideal was why the South had gone to war.
- **Sorority rush exploded in response to desegregation** — after Brown v. Board of Education in 1954, sorority pageantry proliferated on Southern campuses with explicitly racial content, including blackface productions, as a way to assert white Southern identity against the prospect of integration.
- **Bama Rush is a "feminine stratification ritual"** — women sort each other into tiers of social power, but Boyd argues it is "proximity to power rather than true power of its own sake" because the top houses compete to attract the most influential fraternity men, not to gain political power themselves.
- **Trump's Miss USA background shaped the current aesthetic** — Boyd contrasts Miss America (a civic ideal of national womanhood) with Miss USA (overtly sexy, "just there for the flash and the cash") and argues Trump's pageant frame of reference is the TV screen, not any civic standard.
- **MAGA beauty now includes surgical performance for men** — jawline sculpting, forehead work, veneers, jaw implants, Botox, and fillers for men; a flat-makeup "evangelical Christian babe" look for women; Boyd summarizes it as: "It's all about surfaces."
- **MAGA nostalgia is anxiety about the present** — Boyd argues "Make America Great Again" rests on nostalgia for something that never existed, and that "nostalgia is not about living in the past — it's about anxiety in the present that improves the view of the past."

**The Mar-a-Lago Aesthetic as Loyalty Performance**

The episode opens with Demby and Parker describing the "Mar-a-Lago face" aesthetic they had discussed in a prior episode: dramatic eye makeup, long flowing hair, body-conscious clothing, and a commitment to legible, traditional gender presentation. They extend the analysis to men — pointing to Marco Rubio wearing oversized shoes gifted by Trump (Trump reportedly believes large feet signal manliness) and Pete Hegseth's performative masculinity, including a public conflict with the Boy Scouts over being "too soft." Boyd, who was introduced in the previous episode, describes the behavior as "all an intentional play to fit in with Trump aesthetic ideals," functioning as a way to show a prospective employer — the most powerful man in the country — willingness to conform. The performance, she argues, insists on biological sex and gender lining up in a legible way "that can be discerned from a distance, say a bigly rally." She likens it to drag: "It demonstrates the performativity of gender taken to an extreme."

**Confederate Pageantry and the White Queen**

Boyd explains that she came to study Southern beauty after returning to her hometown in Mississippi and being struck by a "wall of femininity" — not natural beauty so much as grooming, etiquette, and precision social performance. Her research focused on three popular performances among young white Southern women: sorority rush, beauty pageants, and the Confederate Pageant of the Natchez Pilgrimage, an annual tour of antebellum homes in Mississippi.

She argues that the antebellum South distinguished itself from the North through slavery, and that to justify and mythologize that arrangement, Southern elites constructed an identity as heirs of European aristocracy, reinforced through elaborate annual pageants. At jousting tournaments, May Day festivals, 12th night parties, and tableau, the culminating moment was always the crowning of a white Southern woman as queen. The queen functioned as both emblem and rationale — "the motif of the White South and its rationale."

After the Civil War, the United Daughters of the Confederacy embedded the same structure into the Lost Cause movement. At Confederate veterans reunions, memorial events, and monument unveilings, a white lady figure was elevated for praise and recognition, framed as the reason the South had gone to war. When the last Confederate veterans died off — roughly 1890 through 1920 — the ideal woman shifted from the pedestal into civic pageantry and public festivals.

**Desegregation, Blackface, and Sorority Rush**

Boyd argues that in the mid-20th century South, the prospect of desegregation triggered a sharp proliferation of pageantry on college campuses. Following Brown v. Board of Education in 1954, Southern sororities staged productions that explicitly asserted white Southern identity — including blackface performances within sorority rush itself. Boyd describes these as rituals about "showing who we are. We're part of this white Southern identity, and we are definitely not this other thing."

She characterizes sorority rush broadly as "a feminine stratification ritual," where women police and sort each other into tiers of social power. The top houses carry the most social capital, but Boyd draws a distinction: that capital is social, not political. Campus political power remains with fraternity men. The women choosing recruits are selecting for how well the new members will improve their brand and attract influential men — "proximity to power rather than true power of its own sake."

Parker shares her own experience as a participant in a Black cotillion at 16 — wearing pearls, a white dress, learning to waltz, studying Emily Post — and acknowledges the cultural rewards and family pressure involved. Boyd connects this to class performance that transcends race, noting that Black cotillions emerged out of exclusion, the same way sororities were founded when women were excluded from men's literary societies.

**Trump, Miss USA, and the Pageant Frame**

Boyd argues that Trump's ownership of Miss USA is significant in understanding the current aesthetic. Where Miss America was constructed as a civic sweetheart representing national ideals of womanhood, Miss USA "never stood in for anything" — it was "just there for the flash and the cash," overtly sexy, heavy on makeup, and exaggerated in presentation. Boyd notes that Trump's entire frame of reference is the TV screen, which explains the visual logic of the administration's look.

She describes specific MAGA aesthetic practices: for women, a flat-makeup "evangelical Christian babe" look; for men, jawline sculpting, forehead work, veneers, jaw implants, Botox, and fillers. Boyd summarizes the communicative function plainly: "It's all about surfaces. If you could look good doing it, then that's what we want." She uses a Texas expression to capture the hollowness: "all hat and no cattle."

**Nostalgia as Present Anxiety**

Boyd closes by connecting MAGA aesthetics to the ideology of nostalgia underpinning the movement itself. She argues that "Make America Great Again" rests on longing for something that never existed — a time when America was great. She runs through the historical record: the removal of Native Americans, slavery, post-WWII policies that pushed women out of the workforce so men could take their jobs. Her reframe of nostalgia is direct: it is not really about the past. It is "anxiety in the present that improves the view of the past."

**The Seven Sisters Cartel and Oil-Producing Nations' Grievances**

Starting in the 1950s, the world's oil market was controlled not by the countries with the most oil—Saudi Arabia, Venezuela, and others—but by American and European oil companies operating as a cartel known as the Seven Sisters. Companies like Gulf, Shell, and Texaco held exclusive drilling rights and set the prices they would pay to oil-producing nations for their crude, coordinating their actions to maximize profit while minimizing payments to host countries. This arrangement grew increasingly frustrating to oil ministers in OPEC nations, especially after oil companies began cutting the price they offered in the mid-1950s.

Wanda Jablonski, a journalist and publisher of the influential petroleum newsletter *Petroleum Intelligence Weekly*, played a crucial role in revealing how the Seven Sisters operated. Her reporting exposed the accounting tricks and economic mechanics by which the companies extracted wealth from oil states. Jablonski was well-connected across the international oil world and used her access to educate oil ministers about the true economics of their own resources. In 1959, just before the Arab Petroleum Congress in Cairo, she introduced the oil minister of Venezuela and the oil minister of Saudi Arabia—two figures most motivated to challenge the Seven Sisters' control. These two men arranged a secret meeting at an empty yacht club outside town, inviting officials from Kuwait, Iraq, Egypt, and Iran. They met under a tree on the banks of the Nile to avoid being spied on by Seven Sisters representatives. At this meeting, they agreed to form an organization—eventually called OPEC, the Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries—to band together and negotiate for better terms and a larger share of oil revenues.

**OPEC's Early Years as a Grievance Organization**

Although OPEC was founded in 1959, it remained largely ineffectual for more than a decade. The organization functioned primarily as a consultative body where members aired grievances and attempted to negotiate collectively with major oil companies. The oil companies, however, were unwilling to meet OPEC's demands or cede meaningful control. Ibrahim Almohana, who advised Saudi Arabia's oil ministers for nearly four decades, describes OPEC in those early years as an "industry group" and grievance organization rather than a body with genuine market power. The Seven Sisters still controlled prices, and OPEC members remained essentially powerless to change the dynamic.

**The 1973 Embargo and OPEC's Discovery of Real Power**

The turning point came in October 1973 when Egypt and Syria invaded Israel. The United States sent military supplies to support Israel, angering Arab oil-producing nations. In response, some Arab oil states (not all OPEC members, but a smaller coalition) took two actions: they embargoed oil exports to the United States and Israel's other supporters, and more importantly, they cut total oil production by 5% each month for as long as the war continued. Since the U.S. could still buy oil from non-embargoed sources globally, the embargo alone might have been ineffective, but the production cut reduced the total global supply. The scarcity sent oil prices skyrocketing from $3 a barrel to $12 almost overnight—the largest economic shock the world had seen. Gas lines formed in the U.S. as consumers scrambled for fuel.

The embargo lasted only a few months, but its impact taught OPEC members a lasting lesson: their real power lay not in expressing grievances or negotiating politely, but in collectively controlling the global supply. OPEC countries were producing more than half the world's oil, giving them immense leverage if they acted as a bloc to limit how much they pumped.

**Production Quotas and the Challenge of Collective Action**

In 1982, OPEC formalized this insight by introducing production quotas—limits on how much oil each member could pump. All countries received quotas except Saudi Arabia, which volunteered to become the "swing producer," adjusting its output up or down to smooth market cycles and keep oil prices stable for the group. In theory, this was an enviable position: when prices rose too high, Saudi Arabia could pump more and profit; when prices fell, it could cut production to support the price. But in practice, being the swing producer proved painful, especially during downturns when Saudi Arabia was forced to pump significantly less and earn far less revenue. The bigger structural problem, however, was that cheating on quotas proved irresistible. Once quotas were imposed, member countries were constantly tempted to pump slightly more than allowed and sell oil at the high OPEC-managed price. With limited enforcement mechanisms, many countries did exactly that, becoming "free riders" on the group's collective restraint. Saudi Arabia found this cheating deeply frustrating and eventually retaliated.

**Saudi Arabia's 1985 Flooding and the Reassertion of OPEC Power**

In 1985, angered by persistent cheating, Saudi Arabia decided to demonstrate its power by flooding the market with oil. The strategy crashed oil prices everywhere—by spring 1986, some gas stations were selling gasoline for as low as 19 cents a gallon. For a brief moment, OPEC appeared to be in collapse. However, the organization recovered, rebuilt its quota system with improved structures, and actually expanded its membership and influence over the global oil market. OPEC remained the dominant player in global energy markets, though its relative power has waned somewhat in recent decades as the U.S. ramped up domestic oil production and other factors reduced OPEC's leverage.

**The Green Paradox and the UAE's Departure**

In recent years, OPEC's internal cohesion has eroded further due to a phenomenon called the "Green Paradox." As the world decarbonizes and demand for oil faces a long-term decline, each producing nation has an incentive to pump as much as possible *now* to capture profits before demand collapses. The UAE exemplifies this tension: the country invested heavily in new oil infrastructure to dramatically increase production capacity, but OPEC refused to raise the UAE's quota proportionally. The organization was using outdated baseline production data from 2018, back when UAE capacity was lower. The Emirates, feeling underestimated and locked into constraints that no longer matched their capabilities, grew increasingly frustrated. Tensions came to a head in 2021 when Saudi Arabia proposed modest quota increases, but the UAE rejected them as insufficient. The disagreement became public in an unusually direct way—the oil ministers conducted dueling TV appearances, something rarely seen in OPEC's normally private negotiations. The standoff lasted weeks and grew nasty; Saudi Arabia even banned flights from the UAE. Eventually, both sides saved face and the UAE received higher quotas, but the episode made clear that the relationship had deteriorated fundamentally.

The final catalyst for the UAE's departure came when Iran, responding to U.S. and Israeli military strikes, began blockading the Strait of Hormuz, preventing most Emirati oil from leaving the country. With their exports physically blocked and their quota negotiations stalled, the UAE's leadership concluded that staying in OPEC offered no benefit. In early 2026, the UAE announced its withdrawal.

**Gas Prices and the UAE Departure: What It Means**

Kate Durian, a veteran OPEC analyst, warns that the UAE's departure is unlikely to bring meaningful relief at the gas pump in the near term. Iran's blockade of the Strait of Hormuz is the binding constraint—even if the UAE leaves OPEC and pumps at full capacity, most of that oil cannot reach global markets while the strait is blocked. Once the blockade is lifted, there will be a long lag before prices fall meaningfully. Countries must rebuild their strategic petroleum reserves, refineries must restock, and tankers must be repositioned. The world has already lost roughly 700 million barrels due to the blockade, and losses are approaching a billion; filling that gap will take a considerable time. Furthermore, the UAE's additional production—even at full capacity outside OPEC constraints—would add only about 1.5% to global oil supply. The future trajectory of prices depends largely on how OPEC responds to the UAE's departure: whether the group chooses a punishing price war with the UAE or opts to keep prices high and maintain group discipline.

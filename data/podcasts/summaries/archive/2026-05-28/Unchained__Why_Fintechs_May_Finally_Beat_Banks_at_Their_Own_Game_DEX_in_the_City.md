# Unchained — "Why Fintechs May Finally Beat Banks at Their Own Game: DEX in the City"

**Host:** Katherine KK (Starkware; fluent in tradfi and deep tech). **Guests:** V (former SEC official, now web3 operator); Jesse (web3 prosecutor turned protector at Rivet Capital; formerly general counsel at federally regulated crypto bank).

**Executive Order on Fed Master Accounts for Fintechs and Crypto Firms**

The White House issued an executive order directing the Federal Reserve to consider granting direct Fed master accounts to fintech companies, crypto banks, stablecoin issuers, tokenized securities platforms, and digital asset custodians. A Fed master account provides access to what V describes as "the core plumbing of the US dollar system," allowing institutions to settle directly in central bank money rather than through intermediary correspondent banks.

For the crypto and fintech industry, this represents a seismic shift. Companies have historically faced debanking risk—correspondent banks cutting them off—and have lacked a direct, credible bridge to the traditional financial system. Jesse notes this is essential infrastructure for scaling because "enabling seamless connectivity between the traditional fiat system and traditional payment systems and crypto is really the only way that crypto and DeFi can scale."

The context matters: Kraken received what people have called a "master account light"—a limited-purpose Fed account with defined guardrails—earlier in 2026, proving the concept is feasible and signaling regulatory openness. The new executive order pushes further by asking the Fed to formally consider broader access for the entire fintech and crypto ecosystem. However, V raises a critical concern: the EO emphasizes access but offers little on how to protect retail customers once access is granted. The guardrails that work for traditional banks may not translate to crypto entities. "We're plugging them into critical infrastructure," V says. "You need to be mature and responsible enough to take that on."

KK observes that traditional banks should be terrified. Fintechs are poised to strip away two of banking's fundamental advantages: custody of customer funds and payment facilitation. Under the GENIUS Act framework, fintechs and crypto firms can now perform functions "identical from the retail customer perspective" to what banks do, while banks retain only FDIC insurance and the ability to borrow customer deposits for their own use. The policy implication is stark: what differentiates a traditional bank from a fintech anymore?

**The Unbundling of Banking**

Jesse argues this is part of a larger trend of "unbundling banking from the rest of the financial system." Historically, if you wanted access to Fed rails, you had to fit into the traditional bank box. But fintech and crypto have spent years demonstrating that banking services—liquidity, custody, payments—can be offered separately and more efficiently outside that box. The policy is catching up.

The danger is that the same dynamics that destroyed fintech banking-as-a-service (Synapse, other collapses) could resurface. Those platforms regulated at state level or not at all, lacked the proper structure, and failed catastrophically. Jesse sees the difference now: "There's something to learn here from how FinTech got customers on board, but there's also something to learn with perhaps they did so too quickly without the proper structure in place."

KK agrees this is not a partisan issue. Traditional views that "Dems hate innovation and love banks" are shortsighted and historically inaccurate. Democrats have historically pushed back against big banks and supported community banks—and fintech infrastructure has helped community banks access new customers and geographies. The executive order, though issued by the Trump administration, will likely survive a Democratic successor because the underlying trend is sound policy regardless of party.

**Prediction Markets: Litigation, Access, and Controversy**

The prediction market litigation war escalates daily. In recent weeks: Rhode Island's Attorney General sued Polymarket, New York launched a congressional probe, the New York State Legislature advanced a bill to prohibit under-21 participation, and the NHL and CFTC signed a memorandum of understanding to prevent manipulation of sports betting. The volume of lawsuits is staggering, with new actions nearly every day.

Polymarket announced a major partnership with NASDAQ Private Market, granting retail participants access to prediction contracts on private company valuations, IPO timing, and funding rounds. Historically, private market alpha has been accessible only to the wealthy and connected. This move democratizes access, which KK finds compelling in theory: "The private market has always been this massive market with significant alpha that has long been pretty much inaccessible to retail."

But the controversy surrounding prediction markets is fierce, recently epitomized by a scathing New York Times article. KK has mixed feelings. While she is a longtime Times subscriber (for real estate and wedding announcements), the crypto coverage has been overwhelmingly negative and frequently one-sided. This particular article, while making serious allegations about improper CFTC behavior, is marred by unnecessary snark—calling CFTC acting chair Fam a "former intern" and noting Commissioner Celix's age in ways that undermine journalistic credibility.

V and Jesse defend the substance of the allegations while critiquing the tone. V, who knows the reporter and trusts his general integrity, reframes the story as a potential government corruption piece rather than crypto-specific: "It should be seen as a potential government corruption article," she says. The article alleges that staff were fired for raising ethical concerns about investigations and suggests improper conduct at the leadership level. These are serious ethical allegations that deserve investigation, regardless of the snark.

Jesse makes a crucial point: "Careers were potentially impacted here. And so, I want us all to take it seriously." But she also notes the revolving door between government and industry is normal, and if the implication is improper, it must be applied consistently to all parties, including the alleged whistleblower victims who now work for market participants.

The group agrees the Inspector General at the CFTC—an independent watchdog office within the agency—should be investigating these allegations. If the allegations are true, they represent a failure of government ethics. But the snark and one-sidedness of the Times article raise legitimate questions about motivation and credibility. The larger point, KK suggests, is understanding why so much mainstream media coverage of crypto is negative. If the goal is industry maturation and broader adoption, taking criticism seriously (even snarky criticism) may be more productive than dismissing it outright.

**Prediction Markets Are Not Crypto**

A recurring pushback: prediction markets and crypto are often conflated but are distinct. Many prediction markets have nothing to do with blockchain and use no crypto rails. Lumping them together under "crypto bad" conflates two separate phenomena. However, when Polymarket integrates with NASDAQ, it becomes harder to separate the conversation. The group agrees that levels-the-playing-field argument—giving retail access to alpha previously reserved for institutions—is one of the most compelling reasons for prediction markets to exist, regardless of whether they use blockchain.

**SEC Innovation Exemption Delayed Indefinitely**

The SEC was expected to release a long-awaited innovation exemption this week that would clarify the regulatory treatment of tokenized securities trading on public blockchains. But after Bloomberg and Reuters reported the exemption could allow "synthetic rappers" (wrapper tokens providing economic exposure to stocks like Apple or Amazon without issuer sponsorship), the plan collapsed. Commissioner Pur clarified that the exemption was always meant to cover only issuer-sponsored tokens—digital representations of the same underlying stocks, fully backed by regulated custodians—not synthetic products.

The distinction matters. Synthetic wrappers could create investor confusion (what rights do they have?), market fragmentation, and voting/dividend complications. Issuer-sponsored tokens, by contrast, would trade on-chain but represent real shares held in escrow. Companies like OpenAI have publicly distanced themselves from unauthorized wrapper tokens. The SEC chose to delay rather than release guidance that could enable messy outcomes. KK sees this as appropriate: "Halfbaked guidance is not going to be futureproofable. I think that's why we're also eager for legislation."

**Commissioner Pur's Departure**

Commissioner Pur announced she is leaving the SEC to become a law professor at Regent University School of Law in Virginia Beach. The speakers expressed profound loss. KK said, "It is such a loss for the SEC. Uh not just for crypto, for the SEC, frankly, because she has always been intellectually honest enough not to make this a crypto thing." Jesse and KK agreed that Pur's thoughtfulness, willingness to engage nuance, and refusal to demonize either side have been invaluable. Pur's students at Regent, KK joked, have no idea how lucky they are to learn from someone so deeply versed in fintech and crypto regulation.

**AI Policy and the Four-Faction White House**

Jesse delivered a breakdown of the internal White House power struggle over AI policy. Four factions are locked in "knife fighting" over the direction:

Camp One is the accelerationist wing, which won this round. They want zero oversight, free markets, and innovation above all. "We got to beat China," as Jesse summarizes their argument. Camp Two is the "operationalist" or "safety-curious" wing, led by figures thinking about FDA-style reviews. They're quietly convening Wall Street to discuss cyber risk and exploring regulatory guardrails. Camp Three is the national security establishment, which is fractured into its own internal battles: the NSA blacklists Anthropic while using Mythos; CISA (the nation's top cyber defense agency) can't access Mythos. Because the national security camp can't agree, it has no sway. Camp Four is Chief of Staff Susie Wilds, who acts as gatekeeper for what reaches the Oval Office. Wilds, largely undiscussed in media, has more power than people realize, and she sided with the accelerationists this round.

The outcome: a voluntary AI model review executive order was canceled at the last minute (literally as CEOs arrived to watch the signing). No rules for AI now exist, not even voluntary ones. Jesse finds this alarming because the AI industry has been preaching responsible alarm for years—CEOs saying regulation is necessary, the Pope calling for human-first approaches—but the sector is "building itself on responsible alarm, but without responsible action." The exception is Anthropic, which actually implements safety practices.

Jesse's key argument: crypto can teach AI the importance of verification layers. "Don't trust the CEOs. Just find a way to verify. And maybe this is where crypto is not being so thirsty. Like AI should be thirsty for us because we need to build a receipt layer for AI." Blockchain is one tool for this (model provenance, test records, sign-offs), but the larger point is building accountability infrastructure now, before something goes wrong. "When something goes wrong, the question is not going to be who regulates AI that comes out in the future. It's who can help us now and why did it happen and how can we stop it?"

KK noted the potentially shifting White House dynamics and offered to map the four factions to Game of Thrones families if listeners were interested, providing a touch of levity to an otherwise sobering analysis.

**Tylosaurus Rex: 43-Foot Marine Predator**

As the episode's obligatory good-news segment, Jesse shared news of a paleontological find: a Tylosaurus Rex discovered partially in Texas. The creature measures 43 feet long—roughly the length of a school bus and twice the size of a great white shark. It is the largest water creature of all time. Jesse, who has a dinosaur obsession, highlighted the creature's enormous jaw and bite force. The discovery provides perspective on geological timescales and the incredible diversity and scale of life Earth has supported over millions of years. The Apple TV series on animated dinosaurs was recommended as a window into these ancient creatures.

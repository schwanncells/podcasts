# Freakonomics Radio — "670. Beeconomics 101"

**Host:** Stephen Dubner (intro), Steve Levitt (guest host). **Guests:** Chris Hyatt (beekeeper, Hyatt Honey Company founder's son, past president of American Honey Producers Association); Michael T. Roberts (founding executive director, Resnick Center for Food Law and Policy at UCLA Law School); Wally Thurman (professor emeritus of agricultural economics, North Carolina State University); Alex Ziposnik (historian at King's College London, medieval economic history).

## TL;DR

- **U.S. honey consumption doubled since 2000 (350M to 700M pounds annually) while retail prices tripled, but commercial beekeepers' net income collapsed** — domestic production fell from 70–75% of U.S. consumption 20–30 years ago to only 20–25% today, squeezed by cheap imports and declining per-hive yields.
- **China dumped honey at 30–40 cents per pound in early 2000s—one-tenth production cost** — Commerce Department tariffs in 2001 temporarily doubled prices, but China evaded duties through transshipment falsely labeled as Vietnamese or Indian honey (Myanmar and Taiwan honey imports surged 500% in one year, physically impossible).
- **Honey is one of the top three most frauded foods globally** — Alibaba openly advertises "designer syrups" for mixing with honey to pass FDA tests; adulteration includes immature honey, syrup dilution, resin processing to remove antibiotics/contaminants, and color manipulation.
- **Varroa mites weakened bee colonies by half compared to 1960s baseline** — Hyatt's father achieved 200–300 pounds per hive in Alberta in the 1970s; today's average is 80–100 pounds; beekeepers treat hives 4–6 times annually versus zero in the 1960s, as mites transmit viruses and reduce bee lifespan.
- **Almonds create hidden vulnerability in U.S. food supply—90% of American bees migrate to California for three weeks in February/March** — without bees, the almond industry collapses, forcing growers to subsidize beekeeping through pollination fees; the industry is developing self-fertile almond varieties to reduce bee dependence, which could devastate beekeepers.
- **Honey lacks a federal standard of identity, unlike peanut butter** — the FDA requires a safety issue to act; fraud alone doesn't trigger regulation, so adulterated honey flows freely into the market and industrial food chains (cereals, Robitussin, honey hams).
- **Medieval beekeeping thrived on beeswax for church candles until the Reformation collapsed demand** — wax price fell from five days' labor per pound (1300) to less than one day (1544); beekeepers then lost honey's dominance as a sweetener when sugar arrived from the Americas, paralleling today's threat from almond-tree self-sufficiency.
- **Pollination externalities are partially internalized through market contracts, not Meade's theoretical failure** — Cheung (1973) discovered beekeepers advertised pollination services in rural Washington; today, almonds pay high fees while good honey forage earns beekeepers payments from landowners, depending on season and demand.

**The Honey Market Collapse and Import Pressure**

Chris Hyatt explains that while total U.S. honey consumption has doubled since 2000, climbing from 350 million to 700 million pounds annually, his family and other domestic beekeepers are in crisis. Two to three decades ago, American beekeepers supplied 70–75% of U.S. honey consumption. Today, that share has plummeted to just 20–25%. Retail honey prices have tripled (accounting for inflation), yet Hyatt receives prices far below the market. He identifies the culprit: cheap imports, primarily from China.

In 2001, the U.S. Commerce Department concluded that China was dumping honey on the American market at artificially low prices—as little as 30–40 cents per pound, a price so far below Hyatt's production costs that it made direct competition impossible. Dumping in this context meant Chinese producers were labeling products as honey that were actually syrup-adulterated blends. Tariffs were imposed, and prices temporarily doubled to $1.40–$1.50 per pound, reflecting what Hyatt believes is the true cost of production. However, the effect was short-lived. Chinese exporters adapted by transshipping honey through Vietnam, India, Taiwan, Myanmar, and other countries, falsifying documentation to claim foreign origin. Hyatt points to a Georgetown Economics study showing that Myanmar and Taiwan's honey exports surged 500% in a single year—a figure he calls "physically impossible"—revealing the scale of the mislabeling scheme.

**Honey Fraud and Food Adulteration**

Michael Roberts, food law expert at UCLA, confirms that honey has ranked among the world's top three most frauded foods for years, alongside milk and olive oil. The methods are sophisticated. One major adulteration technique involves harvesting honey too early (immature green honey), removing excess moisture via vacuum processing, and then using a resin-based technology—perfected by Chinese manufacturers—to strip color, contaminants, and antibiotics. Producers then blend the processed honey with cheaper syrups, creating what Roberts calls "syrup-diluted honey." Even Alibaba, China's largest e-commerce platform, openly advertises designer syrups engineered to pass FDA tests when mixed with honey.

Roberts argues that one reason fraud persists is that adulterated honey is not classified as a food safety hazard. The FDA prioritizes safety over authenticity; absent a direct health threat, regulators lack incentive to act. He notes that when he has brought fraudulent products to the FDA, the first question is always: "Is there a safety problem?" If the answer is no, attention fades. This creates an incentive vacuum. In contrast, other foods like olive oil, fish, spices, and vinegars face similar fraud risks, all stemming from a pattern: specialty goods imported in large quantities, relatively easy to adulterate, and difficult for consumers or regulators to verify without expensive testing.

**The Varroa Mite Crisis and Declining Productivity**

Hyatt's biggest challenge is not just price but declining bee health. The varroa mite, tiny as half a grain of rice, has devastated American hives since the 1960s. The mites feed on bee fat bodies, weakening their immunity and reducing lifespan from 40–45 days to fewer days. More critically, varroa transmit viruses that mutate into increasingly virulent strains. Hyatt's father in the 1960s faced no varroa problem; today, commercial operations like Hyatt's treat hives 4–6 times per year.

The impact on honey yield is striking. Hyatt's father achieved 200–300 pounds of honey per hive in Alberta during the 1970s. Today, despite advances in bee science and technology, Hyatt averages 80–100 pounds per hive in a good year. Poor years yield 50 pounds. Habitat loss compounds the problem: fewer blossoms, less alfalfa, and reduced wild sweet clover as agricultural land consolidates. Neonicotinoid pesticides persist longer than earlier generations of DDT and organophosphates, weakening hives further. Hyatt operates roughly 18,000 hives split between California, North Dakota, and Washington, producing 1–1.5 million pounds annually. Caring for a billion bees during peak season—40,000 to 60,000 per hive—involves intensive labor: diagnosing hives, moving frames, administering medication, feeding, and getting stung daily.

**The Almond Pollination Machine and Economic Dependence**

Despite the honey crisis, Hyatt's operation remains solvent because of pollination services, not honey sales. His revenue split is roughly 60% honey, 40% pollination, but in drought years when North Dakota honey production fails, pollination fees can exceed honey income. The reason is almonds.

Wally Thurman, an agricultural economist, explains that nearly 90% of all American bees migrate to California almond orchards in a narrow three-week window in late February and early March. Almonds are the most valuable early-blooming crop, and the industry needs just about every bee in the continental United States. Hyatt rents hives on flatbed trucks carrying 400–500 hives per load, stacked four pallets high. Without bees, the California almond industry would collapse.

Almonds offer little nectar for honey production, so beekeepers earn pollination fees—currently high because early-season bee replacement (forced by varroa losses) requires expensive hive-splitting methods. Thurman notes that the almond industry, facing rising pollination costs and beekeeper attrition, has incentive to reduce bee dependence. Self-fertile and self-pollinating almond varieties have already been introduced to California orchards. If this trend accelerates, beekeepers will lose their financial lifeline.

**The Pollination Externality: Theory and Market Reality**

Thurman discusses how honeybees exemplify the economic theory of positive externalities. In the 1950s, Nobel laureate James Meade theorized that bees benefit apple growers by pollinating their crops, while apple nectar benefits beekeepers, yet neither party compensates the other for these mutual benefits. Meade concluded the free market produces too few bees and too few apples.

In 1973, economist Stephen Cheung challenged this conclusion by discovering that beekeepers in rural Washington advertised pollination services in the yellow pages. The market had already internalized the externality through contracts. Today, payment direction depends on season and crop. Early-season crops like almonds pay beekeepers heavily because demand is acute and honey forage is poor. In late summer, when nectar flows abundantly, landowners sometimes pay beekeepers to place hives on their property as valued honey forage. The market dynamic reveals that positive externalities need not be uncompensated if transaction costs are low and bargaining partners can find each other.

**Colony Collapse Disorder: Market Resilience and Containment**

In winter 2006–2007, colony collapse disorder struck. Adult bees disappeared mysteriously from hives across the nation without dead bees left behind. Overwinter mortality doubled from the historical norm of 15% to 30%. Thurman and colleagues expected this catastrophe to spike pollination prices, honey prices, and reduce bee colony counts. Instead, when they examined USDA data, they found no effect. Colony counts actually increased slightly within years of CCD's onset.

The explanation lies in bee biology and beekeeping management. A 30,000-bee colony can be regenerated in six weeks through hive-splitting—placing healthy bees into an empty hive and letting them rear a new queen. Beekeepers had perfected this method over centuries. The only market signal from CCD appeared in early-season pollination fees, which spiked because beekeepers could not ramp up replacement colonies in time for the February–March almond bloom. Late-summer prices for honey and queen bees remained flat, indicating that beekeepers compensated for losses outside the critical almond window.

**Historical Parallel: Medieval Beekeeping and Technological Disruption**

Alex Ziposnik, a medieval economic historian, reveals that today's beekeepers face a dilemma their predecessors endured. In medieval Christian Europe, honeybees' primary value was beeswax for candles. Churches spent more on wax annually than almost any other expense after building maintenance. The Reformation and later advances in secular lighting decimated wax demand. Between 1300 and 1544, the price of wax plummeted from five days' labor per pound to less than one day.

Just as beekeepers adapted by shifting focus to honey as a sweetener, that market too collapsed when sugar from American plantations reached Europe, offering abundant, cheap sweetness. Ziposnik notes the historical parallel: medieval beekeepers lost one revenue stream (wax), then another (honey as sweetener) to exogenous shocks. Modern beekeepers face the same pattern—honey income compressed by cheap imports, pollination fees under threat from self-fertile almond breeding.

**Regulatory Gaps and the Incentive Problem**

Michael Roberts emphasizes that solving honey fraud requires coordinated action: stricter consumer protection laws, supply-chain accountability, and calibrated criminal enforcement. Yet agencies lack incentive because fraud is not a safety issue. Roberts suggests following the False Claims Act model, which pays bounties to whistleblowers who uncover trade fraud. A similar bounty system for food fraud could realign incentives.

Roberts also notes the role of retail gatekeepers. Decades ago, FDA panels on economically motivated adulteration were nearly empty because lawyers advised companies not to attend ("What we don't know won't hurt us legally"). That attitude has shifted as large retailers shift to private-label products and care about consumer trust. However, individual buyers in the middle of supply chains still prioritize lowest cost over authenticity, creating the meeting point where fraud survives.

**The Broader Food Fraud Pattern**

Roberts illustrates the fraud pattern using olive oil. One popular supermarket brand, which Roberts knew was "like the worst brand on the market," was a prospective student's favorite. Olive oil fraud targets high-value, easy-to-adulterate goods that are hard to verify without lab testing. Producers blend cheaper seed oils (sunflower, soybean, palm) with small amounts of real olive oil, then manipulate color and flavor with dyes. A practical defense exists: consumers should check labels for countries of origin and avoid multi-country blends, which indicate more blending and greater fraud risk. Single-source olive oil from a specialty supplier is more likely to be genuine. Roberts' UCLA class taste tests consistently fail—students prefer adulterated oil because they are accustomed to its taste, highlighting the central tension: fraud is not a safety issue, but it undermines producer honesty and domestic industry viability.

**Paths Forward: Who Bears the Cost?**

Steve Levitt notes that the honey crisis ultimately reflects misaligned incentives. Beekeepers don't capture the full economic value of pollination services—that value accrues to almond growers, food manufacturers, and consumers. Without receiving compensation for positive externalities, beekeepers lack resources to fight fraud or prevent colony losses. Food manufacturers using honey as a cheap input (cereals, yogurt, granola bars, Robitussin) have no incentive to demand authenticity. The most powerful argument for action, Roberts and Levitt agree, is the ecological one: bees underpin pollination for the entire food chain. Losing domestic bee populations due to fraud-driven industry collapse would create a genuine crisis.

Yet policy remains stuck. Honey has never had a federal standard of identity, unlike peanut butter. (That standard took so long to negotiate—Al Gore famously criticized the decade-long, expensive peanut butter debate—that the FDA effectively stopped creating new food standards.) Without a legal definition of honey, regulators cannot definitively exclude adulterated products. Voluntary standards exist (U.S. Pharmacopoeia, International Codex Alimentarius), but they are not binding in the United States.

Hyatt remains guardedly optimistic about the bees themselves, expressing love for the work of caring for them and wonder at a summer's "million pounds of honey flying through the air in North Dakota airspace." But the romance has faded. Building back lost hives each year is depressing work in an industry slowly starved by fraud, cheap imports, and technological obsolescence.

# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Prediction markets are threatening national security. Who's gonna fix it?"

**Host:** Darian Woods, Ricky Mulvey. **Guest:** Richard Blumenthal (U.S. Senator, Connecticut, Democrat; authored Prediction Markets Security and Integrity Act of 2026; focused on financial regulation and consumer protection).

## TL;DR

- **A U.S. Special Forces soldier was arrested for betting on military action in Venezuela** — Gannon Ken Van Dyke allegedly wagered on the capture of President Nicolás Maduro, a mission he was part of, demonstrating the insider trading risk baked into prediction markets.
- **Polymarket operates a "prediction market mullet"** — regulated U.S. business in front (customers identified), unregulated international cryptocurrency side in back (anonymous trading). Majority of trades happen anonymously on the unregulated side.
- **Chainalysis tracks suspicious trades across the blockchain** — Polymarket partnered with the crypto fraud investigation firm to catch insider trading. Cryptocurrency transactions are permanent and traceable, but sophisticated criminals can evade detection using fresh wallets, money launderers, and untraceable coins.
- **Senator Blumenthal's bill would regulate prediction markets like sportsbooks** — the Prediction Markets Security and Integrity Act of 2026 aims to restrict markets tied to national security and sensitive information, increase platform oversight, and limit advertising on broadcast TV (but not social media).
- **Prediction markets offer economic utility but create regulatory blind spots** — they provide real-time sentiment on inflation and recession risk, are often more accurate than political polls, and help answer major questions like "who will be the next president," but regulators are still figuring out what rules to apply.
- **TikTok ads for prediction markets show deceptive marketing** — Blumenthal argues that ads promising easy money (e.g., woman claiming she paid rent with Kalshi profits) violate existing federal consumer protection laws that ban misleading marketing.

**The Insider Trading Risk and the Van Dyke Case**

Prediction markets create real-time betting on news and political events. The economic value is substantial—they often outperform traditional political polls and provide live sentiment on macroeconomic questions like inflation and recession likelihood. However, they also expose a critical vulnerability: people with non-public information can profit from bets placed before that information becomes public. The Van Dyke case crystallizes this risk. A U.S. Special Forces soldier allegedly wagered on the capture of President Nicolás Maduro on Polymarket, a prediction market platform. Van Dyke was directly involved in the military action, placing him in possession of non-public information about whether the mission would succeed. When his trades cashed in, he allegedly attempted to cover his tracks by requesting account deletion and trying to change his email address on his crypto exchange.

**Polymarket's Dual Structure and the Anonymity Problem**

Polymarket operates two distinct businesses. In the United States, the platform functions as a regulated market where customers must be identified—the company knows who is placing bets. Internationally, however, Polymarket runs an unregulated cryptocurrency-based platform where traders can operate anonymously using blockchain transactions. The majority of trades on Polymarket happen on the unregulated, anonymous international side. This structure—described as a "prediction market mullet" (business in front, cryptocurrency party in the back)—allows Polymarket to claim it is managing insider trading risk while preserving the lucrative anonymity that attracts traders to crypto markets. The irony is stark: while individual traders remain anonymous, all trades are recorded permanently on the blockchain, making them visible to anyone with forensic capability.

**Tracing Cryptocurrency and the Limits of Detection**

Despite anonymity, cryptocurrency is not impenetrable. Polymarket recently partnered with Chainalysis, a crypto forensics firm specializing in fraud detection, to identify suspicious trades by tracking blockchain transactions. Adam Hart, an investigator at Chainalysis, notes that the permanence of blockchain records is a significant investigative advantage. Transactions cannot be deleted; if a perpetrator makes a single mistake revealing their identity, evidence persists indefinitely. U.S. law enforcement has become proficient at seizing illicit crypto—the IRS Criminal Investigation Service reportedly seizes more money in cryptocurrency than in all other asset classes combined.

However, sophisticated criminals have countermeasures. They can use fresh wallets, hire money launderers to obscure transaction trails, and trade in cryptocurrencies marketed as untraceable. International actors using Polymarket's offshore platform may be entirely beyond U.S. authorities' reach. The Van Dyke case succeeded partly because he was not sophisticated—he made obvious mistakes, making identification straightforward. A more careful adversary would likely evade detection.

**Blumenthal's Regulatory Approach and the "Fiction" of Offshore Separation**

Senator Richard Blumenthal, a Connecticut Democrat, argues that Polymarket cannot have it both ways: operating a U.S. regulated platform while claiming the international side is separate and unregulated. "They really are an American company," Blumenthal contends. "They do have an American platform. They say one of their platforms is offshore, but that is a fiction that we need to cut through." His proposed legislation, the Prediction Markets Security and Integrity Act of 2026, treats prediction markets similarly to how states regulate sportsbooks. The bill would restrict the types of events that can be traded—specifically banning markets involving classified information or national security matters—and increase regulatory oversight of platforms.

Blumenthal acknowledges that prediction markets provide genuine economic utility. Unlike pure gambling platforms like FanDuel and DraftKings, which take substantial cuts and offer no broader value, prediction markets help inform public discourse on major questions: who will win an election, will a recession occur, will inflation rise. These markets often outperform traditional polling. Blumenthal clarifies that his goal is not to ban prediction markets entirely or prohibit gambling, but to increase transparency and prevent insider trading and national security leaks.

**Deceptive Advertising and Existing Consumer Protections**

During the episode, the hosts presented Senator Blumenthal with screenshots of prediction market advertisements on TikTok that he had not previously seen. One ad showed a user claiming they could profit from rising gas prices on Kalshi. Another featured a woman asserting that she had paid her rent with money made betting on predictions. Blumenthal's reaction was swift: these ads potentially violate existing federal consumer protection laws that prohibit deceptive and misleading marketing. No new legislation is required to address them—federal law already bans false advertising that implies easy profit or guaranteed returns. Blumenthal indicated he would pursue enforcement action against platforms under existing law.

**The Unresolved Regulatory Frontier**

The episode underscores that prediction markets remain a regulatory frontier. Even a senator who has authored legislation to address the issue acknowledges blind spots. Blumenthal's bill limits prediction market advertising on broadcast television but does not address social media, where Kalshi and other platforms are actively recruiting users with claims of easy returns. The regulatory landscape is fractured: no consistent standards exist for platform transparency, no unified rules govern what events can be traded, and no clarity exists about how national security risks should be policed. As prediction markets grow in popularity and volume, the gap between economic utility and regulatory risk continues to widen.

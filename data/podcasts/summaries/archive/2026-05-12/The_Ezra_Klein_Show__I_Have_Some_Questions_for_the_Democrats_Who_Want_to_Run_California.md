# The Ezra Klein Show — "I Have Some Questions for the Democrats Who Want to Run California"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guests:** Tom Steyer (businessman, former presidential candidate); Javier Becerra (former California attorney general, former U.S. Health and Human Services Secretary); Katie Porter (U.S. Representative, California); Matt Mahan (Mayor of San Jose); Antonio Villagosa (former Mayor of Los Angeles, former California Assembly Speaker).

## TL;DR

- **California construction costs 2x Texas, 4x for affordable housing** — A RAND study found multifamily apartments cost over twice per square foot to build in California as in Texas, and affordable housing costs four times more per square foot than market-rate in Texas. Construction timelines are 49 months in California vs. 27 in Texas.
- **Modular construction promises 20% cost reduction, but past efforts have failed** — Tom Steyer cited Katerra ($2B raised, bankrupt), Veve, Intecra, and Factory OS as cautionary tales. He argues the state must guarantee orders and update building codes to make the economics work for manufacturers.
- **Cities layer on 10-20% costs in fees alone** — Matt Mahan itemized over 10 pages of accumulated fees (parking, parks, affordable housing mitigation, traffic) that collectively kill project economics. San Jose cut fees by two-thirds, triggering 4,000 housing starts in two years. Mahan advocates state fee caps with developer-led "builder's remedy" override if cities miss deadlines.
- **Labor costs and prevailing wage create a wrenching trade-off** — Javier Becerra proposes height-based tiering: infill/low-rise (8 stories or less) gets flexible labor standards; taller projects require prevailing wage. Katie Porter broke with labor unions, rejecting prevailing wage for residential, to protect affordability. This splits the Democratic field.
- **Affordable housing faces compounding obstacles** — Katie Porter explained affordable projects get more delays, community resistance, zoning restrictions, and financing from seven separate funding pools. Consolidating financing and state land contribution would help; so would eliminating redundant design standards for small multifamily (4-unit projects shouldn't match 400-unit rules).
- **Prop 13 drives perverse incentives but no consensus to reform it** — Antonio Villagosa opposed Prop 13 since 1978 but defended "holding the line on property taxes"; he calls instead for comprehensive tax reform (broadening the base, rebalancing commercial/residential split that has flipped since 1978). Becerra, Steyer, and others want to close the corporate real-estate tax loophole to fund housing.
- **State enforcement tools: litigation vs. builder's remedy** — Becerra defended AG-era lawsuits (Huntington Beach, San Mateo County) but acknowledged litigation is slow and results are mixed (Huntington Beach still hasn't built promised units). Mahan argued builder's remedy—automatic approval rights if cities miss permitting deadlines—is more effective than years of legal wrangling.
- **No Republican candidates showed up** — Klein noted Republicans were invited but did not attend the Oakland forum, making this a Democratic-only debate on housing strategy.

---

**The Construction Cost Crisis**

The RAND Corporation study formed the empirical spine of the forum. Klein highlighted two stark findings: construction costs per square foot for multifamily apartments exceed twice the Texas rate, and affordable housing in California costs four times as much per square foot to build as market-rate housing in Texas. Time magnifies the cost problem. A Texas multifamily project takes 27 months; Colorado, 37; California, 49. Klein noted that time acts as a cost multiplier because interest accrues, land holdings lengthen, and regulatory uncertainty compounds, forcing developers to add risk premiums to financing.

Steyer emphasized three cost drivers: labor, materials, and financing. He advocated modular construction—manufacturing housing parts off-site like cars and assembling them on-site—citing industry estimates of 20% cost reductions, with some manufacturers claiming higher savings. However, he acknowledged a graveyard of failed modular startups: Katerra (raised $2B, went bankrupt), Veve, Intecra, and Factory OS (recently rebranded and recapitalized). Steyer argued failure stems from lack of orders and revenue. The state can unlock modular economics by guaranteeing procurements and amending building codes to accept factory-built components. He noted 40,000 permitted housing units in San Francisco sit unbuilt because construction costs make finished units unaffordable, even though zoning already allows them.

On financing, Steyer highlighted a community bank he and his wife founded in Oakland that has financed 17,000 low-income units. He advocated aggressive use of state financing programs and supported Buffy Wicks' proposed $10 billion housing bond. He also outlined a broader strategy: closing a corporate real estate tax loophole worth over $20 billion to the state, which would shift housing from an "unfunded mandate" to a "funded mandate" for cities and counties, removing the fiscal burden that makes mayors and councils resist density.

**The Fee Problem and Local Control**

Matt Mahan brought hard numbers from San Jose. His city had approved 22,000 housing units that weren't being built because project economics didn't work. He isolated two major levers: time and fees. Over two years, San Jose moved multifamily housing approvals in its downtown and transit corridors to "ministerial approval"—essentially by-right, exempt from planning commission review and CEQA environmental review. A 560-unit project recently secured approval "in no time," he said.

Mahan then addressed fees. He described over 10 pages of accumulated charges: parking fees, park fees, affordable housing fees, traffic mitigation. Each sounds justified on paper, but stacked together they add 10-20% to housing costs. San Jose's city council had a "really tough conversation" about fee reduction. They cut fees by over two-thirds, immediately triggering 2,000 housing starts in the following year, with another 2,000 securing financing. However, a council member lost his seat, and the previous mayor lost his council majority, because fee cuts were framed as giveaways to developers.

Mahan proposed the state cap local fees and require cities seeking higher fees to produce feasibility studies proving the project still "pencils" (achieves financial viability). He also advocated restoring a form of tax-increment financing through limited redevelopment authority, allowing cities to bond against projected future property-tax increases to fund infrastructure, easing the upfront cost burden on new housing.

**The Labor-Cost Dilemma**

Javier Becerra's comprehensive housing plan calls for more union labor and higher wage standards. Klein presented a Turner Center analysis showing prevailing wage standards increase housing costs by roughly $94,000 per unit—a painful trade-off for Democrats who champion both affordable housing and high wages.

Becerra proposed a height-based split. Infill housing and projects up to eight stories would have flexible labor standards (developers negotiate wages, unions can pursue private legal action for wage violations). Projects taller than eight stories trigger prevailing wage requirements. He also proposed a $10 billion housing bond to stabilize financing and give developers predictable funding.

Katie Porter broke decisively with labor unions, stating she would not impose prevailing wage or skilled-trades requirements on residential housing at this moment. She said she took "heat from labor" at a meeting with the labor federation for refusing these mandates, but she cannot justify making housing unaffordable when she has three teenagers who might otherwise end up homeless or on a couch. Porter advocated instead for robust labor-law enforcement through government oversight of wage and workforce violations, a role unions have traditionally filled but the state can fund directly.

Becerra doubled down on his point that workers building homes should be able to afford homes themselves, but acknowledged Ezra's challenge: if you raise one cost driver (labor), you must cut another (materials, financing, or permitting time). He pointed to Trump-era tariffs inflating material costs and to the need for stable financing as the true cost-reduction lever.

**Affordable Housing's Compounding Problems**

Katie Porter explained why affordable housing costs so much more to construct. Affordable projects face more delays, community opposition, zoning restrictions, and land-use uncertainty. They also require piecing together financing from seven different funding streams—each with its own terms, expiration dates, and conditions. By the time developers secure the seventh pool, the first may expire or change terms, resetting the financing puzzle. Porter advocated consolidating financing into one larger pool (similar to what market-rate developers use when accessing Wall Street capital) and having the state contribute land for affordable housing, one of the largest cost factors.

She also noted that affordable housing design standards force small multifamily projects (4-unit buildings) to meet the same architectural and infrastructure requirements as 400-unit projects, adding unnecessary time and cost. Scaling design standards to match project size could accelerate smaller multifamily development.

Porter emphasized that affordable housing inefficiency is not inevitable. She highlighted Colorado, which builds housing much faster (37 months vs. 49 in California) while maintaining environmental and labor standards. The speed differential alone could reduce California housing costs by 10-20%, according to the RAND study.

**Prop 13 and Tax System Reform**

Klein asked Antonio Villagosa about his campaign site's defense of Prop 13, California's 1978 property-tax cap. Villagosa clarified he had opposed Prop 13 since 1978, but supported "holding the line on property taxes" under current law. He argued for comprehensive tax system reform rather than Prop 13 repeal alone.

Villagosa outlined the "fiscalization of land use" dynamic Steyer had mentioned. Prop 13 created perverse incentives: cities prefer low-tax commercial (strip malls, car lots) over housing because housing requires school and healthcare infrastructure investment, while retail generates property tax revenue with no burden. Commercial property paid 60% of property tax in 1978; homeowners, 40%. The split has flipped.

Villagosa proposed fixing the whole tax system: addressing the unfair disparity between pre-1978 homeowners (locked in low assessments) and buyers today; introducing a statewide services tax (most states have one); and broadening the income-tax base so California doesn't over-rely on top earners for revenue. This relieves pressure on property taxes while incentivizing cities to build housing rather than retail.

Klein pressed Villagosa on how these reforms would translate to fee relief. Villagosa and Mahan agreed: impact fees are the immediate culprit, and the state must cap them.

**Enforcement: Litigation vs. Builder's Remedy**

Governor Newsom has sued 15 cities for housing noncompliance. Becerra defended AG-era litigation (Huntington Beach, San Mateo County), noting the state must enforce housing law. However, he acknowledged litigation is slow and penalties often become "a cost of doing business." His approach: first offer incentives (priority access to state housing funds), then escalate to penalties that grow over time.

Mahan pushed back, arguing litigation is ineffective. Huntington Beach is still not building despite years of legal wrangling. He advocates builder's remedy—automatic approval rights for developers if cities miss permitting deadlines or use fees/codes to block housing. This is more efficient than years of appeals and policy rewrites.

Klein asked if persuasion rather than enforcement could align cities and state. Mahan suggested restoring limited tax-increment financing, which allows cities to fund infrastructure without passing all costs to developers. This makes housing projects more economically attractive to local elected officials and communities.

**Los Angeles as a Cautionary Tale**

Villagosa recounted his housing record as LA Mayor: more market-rate, workforce, affordable, and homeless housing in eight years (amid the recession) than in the 12 years prior. The downtown skyline grew from 20,000 to 60,000 residents. He initiated transit-oriented development districts, a model Becerra and others reference.

Klein noted recent LA policy setbacks. Mayor Karen Bass signed ED1 (expedited affordable housing) but rolled it back after local opposition when it worked "almost too well." LA's Measure ULA (5% transfer tax on property sales over $5M) has dampened development. SB 79 (state law increasing density near transit) was undercut by LA City Council rezoning to slow it down. Villagosa opposed Measure ULA, arguing the transfer tax's 5% rate, though marketed as a "mansion tax," actually applies to multifamily and commercial property and discourages development broadly.

---

**Common Ground and Remaining Tensions**

All five candidates support building more housing, streamlining permitting, and reducing costs. They disagree on the role of prevailing wage (Porter vs. Becerra) and the proper enforcement mechanism (litigation vs. builder's remedy). Villagosa and Mahan emphasize local cooperation and fiscal tools; Becerra stresses state enforcement; Steyer highlights modular manufacturing and financing innovation.

One point went unchallenged: time is a cost driver. Every candidate acknowledged that California's 49-month permitting timeline vs. 27 months in Texas inflates construction costs by billions. The solutions offered—by-right approval, ministerial review, fee caps, timelines with teeth, builder's remedy—all target time reduction. Whether these prove sufficient remains an open question, but the forum established that housing affordability in California ultimately turns on process speed and cost structure, not just political will.

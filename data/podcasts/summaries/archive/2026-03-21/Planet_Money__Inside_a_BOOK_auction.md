# Planet Money — "Inside a BOOK auction"

**Host:** Alexi Horowitz-Ghazi. **Guests:** Alex Goldmark (executive producer of Planet Money; "grand boss"); Laura Nolan and Jane Von Maren (literary agents who brokered the Planet Money book deal, earning a ~15% commission); Tom Mayer (executive editor, W.W. Norton).

## TL;DR

- **The Planet Money book started with agents, not Planet Money** — NPR retained literary agents Laura Nolan and Jane Von Maren to scout podcast properties for book potential; they cold-approached Goldmark based partly on Planet Money's track record of selling nearly 17,000 comic books and over 20,000 t-shirts.
- **The book concept is a field guide to the global economy** — after Goldmark's initial ideas (pamphlets, narrow industry explainers) were shot down as too narrow, the team settled on a broad frame: a series of vivid reported stories bringing daily life into economic focus, covering work, dating, investing, and more.
- **Publishing is now dominated by five conglomerates** — the "Big Five" have swallowed formerly independent publishers over decades, increasing pressure to bet on authors with built-in audiences rather than riskier first-time voices; the industry follows a power law where 20% of books make 80% of the money.
- **Tom Mayer receives ~500 proposals a year from vetted agents, buys 10–12** — that is a 2% acquisition rate from an already pre-filtered pipeline; editors do not accept unsolicited proposals, making agents the first of many gatekeeping layers.
- **23 publishers requested meetings — the most agents had ever seen** — this allowed Laura and Jane to run a "wedding cake auction" (two-round best bid), advancing the top bidders in tiers rather than forcing a single high-pressure shot.
- **The final contest came down to Norton (a dolphin) vs. a Big Five whale** — the whale offered more money and wider distribution; Norton offered slightly less but proposed full color and an untested pitch to get the book into educational courseware and economics syllabi.
- **The advance was a seven-figure sum, less than two million dollars** — as disclosed in a Norton press release; agents declined to give the exact number on tape.
- **The "winner's curse" haunted the bidding** — the term for the tendency of auction winners to overpay; Norton led after round one, which set the floor for round two and made Mayer simultaneously feel like a winner and fear he'd overpaid.

**How a Podcast Becomes a Book Proposal**

The Planet Money book originated not from editorial ambition but from market signals: two literary agents retained by NPR to identify bookable properties approached executive producer Alex Goldmark after Planet Money's live musical based on its Microface superhero sold out. Goldmark was initially lukewarm — Planet Money's participatory projects (a t-shirt, a barrel of oil, a comic book, a record, a board game) were typically not designed to turn a profit. But agents Laura Nolan and Jane Von Maren argued that a book could extend the show's reach into airports, libraries, and schools in ways a podcast cannot.

After Goldmark agreed to explore the idea, the four parties — Goldmark, writer Alex Myasi (who had reported Planet Money episodes and co-wrote the Atlas Obscura food book), Nolan, and Von Maren — spent over a year passing a shared Google Doc back and forth. Goldmark's early ideas (short pamphlets on individual industries) were too narrow; the agents pressed for the broadest possible frame. They landed on a "field guide to the global economy" — a series of reported stories translating economic forces into daily life, from how people work and date to how they invest and retire. The resulting proposal laid out sample writing, a full chapter outline, and a business case. Book proposals, Mayer explains, serve dual purposes: they are the vehicle by which nonfiction books are bought and sold, and they give publishers a preview of how they might market the book to readers.

**The Publishing Industry's Structure**

Mayer explains the consolidated landscape of book publishing. Once thousands of independent publishers, the industry has been swallowed down to five conglomerates — Penguin Random House and Macmillan (each owned by large German corporations), HarperCollins (News Corp), and others — plus a handful of independent publishers like Norton, which is employee-owned. The consolidation has made the bottom line more important and pushed publishers to favor authors with platforms and built-in audiences, similar to how Hollywood has leaned into sequels and familiar IP. Mayer likens his annual list to a portfolio of stocks: 150 books a year, some doing very well, some failing, the goal being diversity and an upward-trending overall portfolio. He also notes that "publishing is a power law business" — 20% of books generate 80% of revenue, and many books sell virtually zero copies.

**Seeding the Market and the Speed-Dating Gauntlet**

For months before sending out the proposal, Nolan and Von Maren worked the informal intelligence network of the publishing industry — an interconnected world they compare to a high school, where key information travels through power lunches, breakfasts, and drinks between agents and editors. By the time the proposal was ready, 23 publishers had requested meetings. Before the pandemic, agents would tour authors around town in black cars; the 23 meetings were held over four days on Zoom. Publishers pitched different visions: one offered a glossy, graphics-heavy coffee table book; Norton pitched a textbook and courseware play to get the book into economics syllabi.

**Auction Formats and the Wedding Cake**

Mayer describes three classic book auction formats. A "round-robin" auction proceeds like poker — each bidder can top the previous bid in turn, producing the most rational price but potentially taking a very long time. A "one-round best bid" gives everyone a single shot with minimal information about competitors, leading to unpredictable results and risk for all parties. Because of the strong competitive interest for the Planet Money proposal, Nolan and Von Maren chose a "two-round best bid" — Mayer's preferred nickname is the "wedding cake auction," because bidders are culled in tiers like layers of cake until the top two finalists sit atop it like cake toppers.

A key information-management move: Nolan and Von Maren strategically let slip that 23 publishers had met, rather than keeping that number secret. For Mayer, chasing a "literary high," the revelation had its intended effect — he began strategizing immediately to make the second round. Sixteen bids arrived by the 11 a.m. deadline; enough ties existed that 10 editors advanced to round two. The agents told the 10 finalists the current top bid but not how many had advanced. Publishers knocked out of the first round were told delicately, to protect ongoing relationships in an industry where agents pitch the same editors week after week.

**The Beauty Contest and the Decision**

After Norton led round one (setting the new floor for round two), Mayer and his team faced the "winner's curse" — the economic concept describing how auction winners often overpay when an asset is hard to value and competition is intense. He had to confab with Norton's sales and marketing teams, build sales projections using "comps" (comparable titles from similar podcasts, including the 99% Invisible book that became a New York Times bestseller), and estimate the advance against royalties that would be justifiable. He also had to think strategically: Norton is employee-owned, meaning it is "not playing with house money," and its bids carry more internal weight than those from conglomerate publishers.

After round two, the bids clustered closely again, triggering the "beauty contest" — the final stage where numbers are essentially tied and the decision comes down to chemistry, vision, and strategy. Norton and one Big Five whale remained. The whale offered more money, a full-court publicity press, and proven access to airport and indie bookstores. Norton offered slightly less money but full-color printing and the untested pitch of getting the book into educational courseware. Goldmark, after days of deliberation, chose Norton, citing the educational mission alignment. The deal, disclosed in a Norton press release, was a seven-figure advance — more than one million dollars, less than two. As Goldmark put it: "We got a lot of money, Alexi."

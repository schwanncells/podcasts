# Unchained — "Bitcoin Stalls, Stocks Soar: The Disconnect That Defines This Cycle"

**Host:** Steve Ehrlich (head of research at Sharplink; host of Bits + Bips). **Guest:** Steve Sosnick (chief strategist at Interactive Brokers; repeat guest; markets commentator specializing in cross-asset macro and derivatives).

## TL;DR

- **"Ratchet effect" driving equity gains** — stocks rally on every positive Iran/Strait of Hormuz headline but refuse to give back gains when deals fall through, pushing major indices near all-time highs despite unresolved Gulf tensions.
- **AI stocks alone are lifting the market** — equal-weighted S&P 500 is flat since February; semiconductor memory stocks like Micron have posted multi-hundred-percent moves while the broad market stagnates.
- **Dot-com parallel: bandwidth companies vs. AI infra** — Global Crossing, Lucent, WorldCom, and Northern Telecom proved bandwidth was necessary, yet most went bankrupt from misallocated capital; Sosnick argues the same dynamic threatens AI infrastructure spenders who can't all win.
- **Two specific vulnerabilities named** — (1) a single major hyperscaler (e.g., Alphabet or Microsoft) pausing AI capex would trigger a "screeching of the brakes"; (2) future AI winners likely include companies that don't yet exist, just as Google and Facebook postdated the dot-com boom.
- **$75–100B IPO supply wave coming** — SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic are expected to collectively bring that amount of new equity to market, threatening the favorable supply-demand dynamic that has supported prices.
- **Rate expectations flipped 180°** — from 62.5 basis points of cuts priced by December 2025 to ~18 basis points of rate hikes expected, with a full cut not priced until March 2026; Christopher Waller's pivot from dove to hawk drove the yield curve flat.
- **Kevin Warsh faces a skeptical Fed committee** — new chair inherited a fractured FOMC (four dissents at the last meeting); may try to switch the inflation benchmark from core PCE to a trimmed measure to show better progress.
- **Bitcoin losing momentum trade to AI** — implied volatility at a 9-month low, ETF inflows reversing as "crypto tourists" who bought on performance chase now rotate into semiconductor and AI names; non-interest-bearing asset backdrop with rising rates is an additional headwind.
- **Bitcoin miners pivoting to AI data centers** — companies like IREN and NBIS switched from mining to providing AI compute capacity; Roundhill Memory ETF (DRAM) launched and either surpassed or matched IBIT as the most successful ETF launch in history, depending on the metric.

---

**The Ratchet Effect and Geopolitical Risk**

Sosnick describes the past several weeks as a "ratchet effect" in equities: each positive story about resolving the Iran-Strait of Hormuz situation sparks a rally, but when deals fail to materialize, stocks retain their gains while oil and yields give back their moves. He notes that by the time of taping, an Axios report described a 60-day memorandum of understanding to extend the ceasefire, though the agreement left major details unresolved. The market reacted positively but with less enthusiasm than prior stories, which Sosnick read as either prices already reflecting a deal or simple trader exhaustion — two scenarios with opposite implications for further upside.

The Gulf situation complicates inflation: prolonged closure could push gasoline to $5-plus per gallon, and copper prices are already elevated from data center buildout demand. Core PCE came in at 3.3% (its highest in years) and headline at 3.8%, though the monthly core reading of +0.2% versus an expected +0.3% was described as a mild positive that barely moved stocks before the Axios story.

**Why Stocks Are Up Despite the Macro**

Sosnick credits two drivers: solid earnings with positive guidance — particularly in semiconductor memory where guidance he describes as "extreme" — and the ratchet effect. He cautions that the magnitude of the rally is questionable, noting Nvidia's stock declined slightly despite beating earnings expectations for roughly its 14th or 15th consecutive quarter, a "victim of its own success" where every incremental buyer already knows the story.

The equal-weighted S&P 500 being flat since February exposes the concentration: AI and semiconductor names are carrying the index while cyclicals lag. Goldman Sachs flagged historically elevated short interest in cyclicals, which could fuel a short-squeeze-driven broadening, but Sosnick notes the flipside is that margin debt as a percentage of market cap is also at a record peak — "dry powder of short covering" on one side, "record reliance on borrowed money" on the other, with no visible risk aversion in either camp.

**The Dot-Com Parallel**

Sosnick extends a parallel to the late 1990s bandwidth boom. Bandwidth proved genuinely necessary, and the internet changed the world beyond even the most optimistic forecasts — yet Global Crossing, Lucent, Northern Telecom, WorldCom, and others collapsed because capital was deployed faster than demand materialized. He argues AI infrastructure investment may follow the same arc: every company must "take the shot" (citing the Wayne Gretzky theory — you miss all the shots you don't take), but not all will deploy capital wisely, and future AI winners likely include companies that haven't been founded yet, just as neither Google nor Meta existed during the dot-com bubble.

The specific vulnerability he names: if Alphabet or Microsoft were to announce they would "sit tight with what we have and see if those investments pay off," that disclosure would trigger a sharp market correction. Until that happens, there is no visible catalyst to break the AI spending narrative.

**The IPO Supply Problem**

The approaching listings of SpaceX, OpenAI, and Anthropic — collectively estimated at $75–100 billion in new equity supply — threaten to reverse the favorable supply-demand dynamic that has supported prices. Sosnick notes that buybacks and private equity activity have reduced the float over the past cycle; a wave of large, not-yet-profitable issuers absorbing that much capital "at least in the short term" puts the supply-demand picture "into retrograde." He also points to $120 billion flowing into money market funds in a single month and repo rates falling below the bottom of the Fed's target range as signs of abundant cash, though he is skeptical of the "cash on the sidelines" argument: some of that money is structurally parked and won't rotate into equities.

**Kevin Warsh and the Fed**

Warsh took the seat vacated by Dr. Myron, who had dissented at every meeting in favor of lower rates. At the most recent meeting there were four total dissents: Myron for cuts, and three others advocating to remove the easing bias from the Fed's statement. Waller, previously one of the more dovish members, came out shortly before taping calling for the easing bias to be removed — a shift Sosnick declined to editorialize on.

The rate expectation swing is stark: from 62.5 basis points of cuts priced by December to approximately 18 basis points of hikes, with the first full cut not priced until March 2026 — "roughly 80 basis points and change at the front end of the curve" relative to prior expectations. Sosnick frames this as driven not by a stronger labor market picture but by the Gulf-related price pressures bleeding into core inflation, combined with labor market data solid enough (4.3–4.4% unemployment) to remove the dovish case from the employment side of the dual mandate.

Warsh's probable tactic, Sosnick suggests, is to shift the Fed's inflation benchmark from core PCE to a trimmed inflation measure, which would smooth out energy-driven spikes and allow him to show more progress. But convincing a fractured 12-member committee — "he's one of 12" — and navigating what Sosnick calls the "real-world tests" that markets historically throw at new Fed chairs means it "is not smooth sailing."

**Bitcoin and the Crypto Disconnect**

Bitcoin sat near $73,000 at the time of taping with implied volatility at a 9-month low and sparse positioning for convex moves in either direction. Sosnick attributes crypto's underperformance to momentum: overlapping investors who hold both crypto and tech are directing fresh capital toward AI and semiconductor names that have outperformed. He describes crypto as having lost its "sexy flavor of the month" status.

Two additional structural headwinds: (1) rising nominal and money market rates penalize non-interest-bearing assets; gold is experiencing the same effect. (2) ETF adoption brought "normies" into the asset class, which drove prices higher on the way in but creates a "crypto tourist" dynamic on the way out — performance chasers who bought ETFs will rotate when something else outperforms. Sosnick frames this as "not taking a view one way or another on crypto as a long-term investment" but notes money flows can't be dismissed.

Ehrlich adds that Bitcoin mining companies IREN and NBIS have largely converted their data centers to AI computing capacity, securing multi-year hyperscaler contracts rather than continuing to mine increasingly competitive Bitcoin. Both hosts note the Roundhill Memory ETF (ticker DRAM) either surpassed or matched IBIT as the most successful ETF launch in history — a data point Sosnick uses to illustrate where speculative momentum has rotated.

**What to Watch**

Sosnick identifies the peace deal scenario as the top near-term variable: if a deal materializes, it could be a sell-the-news event if already priced in, or further upside if market skepticism is what's keeping prices from ripping today — "two very divergent outcomes from the same potential piece of news." He also flags the yield curve flattening (Waller's speech pushed the back end down relative to the short end), the record margin debt reading, and the upcoming midterm elections — noting the only two down years in the past 10–12 (2018 and 2022) were both midterm years with new Fed chairs, advising against assuming "the path is ever upward."

**Rustin's Formative Philosophy**

Bayard Rustin's commitment to nonviolence stemmed from his Quaker grandmother, Julia Davis Rustin, who raised him and instilled Quaker values from childhood. Later, Rustin was profoundly influenced by Mahatma Gandhi's teachings. Rustin himself said: "My activism did not spring from being black. Rather, it is rooted fundamentally in my Quaker upbringing and the values instilled in me by my grandparents who reared me." He regarded Gandhi's achievement—tearing down an empire through peaceful protest and words alone—as revolutionary. This philosophy would guide Rustin's activism throughout his life. He believed that as long as activists adhered to nonviolence, they could not be destroyed.

**Proposing the March**

In the early 1960s, Rustin, A. Philip Randolph (a socialist labor rights leader), and other organizers developed a proposal for a massive march on Washington. Randolph believed that decent, well-paying jobs would lead to social and political freedom, especially for Black people. In January 1963, they conceived a two-day event: the first day would involve sit-ins at congressional offices of those opposed to civil rights legislation; the second would be a mass demonstration. The goals emphasized that "no worker in America is generally free" and demanded "a program of action in 1963 that will ensure the emancipation of all labor, regardless of color, race, or creed." Jobs and economic justice, not just civil rights, were central to the vision.

Randolph approved the plan, and they scheduled the march for August 1963—only months away. Rustin was tasked with setting up the logistics and engaging every major constituency: Catholic, Protestant, Jewish, intellectuals, labor movement, and civil rights groups. He assembled a team including Rochelle Horowitz, who traveled the country convincing people to participate, and other organizers who met with the Big Six civil rights leaders: A. Philip Randolph, Roy Wilkins (NAACP), Whitney Young (National Urban League), James Farmer (CORE), John Lewis (SNCC), and Martin Luther King Jr. (SCLC).

**Strategic Adjustments and Political Pressure**

As the march took shape, Rustin made strategic refinements. He revised the mission statement to emphasize two goals: freedom (racial justice) and jobs (economic opportunity). He also reduced the event from two days to one, calculating that President John F. Kennedy—who opposed the march and feared it might spark violence and damage the civil rights bill he was pushing—would be less resistant to a single-day event. The Big Six accepted these changes, though Roy Wilkins of the NAACP insisted that Randolph, not Rustin, be the public chair. Randolph agreed on the condition that he be allowed to name his deputy for day-to-day organizing, and he chose Rustin.

**The Smear Campaign and Randolph's Defense**

J. Edgar Hoover, the FBI director, orchestrated a smear campaign against Rustin. The FBI leaked information to segregationist Senator Strom Thurmond, attacking Rustin on the Senate floor as a "pervert and draft-dodger"—references to his homosexuality and his status as a conscientious objector. The attack threatened to force Rustin out again, as had happened eight years earlier when pressure on King about Rustin's sexuality led King to establish a committee exploring whether it was "dangerous" for him to continue working with Rustin. This time, however, A. Philip Randolph called a press conference and declared that Rustin would remain as deputy director and chief organizer, saying he had "full and complete confidence" in Rustin. Randolph's public support was a turning point: for the first time in Rustin's life, exposure didn't lead to exile.

**Organizing with Precision and Discipline**

Rustin's organizing team worked six days a week, using mimeograph machines and telephones to reach potential marchers. They faced both minor and substantive disagreements. A minor one came when organizer Bart announced that the National Council of Negro Women was preparing thousands of peanut butter and jelly sandwiches; Rochelle joked about the choice, and Bart angrily retorted that peanut butter "doesn't spoil." More serious was the absence of women speakers. Joyce Ladner noted that though women like Fannie Lou Hamer spoke articulately at movement events, none were scheduled to speak at the march—some preachers claimed it was bad luck for a woman to cross the pulpit.

Nonviolence, however, was non-negotiable. Rustin trained NYC Black policemen as marshals in nonviolent crowd control techniques, teaching them to hold hands and encircle people if disturbances arose. More militant figures, like Malcolm X, were uninvited. Rustin understood that perceptions mattered: the march had to be nonviolent, and its public face had to be non-threatening and inspirational. That face would be Martin Luther King Jr.

**The Day of the March**

Uncertainty prevailed until the last moment. Organizers didn't know how many people would attend. On the morning of August 28, 1963, reporters asked Rustin where the marchers were. Using a British accent, he pulled a blank piece of paper from his jacket and said, "Indeed, gentlemen, everything is on schedule." Within an hour, marchers began streaming into Washington, D.C. Musical performances opened the event—Bob Dylan, Joan Baez, and Peter, Paul, and Mary sang before the speeches began. John Lewis delivered remarks, though his original speech contained language about marching "through the South the way General Sherman did" if violence didn't stop. Organizers had called him out on this before the event, toning down his message to align with the leadership's nonviolence commitment.

An estimated 250,000 people—or about 200,000 by some accounts—gathered on the National Mall. Observers were ecstatic at the turnout. Joyce Ladner, viewing the crowd from a small Mississippi town representative perspective, felt "a sense of awe and pride." The march is now remembered as the moment when King delivered "I Have a Dream," but the infrastructure—the discipline, the logistics, the strategic vision, and the relentless nonviolence training—came from Bayard Rustin, standing a few feet to King's right, largely unseen.

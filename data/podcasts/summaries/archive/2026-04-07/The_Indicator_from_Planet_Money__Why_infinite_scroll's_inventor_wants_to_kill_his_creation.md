# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Why infinite scroll's inventor wants to kill his creation"

**Host:** Adrian Ma, Darian Boyd. **Guest:** Aza Raskin (entrepreneur, infinite scroll inventor; testified in Meta trial regarding addictive design practices).

## TL;DR

- **Aza Raskin invented infinite scroll in 2006** — designed as a more efficient interface, but social media companies exploited it to keep users endlessly engaged by removing stopping cues.
- **Jury verdicts hold Meta and Google liable for addictive design** — California jury awarded $6 million; New Mexico jury awarded $375 million in damages for knowingly designing apps harmful to children's mental health.
- **Infinite scroll removes the brain's natural stopping signal** — Raskin compares it to a wine glass that refills without the drinker noticing; users scroll much longer without conscious awareness they've reached a behavioral threshold.
- **Internal company memos prove intentional design** — executives and engineers knew exactly what they were doing and chose to prioritize engagement over user wellbeing because dropping engagement lowers stock prices and competitive standing.
- **Proposed fixes: add friction and remove infinite scroll** — Raskin suggests slower loading speeds (like poor airplane Wi-Fi), requiring clicks to load new pages, and disabling autoplaying videos as straightforward ways to reduce compulsive use.
- **Section 230 no longer shields companies on design claims** — plaintiffs focused lawsuits on app design rather than user-generated content, closing a legal loophole that previously protected platforms from liability.
- **Global policy shift: multiple countries banning social media for kids** — Indonesia implemented an official ban March 28th; India is contemplating one; Australia, Denmark, Spain, and France have also taken action, signaling a human movement against tech encroachment.
- **AI companies face the same incentive structure** — Raskin warns that the next phase is "the race to intimacy," where AI companies have massive market incentive to occupy children's most intimate relational space.

**The Invention and Unintended Consequences**

Aza Raskin developed infinite scroll in 2006 as a design innovation meant to improve user experience by efficiently loading more content as users reached the bottom of a feed. Raskin emphasizes he had good intentions and believed he was introducing "just a more efficient interface." However, he acknowledges being blind to how social media companies' financial incentives would weaponize the feature. As he states, "incentives eat intentions." Once Twitter, Google, and other platforms adopted infinite scroll, the technology became a primary tool for maximizing user engagement—not by adding value, but by removing the natural psychological stopping points that would normally cause people to leave the app.

Raskin uses the metaphor of a wine glass that refills without the drinker's awareness: your brain doesn't register that you've consumed more because it never sees the bottom of the glass. In infinite scroll, users never reach the end of the feed, so they never naturally disengage. The inventor admits he eventually had to write software to break his own addiction.

**The Addiction by Design Problem and Legal Evidence**

Raskin testified in the New Mexico trial that infinite scroll specifically "remove[s] a stopping cue, so you keep scrolling." What makes this more than a user preference problem is that it's not a fair fight: "When you open up Instagram or Facebook or YouTube, it's not just your mind trying to have willpower or control. On the other side of that screen are thousands of engineers who have done hundreds of millions of tests using your own psychology to keep you there."

The legal cases provide stark evidence that this design is deliberate. Internal memos and emails from executives and engineers show they "knew exactly what they were doing and chose to do it anyway." The motivation is straightforward: engagement-based business models mean any drop in engagement or users directly lowers stock prices, reduces executive bonuses, and allows competitors to outpace them. The choice to prioritize engagement over user wellbeing, particularly for children, was a calculated one.

**Legal Breakthroughs and Section 230's Limits**

Two recent jury verdicts represent the first major legal accountability for these design choices. A California jury awarded $6 million in damages; a New Mexico jury awarded $375 million. While these sums are modest relative to Meta and Google's annual profits, both companies say they disagree and will appeal. More significantly, there are thousands of similar lawsuits pending.

Raskin stresses the historical importance: "This is really the first time that these companies are being held accountable for the design decisions that they make." Previously, platforms relied on Section 230 of the Communications Decency Act, which shields them from liability for user-generated content. But the plaintiffs in these cases pivoted: instead of suing over content, they sued over the design of the apps themselves, arguing the companies knew their design choices were likely to cause harm and did it anyway. This move closed the legal shield.

Tech companies countered that they have safeguards in place, that no clinical diagnosis for "social media addiction" exists, and that mental health is complex and cannot be traced to a single app. The American Psychological Association does note that social media comes with both benefits and risks, but that "children and adolescents may be particularly vulnerable to the risks because their brains are still developing."

**Proposed Solutions: Adding Friction**

Raskin proposes a straightforward approach: introduce friction into the user experience. When Instagram loads slowly on airplane Wi-Fi, people disengage and do something else. Adding deliberate slowness—speed bumps on the road—doesn't remove freedom; it gives users time to think and pause. Other changes he suggests: remove infinite scroll entirely so users must click to load the next page, and disable autoplaying videos. Even these minimal tweaks, he argues, would substantially reduce compulsive use.

When pressed on the "nanny state" objection—that companies should be free to design products as they see fit—Raskin draws a parallel to seatbelts and vehicle safety regulations. Society has decided certain protections are necessary. The question is whether protecting children's attention and mental health from intentionally addictive design is a reasonable boundary.

**Global Policy Movement and the AI Frontier**

A significant shift is underway internationally. Indonesia officially banned social media for children on March 28th. India is contemplating a similar ban. Australia, Denmark, Spain, and France have also taken action. Raskin frames this as a broader "human movement where we are recognizing that technology is encroaching onto our humanity." Two years ago, such bans seemed impossible; today they're policy.

Raskin also extends the critique to AI companies, which face identical incentive structures. The next frontier, he argues, is "the race to intimacy"—companies want their AI systems to occupy the chief relational spot in users' lives, especially children's. Every moment someone spends with friends or in the outside world is a moment they're not interacting with the AI. Without similar accountability and friction mechanisms, the addiction problem will migrate to a new technology.

**Shifting Standards and What's Next**

Whether outright bans are the right solution is debatable, but Raskin sees the verdicts and policy shifts as signs that the era of unchecked tech influence is ending. The legal system, regulators, and the public are beginning to demand that platforms be held responsible not just for the content on their apps, but for the design choices that make those apps deliberately addictive. As these cases proceed through appeals and more lawsuits advance, the pressure on platforms to redesign—or on legislators to mandate changes—will likely intensify.

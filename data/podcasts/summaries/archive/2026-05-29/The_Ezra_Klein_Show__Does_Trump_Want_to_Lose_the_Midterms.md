# The Ezra Klein Show — "Does Trump Want to Lose the Midterms?"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Liam Donovan (Republican strategist, president of Targeted Victory, former National Republican Senatorial Committee staffer, contributor to The New York Times and The Washington Post).

## TL;DR

- **Trump is prioritizing party control over winning the midterms** — his approval is plus-21 net disapproval (more than twice as unpopular as at this point in his first term), yet he is endorsing scandal-plagued Ken Paxton over John Cornyn in Texas, helping primary out Thomas Massey, and creating a $1.8 billion slush fund for January 6 rioters rather than pivoting toward the center.
- **Trump's behavior suggests he fears a Republican Party with a spine more than Democratic victory** — a Democratic Congress gives him an enemy to fight and frees him from "the tedious work of trying to pass legislation," his stated preference being to claim persecution rather than wield power.
- **Republicans' map advantage is durable but Trump's interventions create real pickup opportunities for Democrats** — in Texas, Beto lost by 3.5 points in 2018 (a very good year for Democrats); if Republicans lose Texas, it likely cascades to the Senate majority given the unfavorable environment.
- **North Carolina and Maine are the gateway pickups for Democratic Senate gains** — North Carolina has Roy Cooper (strong candidate, open seat, fundraising advantage) and Maine has Graham Plattner (high-variance candidate gaining on Susan Collins in recent polls despite her incumbency advantage).
- **Young Republicans and old Republicans are splitting on Israel, Iran, and Gaza in ways both parties struggle to contain** — Thomas Massey lost his primary despite endorsements from Tucker Carlson and Candace Owens, and AIPAC spending against him; these issues are reshaping Republican and Democratic coalitions in ways that defy traditional party loyalty.
- **Attention economy is now eating the political economy** — candidates like James Tallarico (TikTok to Joe Rogan to Democratic Senate nominee) and Graham Plattner (destroying Janet Mills through media dominance) are defeating incumbents tuned for institutional gatekeeping, not online attention.
- **Trump has been unusually effective at directing Republican primary traffic in 2024-2026 compared to 2022** — his operation has learned to coordinate and identify viable candidates; the exceptions (Paxton in Texas, Massey in Kentucky) stand out precisely because they are exceptions, not the pattern.
- **Republicans lost Ohio and will lose it again if the Democratic wave is strong enough** — Sherrod Brown will return only if the environment is Dem +6 or +7; in a +2 or +3 environment (still good for Democrats), the Ohio Democratic brand is too damaged for him to overcome, even against John Husted (an appointed, offense-free incumbent).

**Trump's Unpopularity and Apparent Indifference to Winning**

Ezra Klein opens with a theory: Trump is not trying to win the midterms but rather to control the Republican Party. His approval rating sits under 40 percent and is plus-21 net disapproval—more than twice as unpopular as he was at the same point in his first term. Yet instead of doing the things a president worried about losing would do—moving to the center, defending vulnerable Republicans, supporting strong candidates—Trump is doing the opposite. He is announcing a $1.8 billion slush fund designed to pay out to January 6 rioters, endorsing the scandal-plagued Ken Paxton over John Cornyn in Texas, helping primary out Thomas Massey (the House Republican who released the Epstein files), and threatening to escalate the Iran War. When asked whether American financial situations motivated his Iran policy, Trump said "not even a little bit" and stated his only concern is preventing Iran from acquiring nuclear weapons.

Liam Donovan, a Republican strategist who has worked on Senate elections and the National Republican Senatorial Committee, agrees there is something to this theory but frames it differently. He notes that Trump's mythology of his first term—the reason he sees himself as having lost or been held back—was that he was restrained by the "deep state," "Never Trumpers," and Bush-era Republicans. By installing loyalists in his second term, Trump is "feeling for the first time what it looks like to get what you're asking for." The electorate that reelected him wanted to return to pre-COVID stability, not full ideological implementation. There is a fundamental disconnect: Trump is following his vision of what "America First" or "MAGA" looks like, not the electorate's.

**Trump's Relationship to the Institutional Republican Party**

Klein pushes on a core question: Does Trump want to control Congress or the Republican Party? Donovan acknowledges that Trump cares more about control and is more sensitive to the risk of losing it than four or eight years ago. However, Donovan also notes that Trump does not come from the institutional Republican Party and is fundamentally different from any president in either party in this respect. In 2018, Republicans had a rough midterm, but Trump came out the next day dunking on members who had opposed him. In 2022, Trump exerted a lot of control over Republican primaries, resulting in Blake Masters, Dr. Oz, and Carrie Lake—candidates who lost very winnable races. Right now, Trump is intervening in places like Texas in ways that create the possibility that Republicans will lose key races they could have otherwise won.

Donovan argues, however, that by 2024 and into 2026, Trump and his political operation have actually done quite a good job directing traffic and coordinating candidate selection. The exceptions—like Texas and Georgia—are more notable precisely because they are exceptions. In most competitive races, the Trump operation has cohered around candidates that don't look wildly out of step with their states.

**The Texas Senate Race and Trump's Leverage**

Klein and Donovan discuss Ken Paxton at length. Paxton is the sitting attorney general of Texas, elected statewide multiple times but carrying heavy baggage—impeachment efforts, legal troubles. He has prevailed partly by aligning himself with Trump and leading on initiatives Trump cares about. He "threw himself" into the race against Cornyn. Donovan had worked for Cornyn and describes him as a longtime incumbent from the George W. Bush / Rick Perry era of Texas Republicanism, which "is not necessarily the vanguard." The question Klein poses is: Why did Trump endorse Paxton rather than Cornyn, if Cornyn was not an anti-Trump Republican?

Donovan's answer: Trump wanted the Senate to do certain things (including nuking the filibuster for the Save America Act). When that didn't happen, it became clear Trump had no inclination to back Cornyn. Trump was using Cornyn as a bargaining chip. When Paxton entered anyway, it created "a really difficult dynamic" because Trump has people competing for his affections, which Trump "obviously likes a great deal." Donovan does not think Paxton loses the seat, but Beto lost by 3.5 points in 2018 (a very good Democratic year), and then Allred lost by about six in 2024. If Democrats have a plus-six or plus-eight night, Texas could flip. Klein notes that Paxton candidate odds have shifted from 75 percent (January) to 55 percent (now), according to Polymarket. If Tallarico wins, it could make Chuck Schumer majority leader, "and that's purely on Donald Trump's table."

**The Senate Map: Competitive States and Path to Democratic Gains**

The two map out the competitive Senate races:

**North Carolina** — Roy Cooper (former governor) is the Democrats' strongest candidate, open seat created by Tom Tillis's retirement (run out by Trump), and Democrats have a big resource advantage. Klein notes this is "a prime pickup opportunity," and if all things are equal, Democrats would prefer an incumbent to an open seat. However, given the dynamics with Tillis and a potential ugly primary, coalescing behind Michael Whatley (former RNC chair) has gone "as well as it could go," but it's still lopsided. Donovan calls it "the first one to flip" if there's a Democratic night.

**Maine** — Donovan notes Maine is the one state on the map that doesn't match the lean of the state at the presidential level. Susan Collins is a "survivor" who confounded expectations in 2020 when she was given no chance but Trump was on the ballot. The "unknowable binary" is whether Maine still rewards independent known quantities like Collins, or whether it would vote for Graham Plattner (the Democratic nominee). Recent polling has shown Plattner as more competitive against Collins than Mills was. Klein notes this is interesting because Plattner has "energy" while Mills ran a "somnambulant campaign" and was 77 years old. Donovan agrees but cautions that primary energy doesn't necessarily translate to general election performance.

**Ohio** — Sherrod Brown is attempting a comeback after losing to Bernie Moreno (who Donovan considers a weak candidate). Brown lost by 3.5 points but overperformed Kamala Harris significantly. However, the Democratic brand in Ohio is "such trash" that even a better candidate than Moreno (his opponent) may not be able to overcome it. Donovan notes that if the environment is Dem +6 or +7, maybe Brown wins. If it's +2 or +3, he probably can't. The race is a straightforward question: "How big is the Democratic wave?" and "How much has Trumpism cost Republicans in this year?"

**Alaska, Iowa, and beyond** — Donovan sees Alaska as a state with "enough variance" where Democrats have opportunity. Mary Peltola won in a special election using ranked-choice voting when Republicans were divided (Sarah Palin vs. another Republican). Donovan cautions that the conditions that benefited Peltola (Republican division) don't exist now. Iowa has an open seat (always harder to hold), and Democrats have a strong candidate in Ashley Henson. Michigan has a messy Democratic primary where Abdullah Syed (a more progressive candidate) is dividing the field with Mallory McMorrow and Haley Stevens, and Gaza has become a potent issue none of them have navigated effectively.

**Israel, Iran, and Gaza as Coalition Dividers**

The conversation turns to Thomas Massey's primary loss in Kentucky. Massey had been a Trump critic (though once a supporter) and was key in releasing the Epstein files. He lost to Ed Galrain, who was apparently in Tel Aviv when Massey tried to concede. AIPAC spent heavily against Massey, and he claimed the fights over Israel cost him the race. He did much better among young Republicans than older ones.

Klein and Donovan note a larger pattern: views on Israel, Palestine, Iran, and the war are "splitting both parties in complicated ways." Democrats are forming a consensus (e.g., Chris Van Hollen, Rahm Emanuel, AOC) around a new approach to Israel policy. Republicans, by contrast, are fracturing between Christian Zionists (like Huckabee) and skeptics like Tucker Carlson and Candace Owens, who endorse candidates like Massey. Klein argues this split is harder for Republicans to contain because there is no "pro-war with Iran" faction in the Democratic primary, but the Republican Party has irreconcilable views on whether Israel is great or has led America into a disastrous foreign policy.

Donovan agrees that the ugliness will happen but notes that figures like Massey, Marjorie Taylor Greene, and Tucker Carlson may not be running electoral plays at all. They are operating in the "attention economy" where "you can have your career as a podcaster" or media figure and don't necessarily aspire to higher office.

**Attention Economy Reshaping Politics**

Klein pushes on this: Is attention still an electoral play? He cites examples like Momdani defeating Cuomo and Lander, Graham Plattner destroying Janet Mills through media dominance, and James Tallarico coming from nowhere on TikTok and Joe Rogan to become a Democratic Senate nominee. "The attention economy is eating the political economy," Klein argues. Incumbents tuned for institutional gatekeeping are being defeated by candidates who dominate attention online.

Donovan agrees but qualifies: some of these figures (like Massey) just want to stir up trouble; Tucker Carlson is "toying with all kinds of things" and probably won't run for president. However, Donovan does acknowledge that Trump was "the first figure who did this in a way you couldn't before" and that the Republican Party's old version is not what voters wanted—it's not coming back, and the party needs to find something that acknowledges Trump's appeal without getting stuck solving "the problems of the 80s and 90s."

**Future Republican Leadership and the Succession Question**

When asked about Republican figures who represent an interesting future, Donovan mentions Rubio's "fascinating" transformation, J.D. Vance, and figures at the congressional level like Josh Hawley and Jim Banks who are "playing with" ideas and trying to "feel out" what can harness attention without fitting the orthodoxy of the old party. However, Donovan emphasizes that Trump's insight was that "so much of this isn't about policy. It's about attitude. It's about how you position yourself against the left." He notes a tendency to "over index to interesting political ideas" when what matters to primary electorates is attitude and opposition framing.

On how Republicans should run against Democrats, Donovan advises tying candidates to the national Democratic Party, which is seen as "weak and feckless" and tied to unpopular positions. He recommends using old clips and statements from 2020-2022, making Democratic candidates account for past positions, and "putting them on the defensive." The goal is to "mummify them" in voters' minds through compelling opposition messaging—good old-fashioned opposition research and ad-making.

**Book Recommendations**

Donovan recommends three books:
1. **The Right by Matt Cotten** — A history of the right over 100 years, showing that populist, anti-establishment, and conspiratorial currents are not new to Republicanism and reminding readers that history didn't start in 2016 or 1980.
2. **Apple in China by Patrick McGee** — On industrial policy, foreign policy, and national security implications of tech manufacturing.
3. **On energy dominance** — A book on how America went from scarcity thinking in the 2000s to becoming the Saudi Arabia of natural gas, a revolution that "didn't fully set in" in policy and politics.

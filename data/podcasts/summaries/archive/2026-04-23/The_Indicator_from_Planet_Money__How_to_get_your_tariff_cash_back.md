**The Supreme Court Ruling and the Refund Crisis**

The Trump administration set tariffs last year under the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEPA). The Supreme Court ruled that most of those tariffs were illegal, triggering an estimated $166 billion in refunds owed to businesses. Initially, government officials argued refunds would be difficult or impossible to execute. However, businesses pointed out that tariff refunds already happen through an existing system called ACE (a web portal where importers track customs duties and flag errors for review). After weeks of confusion, U.S. Customs announced it would use ACE to handle mass refund claims, creating a new tool within the system specifically for IEPA tariff claims: CAPE, short for Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries. CAPE opened for the first phase of claims on Monday, and the majority of imports qualified.

**Alfred Mai and the Smooth Filing Experience**

Alfred Mai runs ASM Games, a small California business that makes party card games like "Do You Really Know Your Family?" and "These Guards Will Get You Drunk." The games are manufactured in China, so Alfred pays tariffs on every import. Every penny he spent on tariffs last year was struck down by the Supreme Court. He had 17 shipments and claims the government owes him $162,000 in refunds, including interest.

Alfred was skeptical he'd ever see the money, but he spent considerable time preparing. He leaned on AI to help organize his paperwork and learned his way around the ACE portal despite confusing guidance on spreadsheet formats. When he filed his claim on Monday, clicking the upload button, the process took roughly five minutes. He remains cautious—skeptical that a Trump administration would comply with a court order—but so far found the system straightforward and simple.

**Rebecca Melsky and the Technical Barriers**

Rebecca Melsky runs Princess Awesome and Boy Wonder, a clothing brand, alongside one business partner in Washington, D.C. Unlike Alfred, Rebecca has relied on a customs broker to handle all tariff paperwork, so she never learned how the ACE portal works. Until last week, it wasn't clear whether her broker would handle the refund claim or if she'd have to file herself. Her broker and lawyer said she needs to file herself.

Rebecca's company manufactures clothing around the world, including in Asia and South America. The government owes her company approximately $32,000—meaningful for a very small operation, amounting to roughly 15% more than her expected production costs. To cope with tariff increases, she cut her own pay, raised prices on new designs by about 5%, and even installed a "tip jar" at checkout asking customers to help cover tariff costs. Customers were generous.

When trying to file her claim, Rebecca filled in her name and information, clicked submit by email, and nothing happened. She spent hours on hold with customs trying to fix technical errors on her account and struggled simply to log in to ACE. Her struggle reflects a broader challenge: many small business owners across the country are unprepared to file because they've never dealt with customs forms directly.

**Sarah Wells and the Caution Ahead**

Sarah Wells, from Virginia, makes backpacks and accessories for nursing moms. These products used to come from China but now come from Cambodia. Sarah was well-prepared to file her tariff refund claim and did so on time, but she is not celebrating yet. First, until she sees money in her bank account, "the chapter's not closed." Second, the government says refunds will take 60 to 90 days after a claim is accepted. Third, and most pressingly, Trump has already announced new batches of tariffs under different legal authorities.

The original illegal tariffs were under IEPA. Immediately after the Supreme Court struck them down, Trump announced new tariffs under the Trade Act of 1974, specifically sections 122 and 301. Sarah is already paying an additional 10% tariff under section 122 on top of existing costs. Section 301 tariffs could arrive as soon as July, with the rate still unknown. From Sarah's perspective, any refund she receives will likely be absorbed by the new tariffs, leaving her sitting on money that will go "right back into tariffs again."

# Cautionary Tales with Tim Harford — "The Sightseeing Flight and the Invisible Mountain"

**Host:** Tim Harford. **Guests:** None (narrative storytelling episode using voice actors).

## TL;DR

- **Air New Zealand Flight 901 crashed into Mount Erebus in November 1979** — 257 people died when the DC-10 struck the 12,000-foot active volcano during a sightseeing flight to Antarctica.
- **The night before the flight, Air New Zealand secretly changed Captain Jim Collins' waypoint coordinates** — the route had been corrected to fly 25 miles west over McMurdo Sound, but was silently switched back to a path directly over Erebus without telling Collins.
- **Collins' briefing notes — found intact and readable on the mountainside — were thrown away by Air New Zealand** — the first officer's notes were taken from his grieving widow the day after the crash and then "lost"; the CEO ordered a shredder run to destroy "surplus" documents.
- **The initial crash investigation blamed Collins for flying below the 16,000-foot minimum safe altitude** — but non-executive pilots testified that they had all been briefed that low-altitude sightseeing was permitted as long as they used McMurdo's radar, exactly as Collins had done.
- **Whiteout — a polar visual illusion that makes terrain invisible — was never explained to any Air New Zealand pilot** — senior pilot Gordon Vette discovered the phenomenon after the crash and described it as "like being inside a big milk bottle," where a pilot cannot distinguish flat water from rising hillside.
- **Passenger photos developed from undamaged cameras proved Collins was flying in clear visibility, not cloud** — the disputed cockpit voice recorder line was "this is Cape Bird" (a landmark on the route Collins believed he was flying), not "a bit thick here, Bert" as the Chief Inspector's transcript claimed — a case of confirmation bias by the investigator.
- **Royal Commissioner Judge Peter Marn concluded Collins deserved no blame** — the cause was Air New Zealand's failure to brief him about whiteout and its failure to disclose the changed waypoint coordinates; Marn called the company's testimony "an orchestrated litany of lies."
- **That phrase destroyed Marn** — Air New Zealand successfully challenged the report at the Privy Council for breaching natural justice; Marn resigned as a judge, his health declined, and he died at 62; New Zealand's government formally apologized for the airline's role only in 2019, 40 years after the crash.

**The Flight**

On November 28, 1979, Air New Zealand operated a sightseeing flight from Auckland to Antarctica and back. Captain Jim Collins — experienced, methodical, and thrilled to have made the roster for the first time — brought the DC-10 down through cloud over what he believed was McMurdo Sound, an inlet between the Antarctic mainland and Ross Island. He had coordinated with the American radar station at McMurdo, as briefed. The cockpit display showed him 26 miles north of the next waypoint. That figure made sense on the route he thought he was flying — a route over open water. But the coordinates had been changed. The display actually meant he was 26 miles north of a waypoint directly over Mount Erebus.

The aircraft struck Erebus at low altitude. All 257 people on board — passengers who had been sipping champagne and peering out windows at the ice — died. Among the dead was renowned Antarctic explorer Peter Mulgrew, who was on board as a celebrity narrator and admitted in the final minutes he couldn't identify where they were.

**Who Jim Collins Was**

Harford paints Collins as the kind of pilot who prepared carefully and followed procedures. The evening before the flight, he spread a large map on his kitchen table and drew out the route with his teenage daughters watching. His wife Maria waved him off the next morning with a reminder to pick up fish on the way home. When Air New Zealand called that evening to say the flight was overdue, they asked if she had another adult in the house before telling her why they were calling.

After the crash, some of Collins' friends among the senior executive pilots began distancing themselves, hinting that Jim had been "too low" and that this was "unlike him." One told Maria directly: "I won't be able to see you anymore. I've got to be with the company." One pilot who had taught Collins to fly refused to accept the framing: "This isn't Jim, Maria. This is not Jim's behavior. Something's wrong. I'm going to find out."

**The Official Blame and the Royal Commission**

The Chief Inspector of Air Accidents' report found Collins responsible: he had violated the 16,000-foot minimum safe altitude, and visibility was clearly poor (the cockpit voice recording, the report claimed, proved the crew couldn't see where they were). The government appointed Judge Peter Marn to run a Royal Commission. Marn expected to confirm the report. He recalled: "I presumed that after testing the evidence at first hand, I would see no difficulty in confirming the Chief Inspector's opinion."

The evidence unraveled quickly. Air New Zealand was a state-owned company, which meant that if the airline was at fault, the government faced compensation claims; if Collins was at fault, insurance would cover it. Marn was alert to this incentive from the start.

**The Waypoint Cover-Up**

The original Antarctic sightseeing route used a radio beacon near McMurdo as its final waypoint, which placed the flight path directly over Mount Erebus. Someone eventually noticed this was dangerous and changed the final waypoint 25 miles west, routing the flight down McMurdo Sound over open water. For over a year, pilots received this corrected route. The night before Collins' flight, the waypoint was silently moved back over Erebus.

Air New Zealand's lawyers told Marn this was irrelevant for two reasons: first, Collins had always been briefed that the route went over Erebus (the safe route, they claimed, had been a longstanding typographical error no one noticed); second, Collins could have cleared Erebus at 16,000 feet regardless. But non-executive pilots gave Marn a completely different account of their briefings. Marn noted diplomatically that "I could not help but be struck by the direct conflict of evidence which had emerged." He believed the non-executive pilots, and he believed Collins had plotted his route the night before based on the McMurdo Sound coordinates — the reason his daughters saw him drawing lines on a map was that he was planning a flight down open water.

Evidence that could have confirmed what Collins was told at his briefing — the handwritten notes in the ringbinder found intact on the mountainside — had been "damaged by kerosene" and thrown away by Air New Zealand staff. The first officer's notes were collected from his widow the day after the crash and subsequently lost. The CEO had ordered all documents gathered and surplus ones shredded, citing a need to prevent leaks.

One document that survived complicated the airline's position considerably: an enthusiastic article by the CEO of McDonnell Douglas — the manufacturer of the plane — describing in a trade magazine how he had flown low over McMurdo Sound on an Air New Zealand sightseeing flight. He sent a copy to Air New Zealand's CEO. Air New Zealand's marketing department then printed a million copies and mailed one to every household in New Zealand. The CEO testified under oath that he had never seen the article. Marn noted that, when pressed, "he gave no verbal answer. He simply turned towards me and spread his arms outwards in a despairing gesture."

**Whiteout: The Actual Cause**

Gordon Vette, the senior pilot who had taught Collins to fly, was determined to find out what had actually happened. He consulted experienced Antarctic flyers and discovered a phenomenon called whiteout: in overcast polar conditions, when snow-covered terrain and cloud are both white and light diffracts in a particular way, a pilot loses all ability to perceive depth or distance. One expert described it as "like being inside a big milk bottle." It comes on suddenly and a pilot may not realize anything is wrong until walking into a snowbank — or flying into a mountain.

No Air New Zealand pilot, including Vette himself, had ever been briefed on whiteout before flying to Antarctica. Vette shuddered to consider his own exposure: "If I had been in their position at that time, I would probably have been misled the same respect as they were, and now myself may well have crashed on Mount Erebus."

Vette flew an expert to Marn's commission at his own expense. Later, the Australian Air Force crew who gave Marn a lift home from Antarctica demonstrated whiteout directly: flying toward a snowy ridge with a distinctive black rocky outcrop at the top, they told Marn to raise his hand to cover the dark rock. The ridge instantly vanished into undifferentiated white. Marn thanked every member of that Australian crew by name and rank in his final report.

This explained the mystery of how Collins could have failed to see a 12,000-foot volcano in front of him. The cockpit voice recording captured Collins saying, "We're twenty-six miles north — we'll have to climb out of this." Harford and Vette interpret "out of this" not as cloud, but as that disorienting sense of visual blankness. Collins sensed something was wrong and instinctively wanted to climb. He was out of time.

**Confirmation Bias — Twice**

Passenger cameras recovered from the wreckage, their films intact and developable, showed no thick cloud around the aircraft. Collins had descended below cloud into clear conditions. This made Marn suspicious of the cockpit voice recording transcript, which attributed the line "a bit thick here, Ahbert" to a crew member two minutes before impact — evidence, the Chief Inspector said, that they knew they were in poor visibility.

Marn flew to the United States to have the recording analyzed by an expert. The line didn't sound like "a bit thick here, Ahbert." The expert believed he heard "this is Cape Bird." Marn then flew the same route to Antarctica; at the moment of the disputed line, he looked out of the cockpit and saw a cape. It was not Cape Bird — that landmark was on the flight path Collins believed he was following, 25 miles west. But this cape happened to look nearly identical to Cape Bird.

Harford identifies two layers of confirmation bias: Collins and the crew assumed they were over Cape Bird, so they saw Cape Bird; the Chief Inspector assumed they were flying blind in cloud, so he heard "a bit thick here, Ahbert."

**Marn's Findings and the Aftermath**

Marn concluded that Collins bore no responsibility for the crash. The causes were Air New Zealand's failure to disclose the changed waypoint coordinates and its failure to brief any Antarctic pilot about whiteout. He declared that he had been forced "reluctantly to say that I had to listen to an orchestrated litany of lies."

That phrase proved to be Marn's undoing. A Royal Commission's findings cannot be appealed, but Air New Zealand's lawyers found another avenue: they sued Marn for breaching natural justice by publicly accusing the company of a cover-up without giving it a right to respond. The Privy Council praised Marn's investigative work as "brilliant" but agreed that Air New Zealand had a legal point. Marn resigned from the bench. His health declined rapidly. He died soon after, aged 62. The legal challenge created enough ambiguity for Air New Zealand to evade accountability for years. New Zealand's government formally accepted Marn's report and apologized for the airline's role only in 2019, 40 years after the crash.

Gordon Vette, who had discovered the whiteout explanation and hoped the airline might acknowledge an honest mistake, was hounded out of his job. "I am somewhat sad," he said, "that the price I've had to pay for my attempts to find the truth has been much greater than I expected."

**What Can Be Learned**

Harford closes by noting that Marn's report was written before accident investigators had the vocabulary for concepts like cognitive biases and organizational human factors — ideas later articulated by psychologist James Reason. Reason himself said the Marn Report was "ten years ahead of its time." Marn and Vette had independently grasped what was then a new idea: that organizational failures set traps into which skilled people inevitably fall. Blaming the individual at the end of the chain misses the point entirely.

Harford draws two final lessons, one from each hero of the Erebus affair. From Gordon Vette: seeking the truth can make you powerful enemies. From Peter Marn: "tempting as it is to speak the truth, sometimes it's wiser to let the truth speak for itself."

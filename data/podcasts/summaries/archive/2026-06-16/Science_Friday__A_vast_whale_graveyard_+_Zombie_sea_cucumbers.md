# Science Friday — "A vast whale graveyard + Zombie sea cucumbers"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guests:** Nick Pienzen (curator of fossil marine mammals, Smithsonian National Museum of Natural History); Rachel Sippler (senior research scientist, Bigelow Laboratory for Ocean Sciences, East Boothbay, Maine); Sarah Jobson (PhD student, Memorial University of Newfoundland, St. John's, Canada).

## TL;DR

- **Whale necropolis stretches 745 miles in the Indian Ocean** — researchers conducted at least 32 dives to document a megasite containing whale remains dating back over 5 million years, with at least five active whale fall sites still teeming with life.
- **Megasite density unprecedented** — the researchers called it a necropolis because it represents "a density that we have not seen anywhere else in the world," with skeleton after skeleton lining the seafloor along whale migration superhighways.
- **Whale superhighways guide migration across oceans** — whales follow preferred corridors for feeding, mating, and migration, and researchers expected to find their remains concentrated on the seafloor beneath these routes.
- **Mostly beaked whales in the fossil record** — beaked whales dominate the site, yet scientists "hardly know anything about" this most species-rich group of whales, making the graveyard a window into poorly understood animals.
- **Sea cucumber body parts live independently for years** — researchers observed what they called "zombie sea cucumber parts" surviving at least three years after detachment, including tube feet and tentacles that restructure internally and respond to their environment.
- **Detached tentacles continue feeding behavior** — zombie tentacles move around, respond to touch by retracting as if avoiding predators, and show neural function; tube feet remain stationary but restructure into spherical shapes with red pigmentation concentrated in the center.
- **Immune system sustains severed parts** — immune cells (coelomocytes) migrate to wound sites during healing and appear to prevent bacterial invasion; tube feet also absorb amino acids directly without needing a digestive tract or mouth.
- **Zombie parts don't regenerate into whole animals** — unlike fragmentation in other marine species, these detached parts simply find a way to survive in their new state rather than cloning or regrowing into new sea cucumbers.

**A Whale Superhighway Underwater**

Nick Pienzen explains that whales prefer certain migration corridors across the world's oceans, moving between feeding grounds and mating territories. To illustrate the scale of the discovery, he compares the necropolis to driving from New York to Chicago and finding whale bones littered across the entire highway. The remains span two categories: ancient fossils, and active whale falls—recent whale carcasses that form entire ecosystems. The researchers found baling whales and beaked whales at active fall sites, though fossil sites consist mostly of beaked whales. Pienzen notes that the site accumulates skeletal material over hundreds of thousands of years—some bones on the seafloor have persisted for the entirety of human evolutionary history.

**Understanding the Necropolis**

Pienzen clarifies that while "necropolis" implies human intent to concentrate remains, what researchers are actually seeing is a seafloor exposure where skeletons accumulate naturally over geological time, similar to tar pits or cave sites. This is still happening today; the five active whale fall sites show that whales continue falling and being colonized by organisms that depend on the nutrients from their bones. The word "necropolis" was chosen by researchers, though Pienzen admits it raises philosophical questions about how such sites form naturally. The graveyard also likely contains shark teeth and smaller whale fragments not easily visible from submersibles.

**Why Beaked Whales Matter**

Despite being the most species-rich group of whales on the planet, beaked whales remain poorly understood. Pienzen emphasizes that if you want to find scientific surprises, "the seafloor is where it's at," and this discovery opens a new category on paleontologists' wish lists—the chance to document actual remains of whale migration superhighways beneath the ocean surface.

**The Accidental Discovery of Zombie Sea Cucumbers**

Rachel Sippler credits Emmy Montgomery, a graduate student who first observed feet remaining in their tanks long after detaching from the organism. Normally, marine bacterial degradation should destroy detached parts within days or weeks, yet these feet persisted for months. This puzzlement prompted investigation into what factors allow tube feet to survive under intense bacterial pressure. The discovery was entirely serendipitous observation.

**What Zombie Parts Can Do**

Sarah Jobson describes how the behavior differs by body part. Tube feet remain stationary but restructure and reform slightly. Tentacles, when healing in natural seawater, continue moving around and responding to their environment—they appeared to try feeding, and when poked or disturbed, they retracted as if responding to predator pressure. This behavior suggested ongoing neural function, reminiscent of the detached hand in the Addams Family. Over months and years, tube feet transform dramatically: they grow slightly larger, restructure into perfect spheres, become almost transparent at the edges, and concentrate red pigmentation into a scarlet nucleus at the center.

**The Gray Zone Between Life and Death**

Jobson emphasizes that these zombie parts defy standard categories. They demonstrate "key fundamental processes that keep tissues alive," yet they don't reproduce—something expected of living organisms. They exist in a biological gray zone. The parts don't regenerate into whole new sea cucumbers as seen in some marine species; instead, they simply find a way to survive as a new biological unit in their current state. This challenges conventional understanding of what organisms need to be alive.

**How Severed Parts Stay Alive**

Rachel Sippler used histology to examine internal tissue organization and found that immune cells (coelomocytes) are still present and migrate through tissues to wound sites. Early in wound healing and regeneration, immune cells clean degrading tissue and appear to prevent bacterial invasion. In a separate experiment, when researchers added supplemental amino acids to the water, tube feet were able to absorb these nutrients directly without needing a digestive tract or mouth. This suggests that even without traditional feeding, severed parts can sustain themselves through nutrient absorption.

**Practical Applications and Future Research**

Sippler notes multiple research directions benefiting from this discovery: healing and regeneration of damaged tissues, tissue aging and longevity studies, tissue engineering to keep tissues stable outside the body, and new models for studying immune systems. Unlike mammalian cell research with its biosecurity and access restrictions, sea cucumber parts offer an ethical, more accessible research model. Jobson plans to continue investigating the evolutionary purpose of this adaptation, questioning what environmental drivers maintain this seemingly nonproductive regeneration. Lab staff monitor the zombie parts continuously, with some specimens already surviving over three years.

**The Value of the "Zombie" Term**

Sippler emphasizes that using the term "zombie sea cucumber parts" helps communicate what's happening. It brings life and reality to the research, allowing scientific discovery to connect with broader understanding. The visceral imagery—dismembered parts, the Addams Family reference, the 2026 zombie zeitgeist—makes the phenomenon memorable and accessible.

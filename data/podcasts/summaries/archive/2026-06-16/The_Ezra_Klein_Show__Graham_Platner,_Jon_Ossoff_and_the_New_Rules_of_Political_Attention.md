# The Ezra Klein Show — "Graham Platner, Jon Ossoff and the New Rules of Political Attention"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guests:** Chris Hayes (host of "All In with Chris Hayes," MSNBC; author of *The Sirens' Call: How Attention Became the World's Most Endangered Resource*; former senior political correspondent).

## TL;DR

- **Attention is now the primary currency of politics** — Graham Platner, an unknown oyster farmer, was "cast" by a progressive group to run for Maine governor by analyzing thousands of candidates' donations, occupations, and charisma rather than institutional credentials, and he defeated the sitting governor through sheer attentional magnetism.
- **Charisma has replaced money as the primary advantage** — candidates like Platner and James Telerico succeed by grabbing and holding attention directly (via social media, podcasts, viral moments), whereas the old formula was: raise maximum money, buy TV ads. As broadcast TV declines, you need an alternate theory of attention.
- **Abdul Eliade's rise in Michigan was driven entirely by Gaza and Israel** — Once Hasan Piker's endorsement made Gaza/Israel the central issue, other candidates attacked, intensifying focus on that "attentional superconductor." Eliade's position aligned with progressive voters; the issue drowned out his original agenda (Medicare for all).
- **James Telerico offers a non-populist pathway to breaking through** — Unlike Platner, Telerico has mainstream Democratic credentials (Teach for America, college Dems president), no anti-institutional anger, and instead leverages Christian moral rhetoric and pastoral charisma on mainstream platforms (Joe Rogan). He appeals to voters yearning for decency, not just radicalism.
- **Jon Ossoff is threading the needle on Israel/Gaza via Jewish identity and corruption critique** — As a Jewish senator and Gaza critic who signed Bernie Sanders' letter against additional military aid, he avoids the anti-Semitism trap; he centers a systemic corruption story (not just Gaza) that applies equally to Trump and the Democratic establishment, and he has practiced this message exhaustively in Georgia swingable electorates.
- **Visual branding and authenticity on video platforms matter enormously** — Ossoff uses a "hero shot" (3/4-angle, upward gaze) borrowed from Obama; Spencer Pratt's LA mayoral campaign succeeded on X/Twitter but failed at the ballot despite record-setting online attention, showing that platform hermeticity (especially post-Elon) doesn't translate to real votes.
- **Gavin Newsom is synthesizing contradictory appeals** — He goes everywhere (Ben Shapiro, Michael Savage, Charlie Kirk, CAP Ideas, his own podcast), saying "yes to everything," getting better reps in more rooms, and implicitly offering a vision where partisan fights can be productive rather than reconciliatory—fight with me but we stay in relationship.

**The Attention Substrate and the Death of the Broadcast Formula**

Ezra Klein and Chris Hayes establish that attention—not money, not institutional success, not credentials—is now the primary determinant of electoral breakthrough. For decades, the formula was deterministic: raise maximum funds, buy television ads, win through saturation. This formula worked because broadcast TV reached everyone and scarcity of attention was not the binding constraint. As television audiences fragment and decline, candidates need a theory of how to capture attention without buying it. Graham Platner's case illustrates the shift: he was not recruited from existing Maine power brokers or through the Democratic establishment's conventional networks. Instead, a progressive group (working somewhat like a Hollywood casting director) analyzed thousands of candidate profiles—occupational records, FEC donation history, charisma signals—to find someone capable of *earning* attention rather than *buying* it. They found an oyster farmer with genuine political anger, authentic working-class distance from institutions, and raw charisma. Platner then defeated a sitting governor (Janet Mills) who was the establishment's preferred Senate candidate, despite Mills' institutional advantages, because Platner's charisma-driven campaign generated attention that cascaded across all media: social, cable, newspapers, podcast appearances.

Hayes and Klein explore the relationship between institutional success and attentional success as potentially inverse. Institutions (like the Democratic Party) historically recruited candidates who succeeded *within* those institutions: lawyers, hospital administrators, people with rising resumes, people who "don't make waves." But the personality type that succeeds in institutions—the "company man" who conforms, doesn't rock the boat—is often the opposite of the personality that grabs attention in 2026. Platner's authenticity comes from genuine institutional failure and genuine anger at the system. He didn't have to perform disillusionment; he lived it. Klein and Hayes acknowledge Platner's liabilities—a Nazi-tone tattoo he claims he didn't understand, texting women outside his marriage, some edgelord Reddit posts—but conclude the core risk isn't any single scandal but a pattern of poor judgment and underformation. However, they also note the Democratic establishment's 2020 attempt with a conventionally qualified female candidate lost badly to Collins, so the party was facing political necessity regardless.

**Abdul Eliade and the Attentional Superconductor of Gaza**

In Michigan's open Democratic Senate primary, a different dynamic is at play. Abdul Eliade—a Rhodes Scholar, MD/PhD, former Detroit public health official, Crooked Media podcast host—should embody institutional success. Yet his breakthrough came not through those credentials but through Gaza. Once Hasan Piker (a controversial Twitch streamer) came to campaign for Eliade, other candidates attacked both Piker and Eliade's positions on Israel, centering Gaza as the issue. Klein describes Gaza as an "attentional superconductor"—it has no peer in American politics except Trump himself for the amount of attention it commands. For an engaged Democratic primary electorate, Gaza has become the filter through which other issues are viewed. Eliade, who runs as a populist but who is institutionally embedded (Columbia professor, meritocratic prizes), found that Gaza and Palestine became his defining issue, not Medicare for all (his original signature cause) or his public health platform. The issue shifted not because Eliade changed but because other candidates weaponized it, and the attentional field tilted.

Hayes and Klein note the political danger in conflating opposition to Israeli policy with anti-Semitism, observing that young American Jews increasingly support a one-state solution and that many Jewish progressives are Gaza critics. Eliade's response to the question—distinguishing Judaism from Zionism, drawing a parallel to his opposition to Egypt's government despite his Egyptian heritage, emphasizing his care for both Jewish and Palestinian children—is framed as sophisticated by Hayes. The lesson: Gaza has become an issue that can define insurgent candidates (Eliade is not truly anti-institutional but has emerged as populist-coded through the issue) in ways previous issues could not.

**James Telerico: Decency as Attentional Pathway**

Unlike Platner (anti-institutional anger) or Eliade (institutional but issue-captured), James Telerico represents a third pathway: charisma rooted in authentic decency and Christian moral teaching. Telerico, a former college Democrats president and Teach for America alumnus, is institutionally formed in mainstream ways. Yet he breaks through via pastoral charisma, religious rhetoric, a visually distinctive social media strategy, and appearance on mainstream platforms like Joe Rogan—where he appeals not to progressives angered at the system but to voters yearning for what Klein calls "public virtue." In an era where Trump is "a moral degenerate" and where institutional Democrats have struggled to offer a moral register beyond opposition, Telerico offers something different: not "you should be angry at the system" but "I embody decency and connection."

Telerico beat Jasmine Crockett, a major progressive media figure, in the Texas Democratic primary for Senate. Hayes and Klein discuss the weakness the Paxton campaign (Texas's Republican nominee) has tried to exploit: suggesting Telerico is weak ("soft") because he is humble, has a vegan girlfriend, and speaks of his own limitations. Yet Klein notes that the anti-Telerico attacks actually prove Telerico's framing—they are cruelty and schoolyard bullying that contrast with his offered vision. Hayes adds that Telerico's appearance is distinctively pastoral: "you could cast him as the idealistic young pastor rooting out corruption." This matters because it signals something true about him. The visual grammar of platforms matters: platforms sniff out inauthenticity in a way print journalism or Meet the Press did not.

**Jon Ossoff: Threading the Israel/Gaza Needle**

John Ossoff, a young Jewish senator from Georgia, has emerged as a 2028 dark horse. He is controlled with media (not playing a "volume game," rarely doing podcast interviews), visually distinctive (using Obama-style hero shots), and most importantly, positioned to navigate a major party schism. Gaza and Israel have fractured the Democratic coalition between those who oppose Israeli policy and those who see criticism of Israel as conflating with anti-Semitism. Ossoff, as a Jewish Gaza critic who signed onto Bernie Sanders' letter opposing additional military aid to Israel, is uniquely positioned to represent both sides—or at least to move the conversation from "are you pro-Israel or pro-Palestine" to "are you corrupt or anti-corrupt."

Klein emphasizes Ossoff's approach: he centers corruption—of the political system, including both Trump and the Democratic establishment—rather than leading with Israel/Gaza. Like Obama (whom he visually evokes), Ossoff includes the opposing argument within his own argument: "people say [X], and they're right in this way, but the real problem is [larger systemic corruption]." This moves Gaza from the center to one symptom of a diseased system. Klein notes that Ossoff has gotten his "reps" in Georgia, a swing state, meaning he has practiced these messages with actual voters who are skeptical of him, cross-pressured between Trump and Democratic appeals, and diverse in political opinion. This ground-game feedback has educated him in a way that candidates competing nationally do not get.

**Gavin Newsom's Omnipresence Strategy**

Gavin Newsom, the California governor, is pursuing what Hayes calls the "contradictory synthesis": he is a fighter ("I will rip their throats out for you") *and* someone who fights in public with people he disagrees with (Ben Shapiro, Michael Savage) while remaining in relationship with them. This is different from the post-Obama Democratic tradition of seeking unity through transcendence of difference; instead, Newsom is modeling that fights can be productive and that disagreement need not break relationship. Hayes notes Newsom gets better with each appearance, learning from each "room" (each interview, each audience), much like a comedian learning their craft. He has chosen "omnipresence": he says yes to everything, he hosts a podcast, he appears on right-wing and left-wing outlets, he gets more and more comfortable. Klein is slightly skeptical that Newsom has fully synthesized the contradiction (the "repairer of the breach" rhetoric falls flat because it's unclear how he repairs), but Klein acknowledges the strategy is interesting and sets Newsom apart from other potential 2028 candidates.

**Spencer Pratt's Failure and the Limits of Attention**

Spencer Pratt, a reality television star, ran for Los Angeles mayor in 2026 on an essentially attentional campaign: constant ads on X (the platform formerly Twitter), high visibility, record-breaking spending for an LA mayor's race. Yet he failed badly, underperforming Donald Trump's 2020 performance in the city. Hayes and Klein attribute this to the fundamental hermeticity of X under Elon Musk: the platform has become a "hot house of insanity" that appears normal only to those inside it. His campaign was extremely successful *on X*—clips, memes, constant engagement—but this success did not translate to votes. The lesson: attention is necessary but not sufficient. You still need a reason to exist as a candidate, a theory of governance, a constituency that extends beyond the platform. Pratt had charisma on X; he had no argument. This contrasts with Platner (genuine working-class anger), Telerico (decency + pastoral rhetoric), Ossoff (corruption + Jewish identity threading), or Eliade (institutional expertise + populist framing on Gaza). Pratt had attention and nothing else.

**The Two Traditions: Fighting vs. Uniting**

In closing, Klein notes that most 2028 Democratic candidates are competing to be "the fighter"—the one who will "rip their throats out" for the base. The tradition they are abandoning is the inspirational register: unity, connection, belief that we can live together despite differences. Obama mastered both; Biden tried and failed; now most candidates (Newsom somewhat excepted) are retreating to the fighter role. This may reflect the experience of Trump and the collapse of the democratic norms Obama appealed to in 2008. But Klein suggests the yearning for connection and decency—what Telerico offers—may be deeper than the hunger for a fighter.

**Recommended Reading**

Hayes recommends Ben Lerner's recent novel *Transcription*; Mario Puzo's original *Godfather* novel (which he praises as more faithful to the Coppola film than he expected and containing remarkable prose despite misogyny); and Courtney Moen's *Allan Opts Out*, a dark comedy about a Greenwich advertising executive who moves into a backyard playhouse. Hayes notes Lerner's book had particular potency because Hayes attended Brown, where Lerner's narrator also studied.

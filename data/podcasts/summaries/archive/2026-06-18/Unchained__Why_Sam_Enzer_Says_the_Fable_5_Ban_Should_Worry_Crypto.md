# Unchained — "Why Sam Enzer Says the Fable 5 Ban Should Worry Crypto"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guests:** Sam Enzer (digital assets/crypto legal expertise; previously navigated regulatory conflicts including Biden-era crypto crackdowns); Austin Campbell, Roman Aluwalia, Chris Perkins (hosts of Dips and Dips podcast).

## TL;DR

- **Fable 5 and Mythos 5 disabled worldwide** — US Commerce Department issued an export control directive citing national security concerns related to a jailbreak vulnerability, forcing Anthropic to disable both models globally within hours of the 5:21 p.m. Friday directive.
- **Anthropic disputes the safety basis** — the company stated the jailbreak found "minor vulnerabilities" also discoverable in OpenAI's GPT-5.5 and argued that disabling a commercial model "would essentially halt all new model deployment for all frontier model providers."
- **Process and due process violations** — the enforcement directive was not publicly released; the public has only Anthropic's description of the government's concerns, no explanation of statutory authority, and no notice-and-comment rulemaking, violating the "bare bones of democracy."
- **Export control precedent threatens DeFi and APIs** — if export controls can target APIs, the government could theoretically regulate any US-based server, front end, or DeFi protocol; permissionless protocols previously escaped export regulation due to publication exemptions, but the precedent is unclear.
- **Consolidation risk and regulatory capture** — government overregulation historically drives consolidation, boxing out entrepreneurs and favoring big tech incumbents; crypto already sees this pattern with regulatory moats; decentralized AI is difficult and usually has a centralized chokepoint somewhere.
- **AI becoming a political weapon** — Hillary Clinton cited AI as a major concern, signaling Democratic backlash; Anthropic is betting on a change in the midterms; national security will "always Trump responsible innovation" and can become a lazy policy tool to target emerging tech without coherent limiting principles.

**The Fable 5 Export Control Directive**

The US government suspended Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5 models via an export control directive after learning of a jailbreak technique, according to statements attributed to David Sax of the administration. Anthropic received the directive at 5:21 p.m. Eastern and was forced to immediately disable both models worldwide for all customers, including foreign nationals employed by Anthropic. The administration argued that because Fable is Mythos with guardrails, a jailbreak exposes the underlying model's advanced cyber capabilities to unauthorized users; the administration requested Anthropic either fix the jailbreak or de-deploy the model. Anthropic refused, prioritizing continued commercial availability. Anthropic characterizes the vulnerability as "a small number of previously known minor vulnerabilities" discoverable in other publicly available models, including OpenAI's GPT-5.5, and contends that imposing such a recall standard "would essentially halt all new model deployment for all frontier model providers."

**Process Failures and Constitutional Concerns**

Sam Enzer raises critical procedural violations: the Commerce Department letter has not been publicly released, so neither Anthropic nor the public understands what the substantive national security concerns actually are, what statutory or regulatory authority was cited, or whether robust due process was followed. He notes that well-established First Amendment case law establishes source code and software—including encryption software—as protected speech. The directive bypasses notice-and-comment rulemaking, the "bare bones of democracy," leaving the framework for how export regulations apply to AI models entirely undefined. President Trump rescinded the Biden administration's AI diffusion framework without establishing a replacement, leaving the rules of the road unclear to companies trying to comply. Enzer contrasts this with the Trump administration's complaint about the Biden era: regulation by enforcement rather than by clear guidance. The lack of transparency parallels secret FISA court proceedings, where the public has no way to assess whether government actions were "completely valid and reasonable or complete nonsense."

**Export Controls, APIs, and Regulatory Scope**

The application of export controls to APIs raises a fundamental question: if access to a US API by a foreigner constitutes an export, then export power could theoretically be used against any US-based server, API, or technology. Historically, publication exemptions in export regulations have allowed permissionless DeFi protocols to escape export restrictions—because they're public code, they fall outside export control. However, the Fable 5 directive creates ambiguity about whether APIs, front ends, or DeFi protocols relying on underlying AI models like Fable can still claim those exemptions. This threatens the entire crypto infrastructure if export controls become a tool to regulate AI integration in blockchain systems.

**National Security as a Political Tool**

Chris Perkins observes that national security concerns are "not invalid" but raises the critical question: where is the limiting principle? He notes that national security "will always Trump responsible innovation" and has historically been used to justify overreach—Operation Choke Point during the Biden administration is cited as precedent. He expresses concern that while real national security threats exist, the argument structure is forming a playbook for future misuse, allowing any frontier development with potential cybersecurity implications to be labeled a national security risk. Perkins recalls the original encryption wars and Jean Hoffmann carrying printed code to circumvent export restrictions, noting these same principles are resurfacing two or three decades later.

**Consolidation, Regulatory Capture, and AI Development**

Regulation historically leads to consolidation because compliance costs are high, boxing out entrepreneurs and favoring well-capitalized incumbents. This pattern is already visible in crypto, where regulatory moats protect big players. If AI development faces similar pressures, decentralization becomes an attractive alternative—but truly decentralized AI is "very, very hard, unproven," and "there are parts of the stack that you can decentralize. But generally, you always have somebody centralized no matter what." One speaker proposes an "AI mullet" where decentralized inference runs on the back end while some centralized element remains, though the architecture is speculative. The broader concern is that overregulation will drive AI development offshore, particularly to China, which is producing "very sophisticated models, very cheap models."

**Comparing to Y2K and Market-Driven Solutions**

Enzer argues that market mechanisms—not government mandates—solved the Y2K problem: private companies identified the issue, invested in technology, and solved it without national security directives. The same principle should apply to AI security. If the government's concern is genuine, disabling Anthropic's model doesn't actually prevent malicious actors from using Mythos or Fable if they obtained it during the public availability window; the directive only prevents the *market* from keeping pace and developing defenses. One speaker emphasizes the distinction between technology and its users: "It's not the tech that does anything. It's the users. It's the people. It's the institutions and it's the governments that use it." Regulation should target bad actors and behaviors, not the technology itself—the analogy given is garage door openers, which can be weaponized as IED triggers but shouldn't be banned for that reason.

**Political Headwinds and Election-Year Posturing**

Hillary Clinton's public statement that AI "keeps me awake at night," combined with Democratic emphasis on "moderation" while simultaneously expressing AI concerns, signals that AI is becoming a political issue in advance of midterms. Anthropic appears to be betting on a change in political control in the midterms, and some analysis suggests it has ex-Biden staffers on board. One speaker notes the irony that the current Trump administration, despite being "a huge friend to the digital asset sector and to the AI industry," is the one imposing these controls—suggesting the action may be tied to pressure from specific actors (Amazon's CEO is mentioned in press reports as having raised the jailbreak concern) rather than ideological opposition. The broader pattern is that AI, like crypto before it, is becoming a convenient political target, especially when the substantive limiting principles and governance frameworks remain undefined.

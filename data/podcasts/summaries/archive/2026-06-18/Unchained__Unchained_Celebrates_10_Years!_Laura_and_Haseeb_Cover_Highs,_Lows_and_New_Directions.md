# Unchained — "Unchained Celebrates 10 Years! Laura and Haseeb Cover Highs, Lows and New Directions"

**Host:** Laura Shin (founder, Unchained; journalist covering crypto). **Guest:** Haseeb Qureshi (managing partner, Dragonfly Capital; author, host of The Chopping Block; former effective altruist and donor at Jane Street).

The real issue was SBF's delusions of grandeur enabled by his wealth and power.
- **Laura misunderstood effective altruism as "giving yourself a license to play God"** — she conflated the philosophy with SBF's behavior but acknowledges Haseeb's point that most effective altruists simply earn money and donate it without controlling institutions.

**Unchained's Origin and Early Survival**

Laura started the podcast in 2016 on Necker Island after being invited by BitFury to a gathering with Bill Tai, Vinny Lingham, and Katie Haun. She recorded her first two episodes (Matt Roszak and Elizabeth Rossiello) on the island, using a Yeti microphone that was sensitive enough that she became paranoid about noise. After Forbes dropped its 12-podcast experiment after the first season, Laura was "practically begging" sponsors. She had never sold anything before. Onramp, a company doing websites and marketing for crypto startups, gave her a full-year sponsorship in 2017, precisely when the crypto industry was exploding—"really great bang for their buck," as Laura notes.

Laura's angle was neither purely technical (like Epicenter) nor talk-radio style (like Let's Talk Bitcoin), but rather "for people like me"—everyday people interested in crypto without a technical background. It was reactive positioning, not a grand vision.

**The Podcast Landscape Shift**

Haseeb observes that Unchained is "the only one still going" from the original 2016 wave of crypto podcasts. Laura credits her ability to evolve with the industry and maintain independence. She reflects that independent journalism via Substack and citizen media represents "so much better for humanity" compared to legacy gatekeeping, which she now sees as corrupt and self-interested.

**SBF and Regret**

Laura's biggest regret involves SBF. After FTX collapsed, she wanted to interview him but hesitated because she was simultaneously writing a book proposal about FTX. She feared antagonizing SBF would close off sources. In retrospect, she knows exactly what her first question would have been: "Describe to me the cold storage setup at FTX." The answer would have revealed the gaping security negligence that distinguished FTX from legitimate exchanges—all of which implemented proper cold storage after Mount Gox. From the trial record, FTX had a backdoor from day one, treating customer funds like a personal slush fund rather than a vault.

Haseeb frames effective altruism itself as separate from SBF's misconduct. He met SBF in 2015 at an EA Global conference, when SBF was donating $150,000 (a substantial sum for the EA community at that time) and working at Jane Street. Haseeb saw him as a typical EA donor—nerdy, on the spectrum. Haseeb pushes back on the idea that effective altruism encourages playing God. The philosophy's core is straightforward: use your resources (money, time, energy) for maximum impact. It says nothing about controlling governments, institutions, or corporations. Most effective altruists work at nonprofits or donate earnings—they don't steal from customers. Haseeb describes SBF's actual problem as "delusions of grandeur": the belief that he would keep winning, that crypto would keep rising, that he could save BlockFi and Alameda through market manipulation.

Laura accepts the distinction but notes that she views the philosophy skeptically regardless. She sees effective altruism as implicitly assuming one person can calculate the "greatest good for the most people"—a God-like power she finds inappropriate. Haseeb counters that this is a misreading: effective altruism makes claims about *your own resources*, not about controlling others.

**Laura's Crypto Holdings and Journalistic Ethics**

Laura addresses a widespread fiction: that she never owned crypto. She bought Bitcoin around $200 in 2015 and Ether later. When she left Forbes in 2018 to freelance, the New York Times said she couldn't write for them if she owned crypto. Forbes allowed ownership with disclosure, but the Times did not. Facing the prospect of conflicts at multiple publications, Laura sold her holdings. She later learned the logic was inconsistent: Wired rejected her for running a publication that takes crypto sponsors while advertising tech companies on every page with the same business model.

In 2024, after Trump's election, Laura rebought crypto, concluding the conflict-of-interest rules were arbitrary and the sacrifice not worth the professional benefits. She acknowledges she focused more on book-writing than on rebuilding her crypto portfolio during the interim years.

**Interviewing Philosophy and Regrets**

Laura has never interviewed Brian Armstrong, despite asking him once. Armstrong apparently uses a checklist and only does interviews with "friendly" hosts. Laura sees herself as neutral rather than friendly, which may have disqualified her. She balances her audience's interest in tough questions against guests' comfort. With Michael Saylor, she asked about MicroStrategy's 2000 accounting irregularities not because it was immediately relevant to Bitcoin, but to help the audience understand him fully. Saylor was annoyed. With Charles Hoskinson, her fact-checking of his false PhD claims earned her attacks from the Cardano community, who saw her as attacking their "cult leader." Yet she stands by the fact-check: both colleges confirmed he only attended undergrad.

Laura's hierarchy is clear: audience first (demanding the questions they need answered), sponsors second (in terms of business, but not editorial), guests third. She doesn't ask softballs in sponsored interviews, but in some cases (conferences, paid appearances) she does agree not to be antagonistic since the sponsor is literally paying her for the spot.

**On Interviewing vs. Hosting Panel Shows**

Haseeb runs The Chopping Block, a four-person discussion show (not an interview format). He explains that the best version isn't four people taking turns giving commentary—it's a "play" where characters interact and reveal themselves through reacting to the world. Early on, he gripped the reins too tightly; he's learned that the tight structure should anchor the show while spontaneity brings the energy. Laura praises his moderating, noting his strength in timekeeping, storytelling recaps of the news (with drama and arc), and balancing structure with looseness.

Haseeb notes that The Chopping Block intentionally takes a different stance from Unchained. His show is "on the side of the industry"—not hostile, not trying to gotcha guests. Guests who want a tough interview go to Laura. Laura's neutral stance has been strategic for her career—she needed that credibility to build influence. But with 10 years of authority, Haseeb suggests she could now shift toward more opinion without losing credibility.

**Laura's Personal Transformation and Health**

Laura alludes to a major but still-undisclosed health crisis that radically changed her thinking. The help she received came from "disrespected corners," not establishment sources. This experience radicalized her—she's now more libertarian, less trusting of mainstream institutions and Democratic Party orthodoxy. She questions whether the neutral stance that served her early career is still necessary or even beneficial. She considers being more opinionated on air but hasn't decided. She hints that this transformation is significant enough to affect how she presents herself publicly, and she's working with her agent on how and when to discuss it.

**Future and Identity**

Laura frames herself as "a writer with a podcast"—not a podcaster who happens to write. She loves podcasting but sees writing and books as her core identity. She's published one book (on Ethereum and ICOs) and wants to write many more. Her journalism background, fact-checking rigor, and standards will continue, even as she navigates the tension between traditional journalistic neutrality and her own evolved convictions.

Haseeb affirms that she has the stature in the industry now to shift her approach if she wants to. The journalist identity was necessary when she was building credibility; now, she can choose whether to keep it.

# Science Friday — "Inside the lives of astronauts' families"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guest:** Dr. Tracy Scott (sociologist at Emory University; daughter of Apollo 15 Commander David R. Scott; studies the lives and families of moonshot-era astronauts).

## TL;DR

- **Apollo astronauts' wives had "first lady" responsibilities** — making speeches, traveling to contractors, going on diplomatic state trips to foreign countries, with no pay and no option to decline.
- **Divorce rate among Apollo families was approximately 80%** — the strain of missions, separation during the week, and unpaid public duties took a significant toll on marriages.
- **Apollo-era astronauts were pilots first** — selected from military test pilots starting in 1963; the third group of astronauts, they learned science on the job rather than holding PhDs upfront like modern astronauts.
- **Communication with families transformed** — Apollo-era families heard mission control via squawk boxes in their homes but could not talk back; modern Artemis astronauts can actively communicate with families in real time.
- **Viewing launches as ordinary** — astronaut children grew up thinking their fathers' work was normal, not scary; Tracy Scott read books during the Apollo 15 launch rather than watching.
- **NASA changed from startup to bureaucracy** — when David Scott was selected in 1963 (five years after NASA's founding), it was informal, flexible, and astronauts had input on spacecraft design; today's NASA is more regulated and hierarchical.
- **Seeing Earth from space shifted perspectives dramatically** — astronauts who traveled beyond low Earth orbit experienced perspective shifts, seeing Earth as a unified whole ("little blue marble") where everyone is figuratively family.
- **Modern astronauts express universal love** — Artemis II astronauts use language like "we love you from the moon" directed at all of humanity, reflecting an overview effect that has persisted since Apollo but "we've forgotten."

**The Roles and Demands on Apollo Wives**

Dr. Tracy Scott emphasizes that while children may not have had an explicit "job," the wives of Apollo astronauts did—and it was far more extensive than commonly recognized. The wives made speeches around Houston, traveled to contractors, and after missions went on state diplomatic trips to foreign countries, complete with staff and itineraries. Scott notes that her students have likened the role to that of first ladies. One student asked whether the wives were paid; when Scott answered no, the student asked, "Could they say no?" The answer was also no. Scott observes, "It was a different era. None of them had any idea they would be doing this. They didn't have any training, but they did it, and they did it really well. And they held the families together during the week." Barbara Cernan, wife of Apollo 17 commander Gene Cernan, captured the strain in a memorable phrase: "If you think going to the moon is hard, try staying home."

**Toll on Marriages and Family Life**

The divorce rate among Apollo astronauts' families reached approximately 80 percent or higher, reflecting the emotional and logistical strain. Yet the wives Scott interviewed expressed a paradoxical perspective: despite experiencing heartbreak and significant hardship, many looked back on that era with a sense of profound meaning and communal purpose. One wife summed it up succinctly: "It was the most exciting time of my life because we were going to the moon. We." Scott notes that this use of "we" captures what it felt like—even divorced women saw themselves as participants in something bigger than themselves.

**Children's Perspectives and Normalcy**

For children growing up in Houston's astronaut community, the extraordinary became ordinary. Scott recalls that when a reporter asked her as a child whether she was afraid of her father's missions, she answered, "Of what?" Because her father had been a test pilot (which was actually far more dangerous than spaceflight), and because all her friends' fathers were astronauts, the entire situation felt normal. She even read books during the Apollo 15 launch viewing rather than watching. Scott's generation understood intellectually that their fathers' work was special—particularly when photographers and reporters camped on their front lawns—but emotionally, they treated it as routine.

**The Startup Era vs. Modern NASA**

A key difference between the Apollo and Artemis eras is the organizational structure and culture. When David Scott was selected into the astronaut corps in 1963, NASA was only five years old and operated more like a startup: informal, flexible, with less bureaucracy. Astronauts had direct input into spacecraft design and could communicate freely with engineers and program managers. Today's NASA is far more regulated and hierarchical, with stricter rules and enforcement. This shift changed not only how the space program operated but also the culture that bound astronauts and their families together.

**Communication Evolution**

One stark difference between eras is how families could communicate with astronauts in flight. During the Apollo era, NASA installed squawk boxes in astronauts' homes that carried a continuous feed from mission control, allowing families to hear (but not respond to) communications. The boxes would be muted at night or when sensitive technical discussions occurred. Tracy Scott remembers it as background noise—present but not commanding attention. In contrast, Artemis II astronauts can actively communicate with their families, creating a fundamentally different emotional experience for both the astronauts and those at home.

**The Overview Effect and Perspective Shifts**

A striking theme across the episode is how astronauts who traveled beyond low Earth orbit experienced what Scott calls a perspective shift—what is sometimes called the overview effect. Rusty Schweickart, who flew with David Scott on Apollo 9, described it vividly: seeing Earth as the only color in the blackness of space, surrounded by gray moon and white stars, causes you to realize "that's where all my people are." The insight expands from family to all of humanity—"everybody else is like family. Everyone's in this together." This shift was not unique to one astronaut; Artemis II mission specialist Christina Koch expressed similar sentiments: "It truly emphasized how alike we are, how the same thing keeps every single person on planet Earth alive." Editor Norman Cousins captured the significance in a quote Scott invokes: "What is most significant about the lunar voyage is not that man set foot on the moon, but that he set eye on Earth."

**From the Overview Effect to a Broader Perspective**

Scott observes that this perspective—that humans are unified and interconnected—has been available since Apollo but has been largely forgotten in modern culture. The Artemis II astronauts are rekindling it. Pilot Victor Glover addressed Earth collectively: "We love you from the moon," speaking as if all humanity were his family. Scott contrasts this communal worldview with contemporary culture, which has become "much more individualistic, much more focused on ourselves and what's going to make us happy." She argues that modern society has lost the focus on collective accomplishment and helping each other. By recalling the perspective of astronauts who have seen Earth whole, she suggests, we might recover a more human, communal way of being.

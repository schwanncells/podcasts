**The Spanish-American War and Sudden Imperial Expansion**

In 1898, the United States went to war with Spain ostensibly in support of Cuban and Puerto Rican independence fighters. What followed, however, was a strategic pivot: the U.S. military became involved not only in supporting Cuban and Puerto Rican rebels but also in a rebellion in the Philippines, a Spanish territory on the other side of the world. Rather than grant Philippine independence, the U.S. purchased the islands from Spain for $20 million, effectively removing Spanish colonial rule only to replace it with American imperial control. Within a short period, the U.S. had annexed or taken control of Guam, Hawaii, Puerto Rico, American Samoa, and the Philippines—making it, quite suddenly, an imperial power comparable to the European empires it had previously opposed.

**The Philippine-American War: Brutal Tactics and Scope**

In February 1899, Filipinos began fighting for independence against the United States, launching a war that would consume vast numbers of lives. U.S. military tactics included burning villages, concentrating Filipino populations in camps or garrison towns where they were cut off from food supplies, and employing a form of water torture called "the water cure"—in which dirty water was forced into the mouths of insurgents and suspected insurgents until they could no longer endure it. This water cure bore a discomforting resemblance to waterboarding practices used in later conflicts. The war dragged on from 1899 until approximately 1913, lasting roughly fourteen years, with estimates suggesting it killed some three-quarters of a million people.

The brutality scandalized both the American mainland and international observers. Photographs and accounts of trenches full of Filipino corpses and tortured independence fighters circulated widely, creating cognitive dissonance: how could a nation founded on the principles of liberty and democracy—the "animating virtues" articulated in 1776—reconcile itself with colonial conquest and torture?

**Ideological Justification and Imperial Enthusiasm**

Imperial supporters pushed back against this contradiction by arguing that democracy itself could not function in the Philippines. Teddy Roosevelt, who would become president, stated: "Not one competent witness who has actually known the facts believes the Filipinos capable of self-government at present, or believes that such an effort would result in anything but a horrible confusion of tyranny and anarchy." He added that "the institutions of a free republic cannot at a leap be transplanted into wholly alien soil among a people who have not the slightest conception of liberty and self-government as we use those words."

Beyond racist ideology, other factors fueled imperial enthusiasm. The Census Bureau had declared in 1890 that the American settlement frontier had technically closed—the nation's western expansion had reached its limit. This prompted a theory circulating among elites: that American democracy had been sustained by the presence of a frontier, and without new territory to expand into, the nation faced existential decline. The solution, in this logic, was to "make more frontier"—to seize overseas territory. Additionally, young men of Teddy Roosevelt's generation who came of age in the 1890s had missed the Civil War (their fathers' war) and hungered for opportunities to prove themselves through military conflict. Jingoism and a cultural investment in violent masculinity created appetite for imperial warfare.

**The Name Change: From "United States" to "America"**

As the U.S. underwent imperial expansion and took on overseas territories, something unexpected happened: the nation's name changed. Before 1898, English-language speakers commonly referred to the country as "the United States," "the Republic," "the Union," or occasionally "the United States of America." Calling it simply "America" was discouraged and even corrected. A contemporary British writer observed that before 1898, whenever British sources referred to the country as "America," they were "constantly getting corrected" with responses like "No, no, no. America is not the name of our country. It is the United States." Yet immediately after the Spanish-American War and the acquisition of overseas territories, the correction flipped entirely: when the same writers called it "the United States," they were corrected the opposite way—told "No, no, no. We call it America. That's how we've always called it."

Historical records reveal this shift was abrupt rather than gradual. No pre-1898 American national anthem—including the Star-Spangled Banner, Yankee Doodle, Hail to the Chief, My Country 'Tis of Thee, Battle Cry of Freedom, Battle Hymn of the Republic, or Stars and Stripes Forever—mentions the word "America" in its lyrics. Yet after 1898, new patriotic songs appeared with "America" in their titles and lyrics: "America the Beautiful" and "God Bless America."

The most striking evidence comes from Teddy Roosevelt's early presidency. Upon taking office, Roosevelt began referring to the country as "America" in his first message to Congress and other statements. Records show that in a single two-week period, Roosevelt used "America" to refer to the country more times than every previous president combined had used it in their entire tenures. Once Roosevelt established this linguistic precedent, the shift became normalized and permanent.

**Why the Name Changed: Imperial Identity**

The name change reflected a deeper identity shift. The old names—"United States," "the Union"—denoted a federal republic composed of states joined together. But once the Philippines, Puerto Rico, and other overseas territories became part of the country, these names no longer made sense. The territories were not states and, as colonies, had not joined the union consensually. The contradictions became unavoidable: was this still a union of states? Clearly not. Was it restricted to the Americas? No, it extended globally. Leaders like Roosevelt, who were conscious that American political character was changing, adopted "America" as a shorthand that avoided the semantic problems of calling an empire a "union of states." The name change was, in effect, a linguistic acknowledgment of American imperial identity.

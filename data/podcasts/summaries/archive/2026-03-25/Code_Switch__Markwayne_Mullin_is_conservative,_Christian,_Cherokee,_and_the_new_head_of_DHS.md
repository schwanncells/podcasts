# Code Switch — "Markwayne Mullin is conservative, Christian, Cherokee, and the new head of DHS"

**Host:** B.A. Parker and Gene Demby. **Guests:** Grahamley Brewer (reporter, Associated Press race and ethnicity team; Cherokee Nation citizen; has covered Mullin's political career for roughly six to seven years).

## TL;DR

- **Mullin confirmed as DHS secretary on March 23, 2026** — the first Indigenous person to lead the department, replacing Kristi Noem, who was fired amid bad press including the shooting deaths of two people during ICE raids in Minnesota.
- **Mullin is a Cherokee Nation citizen, former MMA fighter, and former owner of Mullen Plumbing** — he served 10 years in the House before becoming an Oklahoma senator in 2023, with no prior experience running emergency management or border security.
- **Being conservative and Cherokee is not a contradiction in Oklahoma** — Brewer reports it is not uncommon among Cherokee people to be white, conservative, and Christian, and that Cherokee political identity is not monolithic.
- **Mullin privately sponsored undocumented employees through legal citizenship processes** — despite publicly supporting Trump's immigration crackdown and suggesting U.S.-born children of undocumented immigrants should be deported alongside their parents.
- **Mullin has close personal relationships with tribal leaders across Oklahoma** — a stark contrast to Noem, who was banished from almost every reservation in her state; Brewer sees this as the most immediate difference between the two secretaries.
- **Native people have been swept up in ICE raids through racial profiling**, with reports of tribal IDs not being respected by ICE officers — Brewer argues Mullin could mandate new training to address this directly.
- **The central open question is which Mullin shows up** — the bipartisan dealmaker known for inviting Democrats and Republicans to his dinner table, or the fiercely loyal Trump ally who aligned with claims the 2020 election was stolen, raising questions about whether he would comply if Trump again tried to use DHS to seize voting machines.

**Who Is Markwayne Mullin**

Mullin's path to DHS secretary is unusual. He grew up in eastern Oklahoma — solidly within the Cherokee Nation's territory — ran his family's plumbing and HVAC company, Mullen Plumbing, after his father became ill, and was a professional MMA fighter before entering politics. He served a decade in the House of Representatives and has been a U.S. senator for Oklahoma since 2023. Brewer notes that he has no obvious background in emergency management, immigration enforcement, or border security, and that even on immigration specifically, it was not an issue Mullin was particularly known for as a legislator.

His personal bond with Trump runs deep. Brewer reports that roughly six years ago, Mullin's son Jim suffered a serious wrestling injury, and Trump personally offered to fly the family to California for treatment, covered costs, and checked in weekly on his recovery. Mullin has spoken about the moment openly, saying "here's the president of the United States and he did it just because he cared." Brewer says that bond appears to border on a father-figure dynamic and that it shapes how closely Mullin orbits Trump's politics.

**Cherokee Identity and the Trail of Tears Context**

Mullin grew up surrounded by Cherokee people and says he did not think of his tribal citizenship as anything special until he arrived in Washington, D.C. — at which point Tom Cole, then the only Native American in Congress, told him they had "just doubled the size of our Native American caucus." Brewer frames Mullin as part of a long line of Cherokee diplomats and political operators who have navigated Washington, going back to Chief John Ross lobbying against removal before the Trail of Tears. A quarter of the Cherokee Nation died along the Trail. Brewer notes that the Cherokee people were also among the first tribes to adopt a written constitution, develop a court system, and publish a newspaper — all in an effort to demonstrate they could coexist with the American government, an effort that ultimately did not prevent removal.

That history makes Trump's Oval Office portrait of Andrew Jackson — the driving force behind the Trail of Tears — a pointed backdrop for Mullin's new role. Brewer says he is "really curious" to see how Mullin's Cherokee identity will shape his leadership of a department that has been detaining Native people through immigration enforcement.

**Cherokee Reactions: Not Unanimous**

Brewer reports a genuinely mixed reaction within the Cherokee Nation. In Cherokee community Facebook groups, he has seen ongoing debate from people who grew up with Mullin and know him personally. The recurring sentiment: "this could be good for us, but we all know that just having a Cherokee person in the room doesn't necessarily mean that the outcome is going to be good for Native people." He points to history as the cautionary note — Cherokee people were in some of those rooms before the Trail of Tears, too.

Chuck Hoskin Jr., the principal chief of the Cherokee Nation, is supportive of Mullin. And Brewer says that being a conservative, Christian Cherokee is simply not unusual in Oklahoma — the tribe's political views are as varied as American politics generally.

**Native People and Immigration Enforcement**

Brewer pushes back on framing that pits Indigenous people and immigrants against each other. He says the issue for Native people is not hostility toward immigrants but the specific injustice of being stopped and detained on land their ancestors fought to remain on. Tribal citizens have been swept up in ICE raids through racial profiling; some report their tribal IDs were not taken seriously by ICE officers. Noem sent a letter to tribal leaders denying that any tribal citizens had been detained, a claim Brewer says was contradicted by people he spoke to directly.

Brewer argues Mullin could use his relationships with tribal leaders and his institutional position to mandate better ICE training on tribal IDs — a concrete, direct intervention that his predecessor made no effort toward.

**The Two Mullins**

Brewer describes two versions of Mullin that he cannot reconcile in advance. One is the bipartisan dealmaker: Mullin ran a bipartisan workout at the Capitol when he was in the House, built friendships with Democrats including Tulsi Gabbard (then a Democrat), Josh Gottheimer, and Joe Kennedy, and was known for hosting members of both parties at his home for dinner. Gottheimer told Brewer that Mullin was "kind, willing to listen, willing to change his mind." That version of Mullin contrasts sharply with the combative figure seen on television, including the Senate hearing where he told Teamsters president Sean O'Brien to "stand your butt up" and offered to take the dispute outside.

The other Mullin is the one who aligned with Trump's claim that the 2020 election was stolen and who, despite being a firsthand witness to January 6th — including seeing Ashley Babbitt shot — ultimately backed the president's broader framing. Brewer says the open question is whether Mullin would comply if Trump, as he did in 2020, ordered DHS to seize voting machines. Mullin voted against certifying the election. Whether he would resist a similar directive now, as the secretary carrying it out, is something Brewer says he cannot predict.

**Mullin vs. Noem**

Brewer draws a sharp contrast between the two secretaries on tribal relations. Noem was banished from nearly every reservation in South Dakota as governor. Her "dear tribal leaders" letter as DHS secretary — denying any tribal citizens had been detained by ICE — was widely seen as disingenuous. Mullin, by contrast, has strong relationships with tribal leaders across Oklahoma and beyond, and his reputation in Congress as a pragmatic, approachable figure who listens to critics could allow him to manage congressional oversight and bipartisan concerns more effectively. Brewer does not predict Mullin will diverge significantly from Trump's immigration policies, but says the tone and process of engagement with tribal nations is likely to be meaningfully different.

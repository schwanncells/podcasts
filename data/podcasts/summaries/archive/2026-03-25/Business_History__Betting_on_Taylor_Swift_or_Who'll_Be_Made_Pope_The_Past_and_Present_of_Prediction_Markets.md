# Business History — "Betting on Taylor Swift or Who'll Be Made Pope: The Past and Present of Prediction Markets"

**Host:** Jacob Goldstein and Robert Smith (Business History). **Guests:** Stacey Vanek Smith and Max Chavkin (Everybody's Business, Bloomberg BusinessWeek) — recorded live show mashup billed as "Everybody's Business History."

## TL;DR

- **Prediction markets now exceed $100 billion a year** — platforms Kalshi and Polymarket let users bet on everything from Jerome Powell saying the word "renovation" in a press conference to whether Jesus Christ will return before 2027 (currently 3%).
- **The key appeal is homework rewarded over luck** — unlike casino gambling or stock markets where large institutions have structural edges, prediction market trader "Domer" bet $400 million on Polymarket and built a career around research-driven edges, including a 25-to-1 bet on Sarah Palin as VP based on a lone Alaska flight landing in a small Ohio town.
- **A 21-year-old won thousands by recording Charlie Puth's Super Bowl national anthem rehearsal** — Kaden Booth bought a parabolic mic used by birdwatchers, flew to San Francisco, stood outside the stadium, timed the rehearsal at 1 minute 51 seconds, and placed his bet before the game.
- **Prediction markets on papal succession go back to 1500s Italy** — brokers in Rome and Florence held legal monopolies on *scommesse a fare il papa*, printing tickets for each cardinal candidate; in 1549, the market put Cardinal Pole at 80% — reflecting his actual 26-of-28 votes — likely through cardinal insider trading.
- **Pope Gregory XIV banned papal betting in 1590 under penalty of excommunication — and it worked** — ambassador dispatches home about conclave odds disappear from the record after the papal bull, trading the value of insider information for the appearance of institutional integrity.
- **DARPA launched a terrorism prediction market in 2003 called PAM (Policy Analysis Market)** — proponents argued it would aggregate dispersed intelligence signals the way markets aggregate information; two Democratic senators called it "ridiculous and grotesque," the deputy Defense Secretary said he'd never heard of it, and it was killed the same day it became public.
- **Sports leagues and the NYSE are now cashing in on prediction markets** — the NHL and Major League Soccer signed exclusivity deals with Polymarket; the New York Stock Exchange acquired a large stake. Insiders like NBA player Jontay Porter (banned for life) have bet on outcomes they controlled.
- **The insider trading problem and the moral backlash are not new** — the pendulum between enthusiasm and repugnance has been swinging for 500 years; the question posed at the end of the show is when it swings back.

**What Prediction Markets Are and How They Work**

Jacob Goldstein explains that on platforms like Polymarket and Kalshi, a price functions as a probability: a contract priced at 10 cents pays $1 if the event happens and $0 if it doesn't. Markets exist on nearly anything — weather, politics, sports, Federal Reserve press conference word choices. Kalshi founder Tariq Mansour described the company's goal as to "financialize everything and create a tradable asset out of any difference in opinion," with a total addressable market he called "quite massive." Stacey Vanek Smith notes the argument for everyday use mirrors how Wall Street hedge funds use futures — but made available to ordinary people, including as a form of emotional hedging (betting against your own team to soften the loss).

**Why Homework Can Win**

The hosts' central argument in favor of prediction markets is that they reward research in a way casinos and most investing don't. Casino games have a built-in house edge that no amount of study overcomes. Institutional investors can access satellite images of Walmart parking lots to estimate earnings before individual investors can; the information asymmetry is structural. On Polymarket and Kalshi, however, original research confers a genuine edge.

Pseudonymous trader "Domer," formerly a professional poker player, exemplifies this. His first major prediction market bet came in 2008 on John McCain's VP pick. He noticed the announcement event was scheduled in a small Ohio swing-state town and researched the nearest airport. He found a single anomalous flight from Alaska and concluded it was likely transporting Sarah Palin. She was at 25-to-1 odds; he placed $1,000 and made $25,000. By the time Business History spoke with him, Domer had placed roughly $400 million in bets on Polymarket, including a $1 million bet on Taylor Swift album sales. His career rests entirely on research, not luck.

Kaden Booth, a 21-year-old, applied the same logic to Super Bowl national anthem length. The song's duration varies widely — Jewel's 1998 version ran 1 minute 27 seconds; Alicia Keys' 2013 rendition ran 2 minutes 36 seconds. Booth bought a parabolic microphone (the kind serious birdwatchers use to capture distant bird calls), a stopwatch, and a plane ticket to San Francisco after spotting Charlie Puth posting on social media that he was headed there a week before the game. He stood outside the stadium, caught the rehearsal, timed it at 1 minute 51 seconds, placed his bet, and won thousands.

**The 500-Year History: Papal Betting in 1500s Italy**

Goldstein and Smith trace prediction markets back to Renaissance Italy, when modern finance was emerging alongside the Medici and the first European banks. Brokers in Rome and Florence received legal monopolies on *scommesse a fare il papa* — betting on who would become the next Pope. They issued printed *cèdole* (vouchers) listing each cardinal's name, priced at fractional odds paying 100 scudi if correct — structurally identical to modern prediction markets.

These markets reflected real information. At the 1549 conclave, Cardinal Pole of England traded at 80%, and historical records confirm he held 26 of the 28 votes needed to win. The most plausible explanation for such accuracy: cardinals were insider trading despite the conclave's literal lock-and-key secrecy (the word "conclave" derives from Latin for "with a key"). Pole ultimately lost when a French cardinal arrived and accused him of being a crypto-Lutheran — a secret Protestant — and the papacy went to an Italian instead.

**The 1590 Papal Bull and the Backlash Cycle**

In 1590, Pope Gregory XIV issued a papal bull banning papal prediction markets under penalty of excommunication. Remarkably, it worked — ambassador correspondence home about conclave odds disappears from the historical record after that point. Goldstein frames the tradeoff clearly: the Church appeared less corrupt and more holy, which was useful during the Counter-Reformation. But the wool merchants, ambassadors, and foreign governments who had genuine material stakes in knowing who the next Pope would be lost a valuable source of intelligence.

Goldstein's closing historical argument is that this is not a story of linear moral decay. The pendulum between enthusiasm for prediction markets and moral repugnance at them has swung back and forth for literally 500 years. The papal bull is technically still in effect.

**American Election Markets and the 1920s Prohibition**

Before polling existed in the United States, prediction markets were used to gauge presidential races. Reporters tracked crowd toasts at bars — counting "huzzahs" for each candidate — which Smith describes as "the most fun but a terrible way to predict the presidency." Actual prediction markets on presidential elections emerged and then were banned in the 1920s when states determined they were inappropriate.

**DARPA's Terror Market and Its Instant Death**

In May 2001, the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency put out a call for "electronic market-based decision support" — exploring whether prediction markets could help the U.S. government forecast global events like the probability of a revolution in Tunisia or economic growth in Mexico. After September 11, proponents argued the 9/11 Commission's finding that relevant signals existed in different agencies was precisely the problem markets could solve: aggregating dispersed information into a single price signal.

The project, named PAM (Policy Analysis Market), was designed as a small test — $100 in play money per participant, focused on Middle East scenarios. Economist Robin Hansen was a key proponent. Before it launched in the summer of 2003, Senators Byron Dorgan (South Dakota) and Ron Wyden (Oregon) learned of the plan and called a press conference. Dorgan called it "ridiculous and grotesque." Wyden warned that terrorists could use it to profit from attacks or to mislead authorities. The deputy Secretary of Defense told reporters he had never heard of the program until reading about it in the paper that day and immediately cancelled it.

**Moral Tension and the Insider Trading Problem**

Chavkin argues that prediction markets are trending toward the same structural disadvantage as casinos and hedge funds — big players are buying their way to edges. Sports leagues have signed exclusivity deals with Polymarket (NHL, Major League Soccer) rather than fighting the corruption risk, because they want a share of the revenue. The New York Stock Exchange acquired a large stake in Polymarket. NBA player Jontay Porter was banned for life after allegedly betting on his own faked injuries on the sidelines. Domer told the hosts about a suspiciously large bet placed on Polymarket from a new account just before Eric Adams dropped out of the New York mayoral race.

Kalshi has run advertising during the Olympics using historical imagery — David with a slingshot, Paul Revere's midnight ride, the Wright Brothers' first flight — framing them as overlooked betting opportunities. The hosts note that this reframing of historical moments as financial events captures something real about how these markets see the world.

**The Open Question**

Smith closes by arguing the Internet changes the scale of the phenomenon qualitatively, not just quantitatively: frictionless access, constant marketing, and the ability to bet on every aspect of every event simultaneously makes the current moment different from 16th-century Italy, where placing a bet required physically handing a piece of paper to a broker. Chavkin predicts that some future atrocity will trigger a senator to stand up and call the whole thing morally repugnant — and the pendulum will swing back. Goldstein frames the question as open: will prediction markets be banned by 2030? As he notes, the problem with that bet is that if you bet yes and win, collecting may be complicated.

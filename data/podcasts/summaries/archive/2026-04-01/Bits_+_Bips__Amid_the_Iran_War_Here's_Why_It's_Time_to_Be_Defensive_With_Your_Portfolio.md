# Bits + Bips — "Amid the Iran War, Here's Why It's Time to Be 'Defensive' With Your Portfolio"

**Hosts:** Austin Campbell (adjunct professor, Columbia Business School; founder, Zero Knowledge Group); Ram Ahluwalia (CEO, Lumida Wealth); Chris Perkins (president, CoinFund).

## TL;DR

- **Brent crude hit $116/barrel as Iran conflict enters week 5 with no off-ramp in sight** — Trump told the FT he wants to "take the oil in Iran" and cited Karg Island, which handles 90% of Iran's crude exports; a 15-point Whitcoff peace proposal was publicly rejected by Iran, which countered with five conditions including war reparations; no Pentagon briefing in nine days; U.S. military spending is running at $200 billion/month (~$2 trillion annualized).
- **All three hosts recommend defensive positioning** — Ram says the S&P could fall to 6,000–6,200 from Friday's close of 6,368, marking 5 straight weekly declines (longest streak since 2022), with QQQs in correction, Moody's recession model at 50%, and Goldman Sachs recession odds at 30%; he bought Berkshire Hathaway at 1.3x tangible book, Microsoft, and Meta at 17.5x forward P/E, while shorting industrials names like Caterpillar, John Deere, and Teradyne.
- **Chris argues "there is no turning back"** — if the IRGC acquires nuclear weapons it holds the Strait of Hormuz hostage indefinitely; Austin says the "head of the snake" military assumption was falsified and the conflict could become a 10-year cascading regime collapse resembling the French Revolution; Ram says kinetics alone won't resolve it and the IRGC "likes the status quo."
- **Bitcoin holding at 66K but largely on STRC inflows** — Ram attributes Bitcoin's relative resilience to Strategy convertible product flows, not organic demand, calling it "not a healthy bid"; crypto has shown continued resilience versus equities but liquidity is being absorbed by gold and oil.
- **The Clarity Act is the key crypto catalyst** — Chris attended the Digital Asset Summit and says it had "the most signups they've ever had" with historically strong institutional participation; if the Clarity Act passes, it represents "a very, very nice tailwind" for the asset class once macro clouds lift.
- **Canton vs. public chains fight went fully public** — Canton co-founder Shaul Fear told regulators "ZK proofs are too dangerous for mission-critical financial infrastructure," citing a Solana April 2025 zero-day in ZK-powered confidential transfers that could have allowed unlimited token minting; Don Wilson called MEV unsuitable for financial markets; Omid Malekan fired back that Fear is a "guy who backs corporate database alternative where a single entity can steal everybody's money."
- **Austin argues ETH faces an unsolvable trilemma** — permissionless layer one, real-world assets, and complex smart contracts: pick two; if Iranian money in a commingled Aave pool triggers a sanctions freeze, it bricks the entire protocol with 2008-style cascading effects; his conclusion is "DeFi needs to go away" for ETH to credibly serve as a global settlement layer with real-world assets.

**Iran War: The Bond Market Is Running Foreign Policy**

Austin opens by noting that Trump's FT interview — in which he floated seizing Karg Island, which handles 90% of Iran's crude exports — landed on top of an oil market already under stress. Brent closed Friday at $112.57 and briefly topped $116 Monday morning; WTI approached $100. The Houthis fired a ballistic missile at Israel; a suspected Iranian-backed attack wounded U.S. troops at a Saudi airbase. Trump's April 6 pause on striking Iranian power plants remains in effect, but the FT interview signals the military track is still active. Meanwhile, the 10-year yield hit 4.48% on Friday — a level that has historically prompted Trump to pause on tariffs, given that most U.S. mortgages price off the 10-year.

Ram frames the dynamics around who has more time pressure. Trump attempted to "manifest" a deal last week, but the IRGC publicly reiterated it is not negotiating. Ram notes the IRGC is "holed up in bunkers and making highly effective meme videos targeting American consumers" using American GPUs — propaganda aimed outward at the U.S. public rather than inward at the Iranian one. His read is that Trump may be angling to seize Karg Island as a bargaining chip — "escalate to de-escalate" — but markets don't like it: rising oil, a climbing 10-year, accelerating inflation, and a stronger dollar are all "toxic for risk assets."

**No Credible Off-Ramp: The Strategic Impasse**

Chris argues there is no path back to the status quo. If the IRGC achieves nuclear weapons, it holds the Strait of Hormuz hostage indefinitely, which is unacceptable for global trade. Asia is "choking," Europe is now under threat from new ballistic missile technology, and Iran recently hit a Kuwaiti desalinization plant for the first time — a new escalation rung. He believes it will "get worse before it gets better" but that the world is ultimately "axed to clear that strait," including China, whose oil supply depends on it.

Austin's counterpoint is that the core military assumption — take out the head of the snake and get a resolution — has been falsified. IRGC legitimacy is rooted entirely in hostility to the U.S. and the West, making it functionally impossible for any Iranian official to negotiate without risking elimination. The negotiating team itself has already been taken out. Austin draws the parallel to the French Revolution: this could be a 10-year process of cascading regime collapse with an unpredictable outcome. The indicator he is watching is factionalist problems within Iran itself — Kurdish uprisings, Azerbaijani involvement, or Pakistani action — as signs that the IRGC is hitting the point of negotiating from weakness.

Ram adds that the initial White House timeline of four to five weeks has now expired, and the administration is now saying "soon" — a formulation he compares unfavorably to "subprime is contained" and "inflation is contained." He estimates even a legitimate negotiated settlement would take 60 to 90 days minimum, and that's assuming a credible counterparty even exists. The IRGC's internal fractures, Austin notes, mean the party they eventually negotiate with may not have come to the table yet.

**The Strategic Level: Resolve, Kinetics, and China's Position**

The hosts converge on the view that this conflict is ultimately a test of political will at the strategic level, not tactical capability. The U.S. wins at the tactical level every day, just as it did in Vietnam, but kinetics alone cannot force a resolution when asymmetric defenders only need "a couple dozen people with rockets" along the Strait of Hormuz coastline. Ram estimates interceptor supply is being rationed — letting some rockets land to preserve inventory — and notes the cost of the conflict at $2 trillion annualized is itself inflationary and will pressure 10-year yields further. He also raises the possibility that China is supplying rockets and targeting to the IRGC, as it benefits from U.S. entanglement in a costly conflict.

Austin frames China's position as deeply uncomfortable regardless of outcome: if the U.S. secures the strait, it gains control over roughly 80% of Asia's oil supply, putting China in a strategically weak position. Chris agrees China is not happy, facing both high oil prices and the risk of the U.S. controlling the chokepoint. For the conflict to resolve, Austin argues, there must be a carrot alongside the stick — the West Germany and Japan post-WWII model, where the end state is "very profitable and viable for both sides." The question nobody has answered is what that carrot looks like for the IRGC.

**Market Outlook: Defensive Until There's a Credible Off-Ramp**

Ram's market prescription is unambiguous: stay small, hold excess cash, and do not try to be a hero. The S&P's fifth straight weekly decline leaves it roughly 7% lower year to date; the NASDAQ is down more than 10% from its October peak; the VIX remains elevated. He targets a potential drawdown to 6,000–6,200, pulling back to Liberation Day valuation lows, noting this is a more serious setup than Liberation Day because oil is an exogenous supply shock that monetary policy cannot fix. Consumer discretionary will get hit as consumer spending pulls back; agricultural commodities like wheat and corn are already rising, adding to domestic inflation.

His specific trades: long Berkshire Hathaway at 1.3x tangible book, long Microsoft, long Meta at 17.5x forward P/E as a flight to safety within large-cap tech. Short the industrials complex — Teradyne, Caterpillar, John Deere — which he calls "a bubble" that existed before the conflict and is now cracking under the weight of higher rates. He explicitly avoids Netflix (still expensive) and Tesla ("egregiously overpriced"). Ram warns against the "denial and complacency" visible on social media, where people are talking each other into buying stocks that are down 40–50%, citing fear-and-greed indicators that have "no relevance given the structural forces around the Strait of Hormuz."

Chris agrees on short-term volatility but remains medium-to-long-term bullish, particularly on crypto: "once this thing gets settled, I think we're going to be in a very good place." Austin adds that for quality companies with strong fundamentals, sentiment-driven drawdowns are actually a decent setup — but demands strict focus on fundamentals rather than macro narratives.

**Bitcoin and Crypto: Resilient but for Complicated Reasons**

Bitcoin's hold around 66K is notable given the broader equity carnage, but Ram is skeptical of reading too much into it. He attributes a significant chunk of the bid to STRC — Strategy's convertible product — noting a new client came in with STRC exposure and immediately sold it. "This is not a healthy bid," he says, and it makes it difficult for crypto to sustain a genuine risk-asset rally when liquidity is being absorbed by gold and oil. Software names like MongoDB, Datadog, and ARM still trade at 50–80x earnings, making it hard to find assets that can rise above a valuation re-rating environment driven by higher interest rates.

Chris is more optimistic: crypto has demonstrated continued resilience, the regulatory environment is "the best we could have imagined," and the Digital Asset Summit had the strongest institutional turnout he has seen. He sees Bitcoin starting to "catch up to the gold narrative" and considers the Clarity Act, if passed, a major structural tailwind. The world is "more and more a trustless, permissionless place," and when the current black clouds lift, Chris believes the asset class is well positioned.

**Canton vs. Public Chains: The Fight for Institutional Real Estate**

The second segment addresses the escalating public dispute between Canton Network and public chain advocates. Canton co-founder Shaul Fear argued to regulators that ZK proofs are "too dangerous for mission-critical financial infrastructure," pointing specifically to a Solana April 2025 zero-day in its ZK-powered confidential transfers that could have enabled unlimited token minting without detection. Don Wilson of DRW separately criticized MEV as inappropriate for institutional financial markets. The public chain community pushed back hard: Meltem Demirors called it a "special or permissioned interest at work," others described it as a "grossly outdated view on MEV," and Omid Malekan wrote that Fear is a "guy who backs corporate database alternative where a single entity can steal everybody's money."

Chris, who knows Canton's founders through DA Holdings (approximately as old as Ethereum), frames the dispute as a land grab: "everyone's fighting for real estate" before institutional adoption crystallizes, and once you have that territory, it's hard to be displaced. His own view is that different chains serve different applications: Ethereum's edge is censorship resistance and decentralization; Solana's is speed and TPS; Canton's is privacy, permissioning, and institutional compatibility. Ethereum leads stablecoins today; Canton is attracting super validators like Visa and investors like Goldman Sachs and TradeWeb because it solves for what Wall Street actually wants — control, compatibility with legal norms, and protection of competitive moat.

**Austin's ETH Trilemma and the Limits of DeFi**

Austin makes a more pointed structural argument. His core claim is that you cannot simultaneously have a permissionless layer one, real-world assets, and complex interconnected smart contracts — pick two. The problem is not Ethereum itself but the DeFi ecosystem built on top of it. Using Aave as a live example: if a commingled lending pool contains a meaningful amount of sanctioned Iranian money and a federal court freezes it, the stablecoin issuer (Circle for USDC) is not going to resist the order — but that freeze bricks the entire protocol, cascading damage onto innocent participants. "We're back to 2008 derivatives markets," he says.

Austin's prescription for Ethereum is stark: if the Ethereum Foundation wants ETH to be a legitimate global settlement layer with real-world assets, it should be "hammering on people every single day to reduce complexity and interconnectivity and to not build things that if somebody pulls that one Jenga block, the whole thing falls over." His conclusion: "DeFi needs to go away" in its current form for that use case. If ETH stays purely as a settlement layer for bilateral asset transfers without complex commingling, he has no objection to it. Chris pushes back, arguing the legal system should regulate behaviors and activities rather than the technology itself, and that a truly decentralized layer one with a million validators cannot be "court-ordered" off the chain — the freeze applies to the centralized application, not the base layer. The debate ends without resolution, but with consensus that the trilemma is real.

**Wall Street's Crypto Instincts and the Road Ahead**

Ram offers the clearest-eyed summary of why institutional adoption does not automatically favor public chains. Wall Street's "first instinct," he says, is to maximize its own value capture — as it did with Visa, MasterCard, ICE, TradeWeb, and MarketAxess. They want control, they want to deepen their moat, they want OFAC compliance and compatibility with legal dispute resolution. "Crypto entrepreneurs make the mistake of focusing too much on product features — they focus on features of the car rather than what the customer wants." Canton has product-market fit with its investor base precisely because it is solving for those institutional needs. Austin adds that Canton's governance challenge is the inverse of DTCC's founding: DTCC worked because the SEC effectively compelled all the major players to adopt it simultaneously, and he does not see Canton having achieved that kind of coordinated buy-in.

Both Austin and Chris agree that sovereign neutrality is Ethereum's genuine edge — the ability for anyone in Botswana or any developing country to move value over the network without permission. But as Chris notes, that edge is a hard sell to institutions and regulators subject to U.S. law: "the road to climb for free money is much higher" than for free speech.

# Unchained — "Do Centralized Real World Assets on DeFi Break Ethereum? - Bits + Bips"

**Hosts:** Austin Campbell (high scholar, Zero Knowledge Group); Ram Alawalia (founder and leader, Lumina); Chris Perkins (Coin Funds).

## TL;DR

- **Trump signaled seizure of Iran's Karg Island** (handles 90% of Iran's crude exports), sending Brent crude briefly to $116/barrel; the 10-year Treasury yield hit 4.48% — the level that has historically triggered Trump to pause tariffs — while $200 billion/month in conflict spending runs at a $2 trillion annualized pace.
- **No credible off-ramp visible** — the 15-point peace proposal floated through Pakistan was publicly rejected; Iran's IRGC reiterated it is not negotiating; there has been no Pentagon briefing in nine days; the White House's initial four-to-five-week timeline has drifted to "soon."
- **S&P at 6,368 logged its fifth straight weekly decline** (longest since 2022), NASDAQ down more than 10% from its October peak, S&P down roughly 7% year-to-date; Moody's recession model at 50% probability, Goldman raised recession odds to 30%; Ram sees a potential 15% correction to 6,000-6,200.
- **Bitcoin held at ~66K** while equities deteriorated, but Ram attributes the bid primarily to inflows from Michael Saylor's new STRC product — "not a healthy bid" — rather than organic institutional demand.
- **Canton Network vs. public chains:** Canton's co-founder declared ZK proofs "too dangerous for mission critical financial infrastructure," citing Solana's April 2025 zero-day; DRW's Don Wilson called MEV on Ethereum "not suitable for financial markets"; Meltem Demirors, Rebecca Reddick, and Omid Malikhani fired back calling it a "permissioned interest at work" and an "outdated view."
- **Austin's trilemma: permissionless, real world assets, and complex smart contracts — pick two.** Commingled DeFi pools containing sanctioned money could trigger a freeze order that bricks an entire protocol — "back to 2008 derivatives markets" — meaning if ETH wants to be a decentralized global settlement layer with real world assets, "DeFi needs to go away."
- **Ram's Wall Street thesis:** institutions want control, permissioning, and OFAC compatibility; crypto entrepreneurs "focus too much on product features" rather than what customers actually want, which is to deepen their own moat — the same playbook Wall Street ran with Visa, Mastercard, and TradeWeb.
- **The blockchain that handles most financial assets in 30 years probably hasn't been created yet** — Austin argues a vast, unexplored middle ground between permissionless Ethereum and permissioned Canton exists, currently occupied only by deeply unfavored chains like Stellar and Avalanche that the market is ignoring.

**The Bond Market Running Foreign Policy**

Trump told the Financial Times he wants to "take the oil in Iran," referencing Karg Island, which handles 90% of Iran's crude exports. U.S. troops are deployed to the region; Brent crude briefly hit $116/barrel on the news, having already closed the prior Friday at $112.57. The Houthis fired a ballistic missile at Israel; a suspected Iranian-backed attack on a Saudi airbase wounded U.S. troops. A 15-point peace proposal was floated through Pakistan but publicly rejected, though Iranian officials privately signaled interest per CBS News — with the caveat that it remained unclear who those officials were or their level of authority. The IRGC reiterated it is not negotiating, with Ram noting the group appeared to be "holed up in bunkers" producing meme videos targeting American consumers using NVIDIA GPUs.

The 10-year Treasury yield reached 4.48% on Friday — significant because most U.S. mortgages are priced off the 10-year, and it is the level that has historically prompted Trump to pause tariffs or reverse course. Ram estimated the conflict is running at roughly $200 billion per month — a $2 trillion annualized pace — which is itself inflationary and upward pressure on rates. There has been no Pentagon briefing in nine days. The White House's initial four-to-five-week timeline drifted to "soon," prompting Ram to compare the overoptimism to "when Powell was saying inflation is contained."

**Military and Diplomatic Outlook**

Chris argued there is no turning back: allowing the IRGC to reconstitute risks them acquiring nuclear weapons and holding the Strait of Hormuz — through which 80% of Asia's oil flows — hostage indefinitely. He sees short-term volatility but remains medium-to-long-term bullish on crypto, expecting the situation to resolve and Bitcoin to "catch up to the gold narrative." Ram and Austin were more skeptical of a quick resolution.

Ram pointed out the internal fractures within Iran: Tehran has higher protest concentrations, but rural areas remain far more pro-Shia, and an effective asymmetric deterrent along the 12-mile-wide strait requires only "a couple dozen people with rockets." Austin argued that the party who will ultimately negotiate an end to the conflict "has not even come to the table yet" because it is not clear who will be in charge when it ends — describing this potentially as a multi-year cascading process more like the French Revolution than a conventional ceasefire. He noted that Iran's IRGC negotiating team was already taken out, leaving the question of who the actual counterparty is.

All three hosts agreed that kinetics alone cannot resolve this. Austin drew the analogy to post-WWII U.S. reconstruction of West Germany and Japan: a durable resolution requires creating a winning condition for both sides — making peace more profitable than conflict. The carrot, all three noted, remains undefined. Chris observed that China is not benefiting from the status quo as much as it might appear: while Chinese tankers still move through the strait and China buys Iranian oil at ~$45/barrel, the risk of U.S. control over the strait — which would give the U.S. leverage over 80% of Asia's oil — puts China in a strategically bad position regardless of outcome.

**Market Conditions and Positioning**

The S&P closed at 6,368, its fifth straight weekly decline — the longest losing streak since 2022. The Dow fell 1.7% and the NASDAQ dropped more than 10% from its October peak. Year-to-date the S&P is down approximately 7%. The VIX remained elevated. Moody's recession model sits at 50% probability; Goldman Sachs raised recession odds to 30%. Ram sees the potential downside as a 15% correction back to 6,000-6,200 (the Liberation Day valuation lows), arguing this is more serious than Liberation Day because oil prices are outside policy control and 53% of S&P market cap depends on them. Standard monetary policy tools — changing interest rates — cannot create more oil.

Ram recommended staying defensive: staying small, holding excess cash, and resisting the Twitter consensus of buying dips on stocks down 40-50%. He bought Berkshire Hathaway (at 1.3x tangible book), Microsoft, and Meta at 17.5x forward P/E as flight-to-fundamentals positions. He is short the industrials complex — Caterpillar, John Deere, Teradyne — which he called a pre-existing bubble that is now cracking. Software names with 60-80x earnings multiples (MongoDB, Datadog, Arm) offer no refuge when interest rates rise. AI hyperscalers that spent heavily on CapEx and impaired their forward cash flow, reducing buybacks, were "among the first to feel the pain."

**Bitcoin's Relative Resilience**

Bitcoin held around 66K while equity markets deteriorated — something all three hosts noted as notable. Ram attributed the support primarily to flows into STRC, Michael Saylor's recently launched product: a new client that day arrived entirely positioned in STRC and immediately asked to exit. Ram called the bid "not a healthy" one, distinct from genuine institutional demand. Chris remained medium-to-long-term bullish: the Digital Asset Summit in the prior week had its highest-ever institutional signup count, and the CLARITY Act, if passed, would be a "very, very nice tailwind" for the asset class. Crypto's continued resilience relative to equities he attributed to underlying fundamental and regulatory improvements forming beneath the macro overhang.

**The Canton vs. Ethereum Brawl**

Austin described the emerging fight between Canton Network and public chains as revealing fault lines that have been under-discussed as institutional capital floods into crypto. Canton's co-founder Shalk Fear told regulators and institutional buyers that "ZK proofs are too dangerous for mission critical financial infrastructure," citing Solana's April 2025 zero-day on ZK-powered confidential transfers that could have allowed unlimited token minting without anyone knowing if it was exploited. DRW's Don Wilson separately argued that MEV on Ethereum is "not suitable for financial markets." The industry pushed back: Meltem Demirors called it "a special or permissioned interest at work," and Omid Malikhani quipped that MEV was "a risk of blockchain, says guy who backs corporate database alternative where a single entity can steal everybody's money."

Chris framed the conflict as a race for institutional real estate: DTCC has both Canton and Ethereum represented, and nobody is certain which way the market will settle. Canton has added super validators including Visa; TradeWeb and Goldman Sachs are investors. He sees each chain winning for different applications — Ethereum's edge is censorship resistance and decentralization, Solana's is throughput, and Canton's is privacy and permissioned access for institutions who "want to deepen their moat." He noted Canton and Digital Asset Holdings have been around roughly as long as Ethereum, and are "doing great" by solving the needs of their Wall Street investors.

**Austin's Trilemma: RWAs, DeFi, and Legal Reality**

Austin's central argument: there is a fundamental trilemma among permissionless access, real world assets, and complex smart contracts — and you can only pick two. Real world assets are ultimately governed by courts and legal systems, not blockchain consensus. He used Paxos as the example: if a federal judge issues a court order on tokens held by Paxos, compliance is not optional. The ledger's rules will ultimately reflect whatever a federal court says they are. "The ultimate dictator of the real world assets are going to be the courts."

The danger in Austin's framing is that commingled DeFi pools recreate 2008-style systemic risk. If Iranian money enters an Aave lending pool containing USDC, and a judge orders Circle to freeze that USDC, the entire protocol bricks — with cascading effects on innocent participants across the ecosystem. His conclusion: if Ethereum truly wants to be a decentralized permissionless chain, the Ethereum Foundation should be "hammering on people every single day to reduce complexity and interconnectivity" and not building protocols where pulling one Jenga block collapses everything. His explicit bottom line: "If ETH wants to be the global settlement layer that is decentralized and have real world assets, DeFi needs to go away."

Chris pushed back using an internet analogy: the internet is used by Iran too, but regulators target behaviors and activities, not the network layer. You can have centralized stablecoins and applications subject to regulation while maintaining a censorship-resistant layer one. Austin clarified he has no objection to ETH as a pure settlement layer for simple sends or bilateral trades — his objection is the interlayered commingling of multiple centralized entities in shared pools where a problem with one breaks everything for everybody.

**Wall Street's Logic and What Institutions Actually Want**

Ram argued that Wall Street's first instinct with any new platform is co-optation for their own value capture, citing how the same banks that are Canton investors created ICE, TradeWeb, and took Visa and Mastercard public. What institutions want is control, permissioning, and compatibility with existing legal norms — OFAC compliance, trade dispute resolution, and the ability to handle legal disputes. Crypto entrepreneurs, Ram said, "focus too much on product features" and "the features of the car rather than what the customer wants."

Austin raised what he called Canton's governance problem: a private chain funded by a small group of financial entities cannot achieve broad adoption by requiring competitors to donate to each other's blockchain. He noted the DTCC worked because the SEC effectively held all the major players at gunpoint and made them jump simultaneously on day one. Canton hasn't done that. Chris's internet analogy also has a structural limit, Austin and Chris both noted: the internet exists in the U.S. because of the First Amendment — "the government shall pass no law regulating speech" — but there is no equivalent constitutional protection for money. The road to free money is "much higher."

**Where the Real Opportunity Lies**

Austin's broader conclusion is that the blockchain that will handle the majority of financial assets in 30 years probably has not yet been created. There is a vast middle ground between a fully permissionless Ethereum and a fully permissioned Canton — the territory occupied by chains like Stellar and Avalanche — that the market is currently ignoring while everyone fights over the two extremes. Austin also noted the shift from an account-based world to an issuer-based world for tokenized assets as an underappreciated structural change that many financial and regulatory participants are missing. Ram summarized the overarching dynamic: "Code is not law. Law is law. US laws are layer zero." How blockchains adapt to that reality — or fail to — will determine which ones are still relevant in three decades.

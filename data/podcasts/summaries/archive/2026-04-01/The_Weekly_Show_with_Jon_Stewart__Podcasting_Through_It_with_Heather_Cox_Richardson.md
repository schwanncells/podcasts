# The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart — "Podcasting Through It with Heather Cox Richardson"

**Host:** Jon Stewart. **Guest:** Heather Cox Richardson (professor of history, Boston College; author; founder of the "Letters from an American" Substack newsletter).

## TL;DR

- **Trump is acting "extra-constitutionally," not just illegally** — Richardson's clearest example: "We still don't know who was in charge of the Department of Government Efficiency," making it impossible to sue or seek accountability through standard legal channels.
- **1920s Congress parallel** — after Democrats dominated under Wilson, Republicans "didn't know how to take the reins," squabbled between old guard and reformers, and let power flow to Andrew Mellon at Treasury and Herbert Hoover at Commerce; Richardson sees today's Republican Congress as having "punted" the same moment.
- **40 years of deliberate system rigging** — not amorphous drift but specific decisions: deficit-creating tax cuts to limit government capacity, a 1960 memo urging politicians to abandon community rhetoric for transactional coalitions, Florida's 1998 voter roll purge that removed roughly 100,000 people ahead of the 2000 election, Citizens United, partisan gerrymandering, and the Chevron doctrine rollback.
- **People's agency is growing** — Tesla parking lot protests damaged the brand; consumer pressure reversed Jimmy Kimmel's removal from air; Richardson compares this to the 1890s, when similar civic muscles developed and eventually powered the Progressive Era.
- **Podcasts and new local newspapers are the modern penny press** — Richardson draws a direct line from Lincoln-era papers (New York Daily Tribune, Chicago Tribune) to TR-era populist plains newspapers to today's podcasts, describing them as "committees of correspondence from the American Revolution."
- **International realignment is underway** — Italy refused to let US military planes land en route to Iran; Canada's Mark Carney is building a coalition of what he calls "middling countries" to maintain global stability; Trump is inadvertently serving as "a vaccination against far-right populism" in Europe.
- **The "limited pie" vs. "expanding pie" framing** — Richardson argues anti-immigrant resource-guarding traces to 1871 Reconstruction backlash, and that since 1945 more than a billion people worldwide have been lifted out of poverty; the US has grown strongest by treating the world as expanding possibility rather than zero-sum competition.
- **Change needs to be bold, not just mechanical** — overturning Citizens United is necessary but insufficient; Richardson calls for a full rethinking of how democracy functions in a globally connected world, noting China has already electrified its grid while Trump tries to return to 19th-century energy technology.

---

**The Feeling of "Careening Toward Something"**

Stewart opens by describing the current political moment as being on a "bobsled course" hurtling toward an unknown outcome, and says he turns to Richardson to impose a framework on his anxiety. Richardson agrees the feeling of impending cataclysm is real but grounds it historically: people approaching catastrophes rarely see them coming. She recounts researching the 1929 crash and finding a newspaper from the night before dominated by opera coverage and diamond-clad socialites, with a small note about a man who died by suicide because he believed his failing business made him "the only failure in this entire city, country, world." Her point: "if he had just managed to hang on for 12 more hours, he would have realized he was not a failure, that the system had failed and that together they could rebuild it." Stewart picks up on this, noting that cataclysm always seems to arrive "draped in finery" — the Titanic's grand ballroom, the opera on the eve of Black Thursday.

**Trump as Extra-Constitutional Actor, Not Just Lawbreaker**

Stewart describes Trump's distinguishing feature as not ideology but disinterest in consequences. Richardson sharpens this: Trump was not merely acting illegally, he was acting "as if there wasn't a constitution" — operating extra-constitutionally, which makes him far harder to fight through normal legal channels. DOGE is the clearest example: "We still don't know who was in charge of the Department of Government Efficiency. How do you sue anybody if you literally do not know who was in charge of it?" Richardson calls Trump "cuckoo for Cocoa Puffs" and frames him not as a politician but as a salesman who recognized that "40 years of Republican rhetoric had created a population that he could exploit." Stewart's framing: Trump is not testing limits the way he did in term one; now "he's just exploiting them" at speed, a "wrecking ball that operates simultaneously with a sort of reality distortion field."

Richardson also develops the media-subsidiary frame: the White House, according to reporting, runs as a daily TV show that Trump must "win at the end of every night," governing through Truth Social posts and hour-by-hour shifting narrative. She notes that this approach was always going to collide eventually with material reality, and that the Iran situation is "the first time I've seen him not be able to just move on."

**A Feckless Congress and the Flow of Power**

Stewart asks whether Congress, as the constitutional system's first safeguard, is capable of reining Trump in. Richardson draws a parallel to the early 1920s: Republicans took over after the Wilson era unable to govern, squabbling between old guard figures like Henry Cabot Lodge and reformers like Fighting Bob LaFollette. With Congress paralyzed, power flowed to the cabinet — specifically Andrew Mellon at Treasury and Herbert Hoover at Commerce. Mellon "was making sure his name was on everything," filling progressive era regulatory institutions with businessmen and reorienting the government around "the business of America is business."

Richardson sees today's Republican Senate as having "punted" a similar opportunity to stabilize the country, while the House has been "laughable" in its disorganization. The Supreme Court has been "grabbing for a ton of power for a while." But she identifies an opening: the American people waking up and saying, "if you guys aren't going to be using your power for us, we kind of like it back."

**How the System Was Deliberately Rigged**

Stewart presses whether the hollowing out of government's relationship to ordinary people was structural drift or deliberate strategy. Richardson is clear: deliberate. She traces a lineage from Reagan through specific mechanisms. Tax cuts created deficits that limited the government's capacity to act for people and divorced citizens from having real stakes in their government. A famous 1960 memo advised Republican and Democratic politicians to stop arguing about community and democracy ("we all agree about that") and to build transactional coalitions instead. By 1986, Republicans under Reagan were already deploying "ballot integrity" language. Florida's 1998 voter roll purge knocked roughly 100,000 people off the rolls ahead of the 2000 election. Citizens United channeled money toward those who already had it. Partisan gerrymandering made Democratic victories nearly impossible in certain states. The Chevron decision stripped regulatory agencies of their authority.

The cumulative effect was to make Americans feel they had no agency in their government while actual levers shifted toward corporate interests — including foreign corporations, not the immigrants that resource-guarding rhetoric targets.

**The Muscle of Popular Agency and the Role of Storytellers**

Richardson argues that political change happens when widespread frustration finds language and leadership that can voice it. The pattern across American history: dissatisfaction builds, communities form around shared grievances, and "storytellers" arise who articulate what people want their society to look like. Lincoln had the new penny press: the New York Daily Tribune, the New York Times, the Chicago Tribune, the Philadelphia Inquirer. Theodore Roosevelt had populist newspapers that "grew up across the American plains and in the American South like mushrooms after the rain." Today, Richardson sees a direct analog in the proliferation of podcasts and new local newspapers, describing them as "writing campaigns that look like the committees of correspondence from the American Revolution."

The civic muscle she sees strengthening in the present: consumer and protest action. Tesla parking lot protests hurt the brand. Consumer pressure reversed Jimmy Kimmel's removal from air. Richardson compares this emerging capability to the 1890s, when similar muscles eventually powered the Progressive Era.

Stewart raises the Obama question: whether 2008's convergence of financial crisis, Iraq war, and social media was a missed progressive opening, with Trump ultimately seizing those conditions instead. Richardson calls it "certainly possible" but notes the backlash Obama faced looked "a great deal more like the long-term rise of something like the elite Southern enslavers who managed to get a whole bunch of people to stay behind them, even though that economic system was grinding them into the ground." Her broader point: Lincoln, Roosevelt, and FDR were not anointed — "the people really created them." The American people were ready for Trump because 40 years of conditioning had prepared them for his message.

**The Race Question and the Expanding Pie**

Stewart raises the contradiction at the heart of the "entitlement" debate: government programs helping one group are resented as handouts; the same type of program for another group is seen as earned. Richardson identifies the missing word: race. The language of "free riders" and "poor people voting" traces directly to 1871, when Reconstruction opponents reframed racial politics as class politics to build a coalition against federal power. The same logic produces today's "heritage Americans" framing and anti-immigrant resource-guarding — the belief that the country is being "given away to people who don't have shit."

Her counter-argument is that the resource-guarding worldview is simply wrong about how prosperity works. "The reality of the way that people like me think about the world is that the pie is expanding." She cites the post-1945 record: more than a billion people lifted out of poverty. The United States has historically grown strongest by welcoming new people, new ideas, and new labor, treating the world as a space of expanding possibility rather than a zero-sum contest over fixed resources. Retreating to a 1920s model means retreating to a world that "excluded most of us."

**International Realignment and Inadvertent Vaccination**

Some pushback is already coming from abroad. Italy refused to let US military planes land en route to Iran, a signal of how Trump's behavior is damaging allied far-right parties who don't want to be associated with him. Canada's Mark Carney, whom Richardson describes as "an incredibly smart man," is actively building a coalition of what he calls "middle countries" to maintain global stability as the US withdraws from its post-World War II role. Stewart raises the idea that Trump may be functioning as "a vaccination against far-right populism" elsewhere, as other nations see how it operates and pull back from the edge.

Richardson is candid that what has happened to American global standing is "heart-wrenching for those of us who remember a period in which we were really a force for good," and acknowledges the US "did it to ourselves." She allows, however, that "maybe what we will see coming out of this is a fairer order around the world, thanks to people in other countries."

**What Bold Change Actually Looks Like**

Both Stewart and Richardson agree the moment calls for more than mechanical fixes. Richardson says overturning Citizens United and restoring gerrymandering protections are "only mechanics for a reworking of a government and a country really that has been dramatically degraded since the 1970s." She points to climate change as an inescapable challenge, noting with exasperation that China has already electrified its grid while Trump tries to push the US back to 19th-century energy technology. She is watching Carney closely for new models of how democracy can function in a globally integrated, instantaneously connected world.

Her historical frame on the stakes: attempts to monopolize resources and close off social expansion "always, always, always ends up the same way." We just don't know the timeline. Her closing optimism: the current moment may produce the same renewal as prior catastrophes, because Americans across political lines can agree on self-determination. "You can take to the bank the idea that it might make us stronger again and renew our faith in those American principles that led those colonists to throw off the greatest empire at the time in the world."

**Post-Interview Coda**

After Richardson leaves, Stewart and a producer reflect on her value: not raw information but framework, the ability to synthesize disparate news through different eras and "bring it forward to here." They pivot to Trump's proposed White House ballroom — the New York Times reported architectural plans showing no doors, stairs that go nowhere, and views blocked by columns — and connect it to the Iran war: Trump "presented a more thorough case with visual aids about that ballroom than he has in the entirety of this war." Stewart expresses frustration that Trump's supporters are newly troubled by his Iran decisions: "this is the same fucking thing we've been dealing with for 12 years." His phrase: Trump is "a movie trailer president" who "doesn't have the stamina to sit through the whole movie." The show closes with audience Q&A on Trump-renamed government buildings, QAnon's decline, and Stewart's wife.

# Code Switch — "'Mar-a-Lago face:' MAGA's aesthetic loyalty test"

**Hosts:** B.A. Parker and Gene Demby. **Guest:** Ine Oh (Senior News and Engagement Editor, Mother Jones; wrote "In Your Face: The Brutal Aesthetics of MAGA," reporting that included interviews with academics, a plastic surgeon, and others).

## TL;DR

- **"Mar-a-Lago face" is the hyper-feminine, heavily made-up aesthetic dominant among women in Trump's orbit** — long flowing hair, dark smoky eyeliner, conspicuous cosmetic surgery, and sheath dresses that Oh describes as signaling "there is no confusion, there's no ambiguity — this is a woman."
- **The look functions as a résumé line** — Oh argues conforming to Trump's aesthetic preferences signals to "the most powerful man in the United States that you are willing to conform and to appeal to his tastes."
- **Kristi Noem is the central case study** — Oh contends Trump installed Noem as DHS secretary despite no law enforcement credentials because the "soft feminine look" juxtaposed with a "very cruel" mass deportation agenda served a deliberate rhetorical purpose, letting him "decorate his administration with women in power" while enacting anti-women policy.
- **The look is almost exclusively white by design** — Oh says the near-total whiteness of this aesthetic is "very much intentional," and that women of color who gain proximity to MAGA power — she cites Lauren Sanchez — tend to alter their appearance in ways Oh calls "an interesting form of assimilation."
- **Usha Vance represents a possible aesthetic counterpoint** — Oh notes that Vance, who attended Yale Law and built an independent legal career, embodies "the opposite of Mar-a-Lago face," and wonders whether a potential Vance-era administration might shift the look — while acknowledging J.D. Vance's policy alignment with Trump on gender is identical.
- **The look mirrors a centuries-old courtier strategy** — Oh cites the historical example of court followers who artificially blackened their teeth to mimic the genuinely decaying teeth of sugar-consuming monarchs, arguing Mar-a-Lago face "mirrors something as ridiculous as purposely rotting your own teeth."
- **Shelf life is already in question** — Oh points to the buccal fat removal trend, where the "hollow cheeks" look reversed within roughly eight months, as evidence that fast-moving social media beauty trends will eventually leave the aesthetic behind.

**What "Mar-a-Lago Face" Is**

Oh describes the look as hyper-feminine and deliberately unambiguous: long blonde or brunette hair, heavy eye makeup, conspicuous cosmetic surgery, body-hugging clothing, and significant jewelry. She points to Erika Kiefer as an emblematic figure — the dark smoky eye, blonde hair, and prominent jewelry were all visible even in a widely circulated image of Kiefer crying. Oh also notes that an AI-generated "ideal MAGA woman" published by the Washington Post looked to her like "essentially a photocopy" of Kiefer: blonde, heavily made up, wearing military-adjacent gear but still in heels — projecting competence "up to a point" while reassuring viewers "I'm not here trying to take away a man's job."

**Aesthetic as Loyalty Signal and Workplace Credential**

Oh draws on a Yale professor she interviewed to argue that plastic surgery and aesthetic conformity have always functioned as membership signals, but that the MAGA context is unusual because membership is being sought within the federal government — a workplace that, in theory, should not reward appearance. She compares the dynamic to fraternity branding: the look "shows your potential employer, your boss, the most powerful man in the United States, that you are willing to conform." Oh also references the reality show *Members Only*, about women seeking and holding membership at Mar-a-Lago, noting that participants share the same overdone aesthetic and can immediately identify "my people" on sight.

**The Noem Contradiction: Feminine Aesthetics and Cruel Policy**

Oh argues that Kristi Noem's appointment as DHS secretary — despite having no prior law enforcement experience beyond serving as governor of South Dakota — illustrates how the look is weaponized ideologically. Placing a woman with a "soft feminine" presentation in charge of mass deportations allowed Trump to rebut accusations of sexism while pursuing aggressively anti-women policy. Oh calls this "an inherent contradiction" that is entirely intentional: the administration can point to women in high positions while the underlying agenda remains hostile to women's autonomy. The most notorious image of Noem's tenure, Oh says, was her photographed in front of cages at an ICE detention center in El Salvador, wearing a Rolex and heavy makeup — the juxtaposition was, in Oh's view, the point.

**Women of Color and Assimilation**

Oh says the near-total whiteness of the Mar-a-Lago aesthetic is deliberate, and that women of color navigating MAGA-adjacent spaces face a distinct set of pressures. She discusses Lauren Sanchez — whom she knew of before Sanchez's relationship with Jeff Bezos elevated her profile — noting that as Sanchez "gained more access, her face began to alter," which Oh frames as assimilation toward an aesthetic standard she did not previously inhabit. Oh observes that despite Sanchez's access to Paris Fashion Week and front-row seats at Dior and the new Chanel collection, critics consistently describe her as looking "really cheap" regardless of what she wears — a dynamic Oh finds both sympathetic and politically revealing. Usha Vance, by contrast, offers a different model: a woman of color with a prominent legal career and an appearance that defies the Mar-a-Lago template, whose potential role as future first lady raises genuine questions about whether the look's centrality would survive a Trump succession.

**Historical Parallels and Long-Term Costs**

Oh opens the comparison to historical courtier behavior through an anecdote her husband sourced: in some historical courts, followers artificially blackened their teeth to mimic the genuinely rotting teeth of monarchs who consumed excessive sugar. She argues the same self-abasing logic drives Mar-a-Lago face — willingness to alter your body in ways that, viewed from outside the echo chamber, register as objectively strange, all to signal affinity with the ruling class. Oh connects this to the buccal fat removal trend, which reversed direction in roughly eight months, leaving people who had undergone the procedure unable to simply "find some fat somewhere else in my body and just go like stick it back in." She expresses genuine uncertainty — and some emotion — about how women who altered themselves to gain access to this administration will feel about those choices decades later, saying she would extend empathy even to those she disagrees with most sharply on policy, like Noem.

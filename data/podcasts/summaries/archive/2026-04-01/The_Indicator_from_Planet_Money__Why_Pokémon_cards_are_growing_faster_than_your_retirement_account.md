# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Why Pokémon cards are growing faster than your retirement account"

**Hosts:** Darian Woods and Rikki Mulvey. **Guest:** Joshua Johnson (co-founder, Card Ladder — tracks collectibles pricing data).

## TL;DR

- **Rare Pokémon cards up ~170% over the past year** — Card Ladder's index of thousands of rare cards shows this outperformance even as gold and the broader stock market declined over the same period.
- **PSA grade 9 vs. 10 is a massive price difference** — a Van Gogh Museum collaboration Pikachu card sells for ~$800 at a PSA 9 but ~$2,500 for a flawless PSA 10; the physical flaw distinguishing them may be a single white dot on a corner.
- **Only ~5% of buyers are buying to play the game** — Card Ladder's Joshua Johnson estimates the vast majority of buyers are collectors or speculators, not players.
- **Modern cards face bubble risk; vintage cards are more defensible** — modern cards are already treated as collectibles from release, so supply is high and prices depend on continued buyer confidence; vintage cards have scarcer high-grade supply because most were played with or discarded.
- **Greater fool dynamics are visible** — some speculators aren't buying because they believe in the value, but because they expect a higher-paying buyer to follow; when that buyer doesn't appear, prices crash.
- **Opening packs has worse expected value than scratch-off lottery tickets** — but the possibility of a big winner drives boxes to sell above retail price on secondary markets.
- **Financial nihilism is fueling the boom** — Johnson argues younger buyers distrust the stock market as "manipulated based on politics" and feel priced out of real estate, making Pokémon cards an appealing alternative bet.
- **No regulatory protection** — trading cards aren't securities, so market manipulation has far fewer guardrails than stocks; "there's not a Pokémon Securities and Exchange Commission."

**Grading and the Premium for Perfection**

The Indicator visited the Denver card show to explore Pokémon cards as an emerging asset class. Joshua Johnson, co-founder of Card Ladder, explains that grading is central to the market. Collectors send cards to Professional Sports Authenticators (PSA), which grades them on a 1–10 scale. The difference between a 9 and a 10 can be nearly invisible — a single white dot on the back corner — but the price gap is enormous. A Van Gogh Museum x Pokémon collaboration Pikachu card sells for roughly $800 at a PSA 9 and close to $2,500 at a PSA 10. Johnson says buyers have a simple philosophy: "They just want to buy the 10s. A 9 obviously has a flaw; I don't want to even have to think about that."

One attendee at the show, Chris Gonzalez, paid $1,750 cash for a PSA 10 Mega Charizard. He said he likes the art and views graded cards as likely to hold value, pointing to YouTubers going viral selling million-dollar cards. He also acknowledged the risk of holding Pokémon as an investment. A few weeks after the interview, his card was listing on eBay for $1,000 more than he paid.

**Speculation vs. Play**

Johnson estimates that only about 5% of buyers purchase card packs to build sets and play the game — "some ridiculously low number." The rest are collectors excited about the characters and art, or speculators chasing price appreciation. This dynamic has pushed prices up but has also made collecting harder for fans with limited budgets. One attendee, Peyton Daigle, said he typically brings $20 to card shows and mostly walks around once the money is gone; the Pokémon boom has pushed prices on his favorite Pokémon, Dragonite, beyond what he can afford.

**Vintage vs. Modern: A Tale of Two Supplies**

Pokémon cards launched about three decades ago, and most early collectors didn't think of them as long-term investments — which is why many were discarded or played with. That created genuinely scarce supply at high PSA grades, and vintage cards have benefited from comparisons to other collectibles with track records: a copy of the comic book featuring Spider-Man's first appearance, graded only a PSA 4, returned roughly 11% per year since 2009.

Modern cards face a different problem. Because collectors now treat cards as potential investments from the moment they're opened, more are immediately graded and listed on eBay. Johnson warns that rising supply combined with rising prices is a classic setup for collapse: "It just requires a lot of the same people continuing to buy the same stuff over and over at these prices. And if there's any sort of loss of confidence, it's going to just come crashing down because there's just so much supply."

**Greater Fools and Pack-Opening Odds**

The episode describes the market dynamic around modern cards as a textbook illustration of the greater fool theory — some buyers aren't purchasing because they think the card is fairly valued, but because they expect someone else will pay even more. When that buyer doesn't materialize, prices fall sharply. The same speculative pull drives pack-opening culture: opening a booster box of Pokémon cards has worse expected payback than scratch-off lottery tickets, but the possibility of a high-value pull keeps demand — and secondary-market box prices — above retail.

**Financial Nihilism and Market Manipulation Risk**

Johnson frames the broader boom through generational economics. Younger buyers feel priced out of real estate ("older generations have all the real estate — they have that unlock") and view the stock market as "a bit manipulated based on politics," leaving them hoping to "make enough money to survive in an apartment." Pokémon cards feel like a market they can access and understand.

But trading card markets carry their own manipulation risks with fewer protections. Because cards aren't securities, it takes only a few coordinated buyers to move prices on specific cards — and there is no regulatory equivalent of the SEC to investigate or penalize it. Johnson is nonetheless bullish on Pokémon as a franchise overall, citing its cultural staying power and the same financial nihilism as a durable demand driver, even while acknowledging "pockets of bubbles" within the market.

**The Community Underneath the Speculation**

The episode closes on a note from Ricardo Morales, a vendor who also buys cards for himself, his sister, and a friend who collects Mewtwo. He says vending "gives me the opportunity to connect with people" and buy cards he wants at a discount. The hosts note that collectors like Morales — driven by affection for the characters and community — are the underlying base of the boom, separate from the speculators chasing returns.

# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Jobs that new college grads are and are not landing"

**Hosts:** Adrian Ma and Darian Woods. **Guest:** Laura Ulrich (director of economic research, Indeed; has data on employer and job-seeker trends across the job listing platform).

## TL;DR

- **178,000 jobs added in March, unemployment dipped to 4.3%** — a rebound from sluggish recent months, raising hopes the labor market is picking up.
- **"Low hire, low fire" is the defining phrase of the current market** — employers aren't cutting many jobs, but aren't adding many either; stagnation hits entry-level job seekers hardest.
- **Recent college grad unemployment sits at 5.6%** — per the Federal Reserve Bank of New York, covering those with a bachelor's degree or higher.
- **Field matters far more than degree level** — data science postings are down 30% compared to pre-COVID; civil engineering postings are up 40%.
- **Tech disruption is real and multifactored** — AI uncertainty, sector overhiring, and broader instability have made computer science, data analytics, and business harder fields to enter.
- **Healthcare, engineering, and skilled trades remain strong** — associate-degree holders going into plumbing or electrical work still face abundant opportunities.
- **Uncertainty is the root cause of stagnation** — the Economic Uncertainty Index has been elevated for over a year; if you already have a job, layoff risk is low, but finding a new job is harder than it was two years ago.
- **Ghost jobs and bots are less systemic than feared** — the bigger driver is more applicants per posting, not a surge in fake listings; Indeed is working to filter automated applications.

**The Jobs Report and the "Low Hire, Low Fire" Market**

The March jobs report showed 178,000 positions added and an unemployment rate of 4.3%, a tick down from recent months. Hosts Adrian Ma and Darian Woods note the report raises hopes after a stretch of stagnation economists have described as "low hire, low fire" — a market where employers are neither cutting jobs nor meaningfully adding them. That dynamic is particularly hard on workers trying to enter the labor force for the first time, who depend on new openings rather than job-to-job moves.

**Not All Grads Are in the Same Boat**

Laura Ulrich pushes back on blanket "tough for everyone" framing. The headline 5.6% unemployment rate for recent college grads, she argues, obscures a wide divergence by field. Data science, her own son's field, has seen job postings fall 30% below pre-COVID levels — a combination of AI-related uncertainty, tech sector overhiring, and broader disruption. Computer science, data analytics, and business have all softened. Civil engineering, by contrast, has posted a 40% increase versus pre-COVID. Healthcare fields and skilled trades are similarly strong — community college graduates ready to work as electricians or plumbers still have plenty of options. Ulrich frames 2026 as an "adjustment phase" for tech-adjacent majors: the jobs will eventually come, but they'll take longer to land and may pay less than graduates expected.

**Economic Uncertainty and What's Driving Employer Caution**

Ulrich ties employer hesitation directly to elevated uncertainty, citing the Economic Uncertainty Index as having run well above historical norms over the past year. She notes that in this environment business leaders struggle to make hiring and expansion decisions — a dynamic she describes as unsurprising from an economics standpoint. Tariff policy from the Trump administration is one contributor to that uncertainty, alongside AI's unpredictable effects on workforce planning. The practical result: if you already hold a job, layoff risk remains low outside a handful of targeted sectors. If you're unemployed and searching, the market is meaningfully harder than it was two years ago.

**Ghost Jobs, Application Bots, and the Volume Problem**

Students at Howard University told Ma and Woods they feel they're competing not just with other applicants but with bots auto-submitting applications — and encountering ghost job listings that may not correspond to real openings. Ulrich says Indeed's data doesn't show a large increase in fake postings. The more significant dynamic, she argues, is that more people are searching, which means more applicants per genuine job — leaving more people who never hear back. Automated application tools are increasing overall volume, and Indeed is actively working to filter them, but Ulrich frames this as an operational challenge for platforms rather than a systemic fraud problem.

**What Students at Howard Are Experiencing**

Monica Maya, a Howard senior in chemical engineering, had applied to roughly 50 positions over six months without landing an offer as of the interview. She had originally been eligible to graduate a year earlier but chose to stay for a fifth year because the job market wasn't yielding what she'd hoped. Sanyu Lakoma, a junior in broadcast journalism, said the anxiety among her graduating friends is driven by a dual pressure: a harder market and rising costs of living. Ulrich's advice to job seekers, including her own children: stay active, keep networking, and be prepared for the first job to look different than expected — because the market will eventually shift.

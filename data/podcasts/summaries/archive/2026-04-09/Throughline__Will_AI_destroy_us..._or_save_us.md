# Throughline — "Will AI destroy us... or save us?"

**Hosts:** Randh Abdel-Fattah, Ramteen Arablui. **Guests:** George Zarkadakis (PhD in AI, author of *In Our Own Image: Will AI Save Us or Destroy Us?*); Francis Collins (physician-geneticist, former director of the National Institutes of Health under three presidents, led the Human Genome Project); Stephanie Dick (assistant professor of communication at Simon Fraser University, PhD in history of science, specializing in mathematics and computing); Meredith Broussard (data journalism professor at NYU, author of *More Than a Glitch: Confronting Race, Gender, and Ability Bias in Tech*); Gary Kasparov (former world chess champion, undefeated for 15 years).

## TL;DR

- **AI is fundamentally mathematics, not magic or destiny** — "AI is not a magic wand, but it's not a Terminator." It emerges from beautiful math to predict patterns and advance human progress, but the desire to control it is deeply entangled with the human desire to control ourselves, the world, and institutions.
- **Hollywood sci-fi has shaped AI research since the beginning** — The Dartmouth Conference (1956), which birthed the field, was driven by engineers and scientists who wanted to make sci-fi real. They named their field "artificial intelligence" aspirationally, modeling their ambitions on books, comics, and films rather than on careful empirical practice.
- **The founding vision was disembodied and homogeneous** — Early pioneers, mostly white men from elite institutions, embedded their biases directly into the field. They decided chess skill was a universal marker of intelligence and reduced the Turing Test to "telling a convincing lie." This framework erased relationship, embodiment, and the colonial legacies underlying the whole project.
- **Deep Blue defeating Kasparov (1997) marked the end of human-machine competition** — Kasparov had never lost a match before; Deep Blue could analyze 200 million positions per second. After losing, Kasparov recognized "the history of us competing with machines will be over soon." Humans began asking: what is uniquely human?
- **The Human Genome Project showed DNA is an information molecule we can read and edit** — Francis Collins mapped 3 billion base pairs and decoded the "book of life" shared by all living things. The breakthrough raised profound questions: Can we find and replace genetic errors like a typo? What does it mean that we can now read our own instruction book?
- **Machine learning flipped the script: let data teach the machine** — Instead of encoding human knowledge upfront, researchers feed massive datasets to neural networks and let the computer find mathematical patterns. This shift, combined with cheaper hardware and proliferating data, ushered in modern AI's exponential growth.
- **AI systems embed the worst of us without adding wisdom** — Models trained on historical data inherit patterns that encode human bias, escalation, and the desire to control others. They lack the embodied, relational intelligence that comes from living with consequences. A machine mirror shows us what we've become, not what we could be.

**Why We Create AI: The Big Bang of the Human Mind**

George Zarkadakis traces the impulse to create artificial intelligence back to a fundamental human drive. As an animal, humans are weak; our only power is our collective and individual minds. For 300,000 years, humans survived through social structure and storytelling. Then, roughly 40,000 years ago, something shifted—what Zarkadakis calls "the Big Bang of the human mind." Humans started creating art, narrating stories about their experience, and transferring knowledge across generations. Through these stories, we project our hopes, fears, and dreams onto the invisible unknown. This capacity for narrative—the ability to imagine ourselves into other forms, other futures—became the engine of human progress.

This imagination shaped every breakthrough that followed. When geneticist Francis Collins was growing up in the 1950s, Life magazine covers announced the discovery of DNA's structure as "the secret of life." The double helix was proof that all living things shared a common ancestor and that this information molecule, though ancient, still contained infinite possibility. Similarly, when George Zarkadakis saw Robbie the Robot in *Forbidden Planet* as a child in an open-air cinema in Athens, he encountered the idea that we could build artifacts—artificial things—that could think and behave like us. These stories—science fiction, genetic code, myths—became the roadmaps that inspired scientists and engineers to pursue creation.

**The Birth of Artificial Intelligence: Aspiration Over Empiricism**

The Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence in 1956 is often presented as the moment AI was born. Ten men—mathematicians and scientists from top-tier universities and labs—gathered in Hanover, New Hampshire for a two-month workshop. John McCarthy, the conference's instigator, wrote a proposal that made a sweeping claim: "Every aspect of learning or any other feature of intelligence can, in principle, be so precisely described that a machine can be made to simulate it." From the very beginning, there was a pronouncement that human learning and intelligence could be mechanized and automated.

Meredith Broussard notes that the field's name itself was chosen aspirationally. Most of the pioneers were "great consumers and lovers of science fiction," and there was "a lot of desire to make science fiction real." They wanted to build a sentient machine. But the conference was, in practice, a flop—full of conflict, tension, and disagreement, with no coherent field emerging. What did emerge was an origin myth, one that served to empower these men to tell their own story. Zarkadakis emphasizes that "the challenge is not to act automatically; it's to find an action that is not automatic." Yet the field that grew from Dartmouth was built on the opposite assumption: that the best of human thinking could be automated away.

**The Hidden Genealogy: Industrial Devaluation and Control**

The deeper history of AI reaches back to the Industrial Revolution. In the late 1700s and early 1800s, factories reshaped the nature of work. Charles Babbage, an English mathematician touring factories, realized they could teach us something about the human mind—namely, how processes could be broken down into elementary steps. But this insight came with a shadow: as machines took over repetitive labor, the humans doing that labor were devalued. Babbage was dismissive of working-class people, calling them "annoying and filthy," and famously said, "I wish to God these calculations had been produced by steam"—by the steam engine, not by human hands.

This devaluation—the equation of human labor with mechanical steps—was embedded in AI from the start. Broussard argues that "the blind spots of the creators" were "reflected in the technological artifacts that they create." The early pioneers, mostly white men educated at elite institutions, decided that chess skill was a universal marker of intelligence. They embedded their own biases directly into the machines they built. The Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing, reduced intelligence to "telling a convincing lie"—to putting on a performance of being something you're not. From the very beginning, with this disembodied conception of intelligence, the field left out embodiment, relationship, society, and all the colonial legacies of "reserving reason for only certain kinds of people and certain kinds of thinking."

**The Dream of Mechanizing Mind: Post-War Rationalism and Control**

After World War II, the atomic bomb introduced existential dread into Western consciousness. But some elite academics and scientists believed that better technology was the key to humanity's future—that advanced machines could help us bypass the messy parts of being human. What if human decision-making was too slow? What if people's judgments were clouded by emotion? Advanced machines could churn out the right answers, the right decisions, the right judgments. In effect, they would "replace God with science."

This confidence was fueled by Cold War fears. Nuclear deterrence relied on information processing capability—knowing where enemy arsenals were, knowing if an attack was coming. The argument went that if the United States could build a system that could think faster and more rationally than generals and admirals, it would have a clear advantage over the Soviet Union. Fear that humans were too limited to preserve peace drove a "double down on high technological hyper-rationalism." That is how artificial intelligence came about.

**The Turing Test and the Erasure of Embodiment**

Herbert Simon, one of the field's key figures, claimed that "human minds and modern digital computers are, quote-unquote, species of the same genus." They are fundamentally the same—symbol processing machines that take symbolic information as input, manipulate it according to rules, and output decisions and solutions. From this perspective, "bodies don't matter. Society doesn't matter." This disembodied vision of intelligence won out, and it came with a profound cost. Broussard argues that what is "shocking about the Turing test is that it reduces intelligence to telling a convincing lie, to putting on the performance of being something that you are not."

**Deep Blue, Kasparov, and the Receding Horizon of Humanness**

For decades after Dartmouth, the field promised imminent breakthroughs. Instead, there were cycles of hype and disappointment, eventually called "AI winters." Funding dried up. The early pioneers had underestimated the complexity of humans and overestimated the capabilities of machines.

By the 1990s, chess remained the supreme test. In 1997, the reigning world chess champion Gary Kasparov faced IBM's Deep Blue in Manhattan. Kasparov, who had never lost a match, was confident. Deep Blue could analyze 200 million positions per second. The match was tense and evenly played. In the end, Deep Blue won after Kasparov's 19th move in the sixth game. Kasparov resigned, calling it a "historic moment, marking the first time a machine had defeated a reigning world champion in a match under tournament conditions." He was "furious and wanted to come back and just to tear this machine down." But within months, he recognized "the history of us competing with machines will be over soon."

Kasparov's loss marked a turning point. Humans stopped trying to compete with machines at games and started asking a different question: What is uniquely human? Is it poetry? Intuition? Creativity? Emotion? As each activity was automated, humans redefined their humanness again and again. This pattern—what Broussard calls "the receding horizon"—continues today.

**Reading the Book of Life: DNA and the Genome Project**

While chess computers were making headlines, Francis Collins was pursuing a different kind of revelation. Growing up in the 1950s, Collins was inspired by the discovery of DNA's structure. The idea that all living things shared a common information molecule—that this chemical blueprint could be read, understood, and potentially edited—suggested that we could unlock the secrets of life itself.

Collins led the Human Genome Project, a multinational effort to decode all 3 billion base pairs of human DNA. When the project succeeded in 2000, Collins stood in the East Room of the White House with 2,400 scientists behind him. They had done something unprecedented: they read their own instruction book. Collins called it "truly a humbling and profound experience." But he also grappled with what this meant. Some worried that sequencing the human genome would "diminish humanity by taking the mystery out of life." Collins disagreed. Yes, "we're chemical computers. This is the program that runs us." But "nothing could be further from the truth" that understanding our genetics diminishes us. There is "a deep desire for the human condition not to be a deterministic output of our chemical or genetic or cultural forces but for there to be something that allows for free will and surprise and creativity that belongs to us."

**The Machine Learning Revolution: Data Over Design**

During the same era, a breakthrough in artificial intelligence's foundations reshaped the entire field. Instead of trying to model the human mind first or encode human knowledge upfront, researchers asked: What if we let the computer learn on its own? Neural networks, trained on massive datasets and given access to cheap computing power, could find mathematical patterns in data without being explicitly programmed.

Broussard explains the method: "You get a ton of data and you feed it into the computer and you say, computer, I want you to create a model of the patterns that you see in the data. And the computer very obligingly makes a model of the mathematical patterns that it sees in the data." This approach, called machine learning, worked. The hardware got cheaper, massive datasets became feasible to store and process, and the field entered a new era of exponential growth. But this shift came with its own blind spots: the machines learned the patterns in the data, including all the biases, inequalities, and structural injustices embedded in human history.

**What Computers Are—And What They Aren't**

As AI systems grew more powerful, some people began to treat them as wise or quasi-sentient. But Broussard emphasizes a simple truth: "Computers are machines that do math. They compute." When you build a computer program, "it'll work if you do it this way, and then it won't work if you do it the other way. But that's not how culture operates, right? That's not how relationships work. So there's a really a fundamental difference between what we can do with computers and what we can do in society." Math is a system of symbolic logic; it is not "the indefinable thing that makes us human."

Yet AI systems are increasingly embedded in decisions that matter deeply: medical diagnoses, criminal sentences, hiring, warfare. These are domains where embodied wisdom, relationships, and understanding of consequences matter. A model trained on historical data will encode the biases of that history. A system optimized for efficiency will choose escalation over restraint. A machine without stake in the outcome will lack the moral intuition that comes from living with consequences.

**The Mirror and the Choice**

George Zarkadakis offers a closing image: "The machine is like a mirror, and if you don't like what we see in the mirror, you have two choices. Either you can work on your body to improve the picture, or you can try to distort the mirror. The latter decision is just, it's a recipe for disaster." In other words, if AI reflects back to us what we have built, then the question is not whether AI will destroy us or save us. The question is what we choose to become.

Zarkadakis remains optimistic, shaped by his experience growing up in the Soviet Union and witnessing the collapse of authoritarianism. "The history of humanity gives us reasons to be optimistic," he says. But optimism is not fate. "I'd like to imagine a future where we have built, we have developed human systems that bring the best out of us rather than the worst out of us." That future depends on choice—on recognizing that machines cannot think like humans and that humans still have the monopoly on both good and evil. The work ahead is to ensure that our tools amplify the best of us, not the worst.

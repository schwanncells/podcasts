# Unchained — "Decks in the City: How the SEC's Crypto Task Force Is Rebuilding Trust with Builders"

**Host:** Katherine KK (general counsel at Starkware; fluent in TradFi, conversant in deep tech). **Guests:** Jesse Brooks (Web3 prosecutor turned Web3 protector, Rivet Capital); V (former SEC staff to Web3); SEC Commissioner Hester Peirce (SEC commissioner); Sumera Yunus (chief of operations, SEC Crypto Task Force).

## TL;DR

- **SEC Crypto Task Force launched over a year ago with a "rebuild trust" mandate** — the agency came out of an environment where industry feared engagement. The task force issued roundtables, written input reviews (hundreds of submissions), and is now in the "pens down" phase of drafting rulemaking and commission statements.
- **Small builders need clear rules quickly; large companies want input on policy** — small teams can't afford lawyers and lack funding runway, so they ask "tell us what to do." Larger market participants invest in advocacy and policy shaping. The SEC is designing compliance pathways proportionate to fundraise size to avoid creating a $2M+ legal barrier to entry.
- **Tokenized securities are a key priority, along with clearer stablecoin positioning** — Peirce and Yunus emphasized that stablecoins are not securities under the law. The SEC is working on rulemaking that addresses gaps where clarity is needed and gaps where Congress may need to legislate.
- **Disclosure frameworks in crypto should differ from traditional equities** — Yunus noted that smart contracts and layered disclosure using AI could make regulatory documents more digestible and interactive for retail investors, beyond the current text-heavy prospectus model.
- **SEC–CFTC coordination is now formal and habitual, not just personality-driven** — the two chairs (Atkins and Selig) have a strong relationship, but staff-level engagement is being built to survive administration changes. Past conflicts (one agency saying no while another said yes) created market confusion; the MOU aims to prevent that.
- **Future-proofing happens through rulemaking (durability) and market adoption** — Peirce emphasized that the more builders actually use new products and services, the harder it is for a future administration to reverse direction. The window for building is now; the task force is moving "as fast as we can."

**The Crypto Task Force's Mission and Operating Model**

Sumera Yunus describes the Crypto Task Force's mandate as a response to an environment where industry feared regulatory engagement. The initial priority was to rebuild trust through roundtables—in-person gatherings where practitioners, developers, and other stakeholders could have foundational conversations with the SEC. The task force published all roundtable recordings on its website and used AI analysis (querying transcripts to extract specific speakers' concerns and timestamps) to help staff identify subject-matter experts and track repeated themes across conversations.

Yunus outlines three phases of the task force's work. First was the roundtables and intake phase—reviewing hundreds of written submissions and developing relationships. Second was issuing "low-hanging fruit" guidance in areas where clarity existed without needing new rulemaking. Third, the current phase, is drafting rulemaking and commission statements on more complex issues. She notes: "The staff is hard at work on doing that." Commissioner Peirce adds that roundtables are one input among many; she reviews them repeatedly because "there's a lot" to digest, and written submissions and ongoing meetings provide additional context.

**Small Builders vs. Large Companies: Divergent Regulatory Pressures**

Peirce identifies a key asymmetry in regulatory needs. Small teams, unable to afford lawyers, essentially ask the SEC to "tell us what to do so we can build." Their funding is running out and their timeline is constrained. Larger market participants, by contrast, have the bandwidth to shape policy and want input into how rules should work. Yunus reframes this as complementary: large players with deeper pockets can invest in legal analysis and advocacy, and smaller teams can leverage the results. However, the SEC is conscious of creating barriers to entry. Yunus emphasizes that guidance is drafted to be broadly applicable and rulemaking will be "proportionate to the amount of money you're raising"—so a $100K fundraise should not require the same compliance infrastructure as a $100M raise.

The task force implemented "Crypto on the Road" to meet smaller teams outside DC, and Yunus stresses that the SEC has an open-door policy. No special access pass or law firm intermediary is required; practitioners can request meetings via the crypto task force website or submit written comments (informal emails are acceptable). Peirce notes she has responded to founders' tweets leading to coffee meetings—emphasizing that regulatory access should be available to anyone with a serious, developed idea.

**Tokenization, Stablecoins, and Jurisdictional Clarity**

Tokenization of securities emerges as a major priority. Peirce notes it is "clearly within our remit" and "touches on a lot of different things," making it a natural focus. By contrast, stablecoins themselves are positioned as outside the SEC's purview—Peirce states: "Those stable coins are not securities." However, yield products built on stablecoins (like tokenized money market funds) may draw SEC attention. Yunus clarifies that the SEC has taken "the position which is consistent with the statute" on stablecoins.

On the broader question of how to prioritize amid an asset class that encompasses "apples and oranges" (NFTs, L2s, DeFi, etc.), Peirce explains the SEC's triage: first, determine what falls outside SEC jurisdiction; second, identify what falls inside; third, tackle the technically or legally challenging areas that require rulemaking. Some guidance can be issued quickly; rulemaking simply takes longer.

**Disclosure and the Future of Investor Information**

Both Peirce and Yunus stress that disclosure standards in crypto must be calibrated to the investor's needs. Peirce emphasizes that "the market shouldn't wait for regulators to tell them what good information is"—the private sector is already developing standards. She notes that disclosure needs in crypto likely differ from traditional equity markets. Yunus, who has spent most of her career writing and reviewing disclosure documents, agrees. She points out that the current disclosure regime creates an "information asymmetry problem especially heightened in a market where there's no requirements right at this point."

Yunus is enthusiastic about leveraging smart contracts and AI to improve disclosures beyond the traditional prospectus model. She sees opportunities to "layer disclosure, use AI to work through different disclosures, and make it interactive." This could make documents more digestible and accessible to retail investors, addressing the critique that regulatory documents are written in a language inaccessible to non-lawyers.

**SEC–CFTC Coordination and Avoiding Market Confusion**

Peirce and Yunus discuss a new Memorandum of Understanding (MOU) between the SEC and CFTC aimed at harmonizing regulation of cryptocurrencies. V notes that practitioners were previously confused when the two agencies took conflicting positions on the same product—a situation that was "very difficult" for compliance teams. The new approach prioritizes routine communication and coordination to prevent one agency from approving something the other opposes.

Peirce explains the historical context: in the Dodd-Frank era, regulatory harmonization on security-based swaps and swaps took years to formalize. She stresses that the current chairs (Atkins and Selig) are "both very committed to sensible approaches and they're not territorial." However, she cautions that chair-level relationships won't always be this strong. Yunus emphasizes that the foundation is being built at the staff level—ongoing engagement between SEC and CFTC staff—so harmonization survives administration changes. She notes: "Staff is going to outlast administrations in most cases."

Yunus acknowledges that industry feedback is essential; practitioners encountering duplicative reporting, gaps, or inefficiencies should highlight them to help the agencies refine the process. V adds that cross-agency communication reduces internal inefficiencies and allows knowledge-sharing on an emerging technology where expertise is concentrated.

**Future-Proofing: Rulemaking, Market Adoption, and Institutional Commitment**

The conversation turns to the risk that a future administration might reverse the crypto task force's guidance and rules. Peirce offers a two-part answer. First, rulemaking is more durable than guidance because it's harder to depart from rules than change commission statements. Second, and more importantly, "the more people are actually using your products and services, the harder it will be for any new administration to change." She urges builders to use the current regulatory window wisely: "This is the moment where you should be really trying to build stuff."

Yunus emphasizes that the framework is not a "10th floor initiative" (referring to the executive level) but is being built across the entire SEC—all policy divisions are contributing intellectual rigor to crypto regulation, approaching it as "just another asset class" rather than a special project. This diffusion of responsibility makes the framework more resilient to personnel changes. She notes: "When leadership changes, if staff has been involved across every policy division… the work is going to go on."

**Personal Reflections and Legacy**

In closing remarks, Peirce emphasizes her pride in the talented staff she has hired, naming Yunus as a standout. Yunus, in an emotional moment, describes Peirce as setting "the standard for what a public servant should be" and credits her leadership and integrity as the reason industry responded to the task force's outreach. She reflects: "She has modeled courage and integrity, and I will benefit from that for any step I take moving forward."

Peirce's closing message to builders is: "Never give up. There will be doors and then there will be walls, and that will always be the case. You have to keep going." Yunus adds that builders drive innovation and that the US is "the best place in the world" because of people willing to create and dream of better futures.

Both emphasize that ongoing engagement from industry is essential. Peirce states: "Please do continue to engage with us. That's the only way that we can get to a good place." Controversial issues will arise, and not everyone will be satisfied, but the process is iterative. If industry sees gaps or problems with proposed rules, the path forward is clear: explain the legal basis and practical realities, and help shape the next step.

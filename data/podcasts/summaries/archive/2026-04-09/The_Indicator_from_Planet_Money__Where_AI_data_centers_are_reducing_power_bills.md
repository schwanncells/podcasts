**The Scope of the Problem: Data Centers and Rising Electricity Bills**

The episode opens with Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez introducing a bill to pause new AI data center construction. Among their central complaints is electricity costs. According to Bloomberg, electricity prices have more than tripled in areas close to data centers over the last five years. These price increases have fueled local opposition and blocked billions of dollars' worth of proposed data center projects. Maine's House recently passed initial approval on pausing most data center construction in the state, with both the Senate and governor supporting the measure, making passage likely.

**J.J. Assyria's Defense: Data Centers as Grid Contributors, Not Culprits**

J.J. Assyria, a utilities consultant who works directly with electricity companies serving data centers, presents a provocative counterargument. He contends that data centers are unfairly blamed for more than a decade of underinvestment in the U.S. power grid. Assyria argues that America's electricity grid has been underfunded for 15 years, predating the AI boom. Problems include power lines vulnerable to storms and wildfires, and major renewable projects unable to connect to the existing infrastructure.

Assyria points out that data centers, by contrast, are actively seeking new ways to acquire power. Many build their own on-site power generation through long-term deals with providers or co-located generators. Some facilities include natural gas combined-cycle plants or even smaller nuclear options. This on-site generation, Assyria suggests, could provide spinoff benefits: excess power can feed back into the grid if the data center doesn't need all of it. Additionally, data centers pay for fixed grid costs—maintaining power lines, clearing overgrown trees, building substations—that utilities must cover regardless of individual consumption. When more electricity is generated and sold across the grid, these fixed costs are distributed across a larger base, lowering the per-unit cost for all consumers, similar to economies of scale in manufacturing.

Assyria's thesis finds some empirical support. Earlier in 2026, PG&E, California's major power company, announced that data center growth had helped it cut electricity rates by 13 percent since 2024, citing the way data centers contribute to paying for more fixed infrastructure costs.

**Philip Krine's Pushback: The Speed Problem**

Philip Krine, a professor of electrical and computer engineering at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, rejects Assyria's framing. Krine argues that data centers are walking in demanding 20% more capacity than currently exists, which is "not a normal situation." The problem is not primarily years of underinvestment, but rather the sheer speed at which data centers are requesting new capacity. He contends that "data centers and computing basically are sucking up 100% of potential future capacity," and this surge is being reflected directly into current rate bases, causing higher electricity bills in many regions.

Krine notes that not all areas benefit from Assyria's optimistic scenario. Northern Virginia, for example, already has a strained electricity grid operating near existing capacity. A new data center there requires entirely new grid investments—high-voltage transmission lines, new substations—that drive up bills for all ratepayers.

**The Historical Precedent and Path Forward**

Krine acknowledges that the U.S. power grid has expanded dramatically in the past. Between the late 1920s and late 1970s, electricity generation and transmission capacity grew by a factor of 12. He argues this historical precedent shows that a 20-30% expansion is achievable. However, the national grid essentially stopped growing in the 1980s because appliances and factories became more energy efficient, reducing demand pressures. Now, with growth from electric vehicles, air conditioning, home heating conversions to electric, and AI data centers, demand is surging again.

Krine identifies the core challenge: achieving this growth requires the right financial incentives to make it economically attractive for generators to build new capacity and transmit it to customers. One concrete obstacle is that U.S. electricity markets are fragmented regionally—evening power demand in New York cannot tap cheap afternoon solar power from Texas because no transmission infrastructure exists to move power across state lines. Building massive cross-country transmission lines could solve this problem, but projects face multiple jurisdictional hurdles and layers of approval. Despite these permitting obstacles, Krine insists the challenge is surmountable if the U.S. draws on past experience and implements the necessary infrastructure investments.

**Conclusion: An Opportunity Amid Disagreement**

Both Assyria and Krine agree on one point: the current spike in power demand—whether viewed as a problem to manage or an opportunity to harness—presents a chance to build a better, more robust electricity grid. Assyria sees data centers as part of the solution through on-site generation and infrastructure investment. Krine sees them as a pressure test that, if met with proper planning and investment, could catalyze long-overdue grid modernization.

# Planet Money — "Dark times for Cuba's economic experiment"

**Hosts:** Erika Barris and Nick Fountain. **Guests:** Ricardo Torres (economist, American University; born and raised in Cuba; taught economics in Havana before emigrating to the U.S. in 2021); Yasser Gonzalez Cabrera (bicycle tour operator and founder of Citicleta, Havana — featured via voice notes as an on-the-ground source).

## TL;DR

- **Cuba is experiencing island-wide blackouts** after the U.S. blocked nearly all oil shipments since January 2026 — on multiple occasions the entire country has lost power for more than a full day, halting buses, food delivery, medical transport, and internet.
- **Cuba's 60+ year economic strategy has alternated between leaning on communist compadres and flirting with capitalist frenemies**, but the U.S. oil embargo is now making both strategies simultaneously impossible.
- **The Soviet Union's 1991 collapse erased Cuba's primary benefactor overnight** — Cuba's GDP fell 35% during the resulting "special period," triggering mass raft migrations to the U.S. and forcing Cuba's first capitalist experiment in 1993.
- **Venezuela replaced the Soviets as Cuba's oil lifeline in 2000**, trading discounted oil for Cuban teachers, doctors, and sports coaches; this stabilized Cuba's economy and funded a tourism-led growth phase.
- **The Obama-era tourism boom briefly transformed Cuba's private sector** — Yasser's Havana bike tours were drawing 400 tourists per month at their peak, with Americans, Germans, Dutch, Colombians, and Mexicans all visiting.
- **Three consecutive shocks destroyed that growth engine**: Venezuela began sending less oil starting in 2016; Trump reimposed sanctions and travel restrictions in 2017; the pandemic halted travel entirely. Yasser went from 400 tourists per month to 25 customers for all of 2024, and zero paying customers in 2025.
- **Nearly 3 million Cubans have left since 2020** — roughly a quarter of the population — including Ricardo Torres himself, who left in 2021 after deciding he had contributed enough to a system he could no longer influence.
- **The Trump administration cut off Venezuela's oil pipeline to Cuba** by capturing Venezuela's president and effectively taking over its oil industry, then warned other potential suppliers like Mexico with tariffs if they sold oil to Cuba.
- **Ricardo Torres concludes Cuba has only one exit**: direct negotiation with the United States, which has become, in his words, "a very ferocious frenemy."

**The Current Crisis: Blackouts, Voice Notes, and a Bike Business with No Tourists**

Erika Barris opens the episode by describing conditions in Cuba in early 2026: doctors unable to reach hospitals, buses not running, food and medicine delivery disrupted, and blackouts so severe that the country has lost power for more than a full day at a stretch. She has been interviewing Cubans through voice notes because cell service and internet are too unreliable for live calls.

Among her sources is Yasser Gonzalez Cabrera, founder of Citicleta, a Havana-based bicycle tour company. Yasser records his messages whenever electricity returns, sometimes at 3 a.m. His voice ranges from conversational — birds in the background, music playing — to what Barris describes as sounding "like he's just sitting by himself in the dark." He worries most about his parents in rural areas, where the government is prioritizing urban electricity; his mother now goes to bed at seven when the sun goes down. At the peak of his business, Yasser was running tours for 400 tourists per month. In all of 2024, he had 25 customers. In 2025, he has had none.

**Cuba's Recurring Strategy: Compadres and Capitalist Frenemies**

Ricardo Torres, who grew up in Cuba and spent years teaching economics there before emigrating in 2021, frames Cuba's entire post-revolution history as an oscillation between two survival strategies: relying on communist and socialist allies (compadres) when market economies shut it out, and cautiously opening to capitalism (its frenemy) when the compadres could no longer deliver.

After the 1959 revolution, Cuba became a fully centralized communist economy. The government employed everyone, set all wages, owned all industries, and issued ration books specifying how much food each family could receive. The U.S. imposed what Torres calls "the mother of all embargoes." Cuba's response was to lean on the Soviet Union, which bought Cuban sugar and tobacco at above-market prices and sold Cuba oil at below-market rates — and in return gained a strategic foothold 90 miles from the United States. For decades, this worked. Cuba was poor, but shelter, food, health care, and education were broadly available to its population.

**The Soviet Collapse and Cuba's First Experiment with Capitalism**

When the Soviet Union dissolved in 1991, Cuba lost its primary economic lifeline almost overnight. The resulting "special period" saw Cuba's GDP contract by 35 percent. Food became scarce, and Cubans built rafts from whatever material they could find and attempted to float to the U.S. Torres, who was ten years old when the Soviet Union fell, recalls that many Cubans still believed in what he calls "moral reserves" — faith that socialism would eventually work, that the Soviets had simply abandoned them. Young Torres was less certain, having read a Soviet economics textbook extolling communism's virtues while watching communism's flagship country collapse.

Out of desperation, Cuba introduced its first capitalist experiment in 1993: limited self-employment and small private businesses. The restrictions were severe — employees had to be family members, and the state retained control over prices, production, and core business decisions. Less than 1 percent of workers were in the private sector. Cuba also took its first small steps into tourism and pegged a second currency to the dollar. None of it was enough to reverse the crisis.

**Venezuela Fills the Gap; Tourism as the New Engine**

Around 2000, Cuba found a new primary compadre: Venezuela under Hugo Chavez. The two countries negotiated a bilateral arrangement in which Cuba sent teachers, doctors, and sports coaches to Venezuela, and Venezuela sent oil at favorable prices. Torres describes the trade concisely: "Venezuela sent oil. That was the trade." Venezuelan oil effectively replaced Soviet oil, and Cuba's GDP began growing again.

Under Raul Castro, who took over from his brother Fidel in the late 2000s, Cuba pushed further into its capitalist flirtation. Raul acknowledged publicly that Cuba's economy was failing and that the economic system itself bore some of the responsibility. The private sector expanded: businesses could hire employees outside the family, and the list of permitted private occupations grew to nearly 200, including barbers, fortune tellers, costumed characters posing for tourist photos in Old Havana, and specific musical acts. The government's guiding principle, Torres notes, was that the private sector was welcome "as long as it is a complement to state activity — but it cannot become more important than the state sector."

**The Obama Boom and Yasser's Rise**

The Obama administration's loosening of trade and travel restrictions with Cuba sent tourism into a new phase. In 2016, Obama became the first sitting U.S. president to visit Cuba since the revolution. Luxury fashion house Chanel staged a show in Havana. The Fast and the Furious franchise filmed there. The Rolling Stones held a massive concert in a country where rock and roll had once been prohibited.

Yasser, then in his twenties and working as a software engineer, had been following developments in urban cycling in the U.S. — bike lanes, city-run rentals — and decided Cuba needed the same. He launched Citicleta, leading bicycle tours through Havana's streets. At the peak, he was guiding groups of 400 tourists per month: Americans, Germans, Dutch, Colombians, Mexicans. Torres explains the tourist logic: many visitors wanted to see Cuba before it changed completely, before what Torres wryly describes as "McDonald's in every corner of Cuba like everywhere else."

**Three Shocks and the Unraveling**

Three overlapping shocks dismantled Cuba's tourism-based growth engine. First, Venezuela's economy deteriorated starting in 2016, and the country progressively reduced oil shipments to Cuba. Second, when Trump took office in 2017, he reversed the Obama-era openings, citing Cuba's human rights record and what he described as its role spreading instability. Travel restrictions returned. Third, the pandemic halted global tourism entirely. Torres describes the combined effect as going "from boom to bust, but like without preparation or anticipation of any kind — like in a few months, all gone, almost all gone."

Cuba's then-new president responded by expanding the private sector further, raising the employee cap to 100 workers. Government statistics counted nearly 10,000 of these larger private companies operating in Cuba. But Torres says the looser economy also accelerated inequality. During blackouts affecting most of the country, wealthier neighborhoods in Havana stayed lit, residents eating lobster. "Cubans did not accept it," Torres says. Street protests followed.

**Inequality, Emigration, and the Current Oil Embargo**

The protests and widening gap between rich and poor depleted what Torres calls the "moral reserves" — the ideological commitment that had sustained Cubans through earlier hardships. Torres himself left in 2021, part of the largest emigration wave in Cuba's history. By one estimate, nearly 3 million people have left since 2020, roughly a quarter of Cuba's entire population. When Torres returned to visit in 2025, he saw Teslas and Cadillac Escalades on Havana streets alongside beggars and people searching for food in trash cans — scenes he calls "unthinkable in Cuba before" and describes "with sorrow."

At the start of 2026, the Trump administration moved to shut off Cuba's last major oil supply. The U.S. captured Venezuela's president and effectively took control of its oil industry, ending Venezuelan shipments to Cuba. The administration also threatened tariffs against Mexico and other potential oil suppliers if they sold to Cuba. In the weeks following, the country experienced multiple complete island-wide blackouts. Torres's assessment is bleak: Cuba faces two simultaneous crises — a dysfunctional domestic economy and a U.S. government 90 miles away that is "a very ferocious frenemy." He concludes that "the only way out for Cuba is through a negotiation with the United States."

Yasser, in his latest voice notes to Barris, sounds hopeless about his business — but he has not stopped working. With no paying tourists, he has shifted to organizing free community bike events, including a large gathering in a park where attendees grilled food and played music during a blackout period. He says people were grateful to have something during days when they have nothing. His larger goal, he tells Barris, is to change how Cubans think about bikes: not as the vehicles of necessity that China shipped over during the 1990s oil shortages, but as instruments of joy and connection — something that can bring people together even now.

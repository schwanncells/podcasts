# Throughline — "Al Capone and the transformation of the IRS"

**Hosts:** Randa Abdel-Fattah and Ramtin Arablui. **Guests:** Joe Thorndike (historian, Tax Analysts — a nonprofit provider of tax information); Paul Camacho (retired special agent, IRS Criminal Investigation Division; board of directors, Mob Museum in Las Vegas); Jason Scott Smith (historian, University of New Mexico; author of two books on FDR and the New Deal); Lawrence Reed (President Emeritus, Foundation for Economic Education).

## TL;DR

- **Al Capone was convicted of tax evasion in 1931** — made possible by a 1927 Supreme Court ruling, authored by Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes, that illegal income is taxable and must be reported.
- **Elmer Irey built the IRS Intelligence Unit in 1919 from seven former postal inspectors** — the unit fired 706 prohibition agents for larceny and prosecuted 257 more between 1920 and 1928, making Irey the public face of an incorruptible IRS.
- **The income tax top rate jumped from 6% to 77% during World War I** — largely targeting war profiteers like J.P. Morgan Jr., Pierre S. DuPont, and Charles M. Schwab, some of whom bribed tax collectors to avoid paying.
- **J.P. Morgan Jr. paid zero federal income taxes for several years in the early 1930s** — legally, because he had no profit after the crash; the public was outraged anyway, and Congress moved to regulate Wall Street.
- **FDR directed the IRS to investigate political enemies**, including Sen. Huey Long, Rep. Hamilton Fish, and publisher Mo Annenberg; his son Elliot later wrote that FDR "may have been the originator of the concept of employing the IRS as a weapon of political retribution."
- **The Victory Tax of 1942 expanded income taxpayers from roughly 5% to 75% of the American workforce** — converting what Thorndike calls a "class tax" into a "mass tax" almost overnight.
- **Tax withholding was introduced in 1943**, turning employers into de facto federal tax collectors and permanently changing how Americans pay — a measure justified by wartime need that was never rolled back.
- **Vivian Kellams, a manufacturer who refused to withhold and sued the IRS**, became a prominent anti-government voice and helped seed the political movement that would become the modern right.

**Capone, Prohibition, and an Agency No One Expected**

The episode opens with the February 14, 1929 Valentine's Day Massacre, in which seven members of George "Bugs" Moran's gang were shot with Thompson machine guns by men disguised as police officers — 70 rounds fired in a garage on Chicago's north side. Moran's response when asked who was responsible: "Only Capone kills like that." Despite a $50,000 bounty on Al Capone's head (equivalent to nearly $1 million today), no law enforcement agency had successfully prosecuted him. Witnesses died before they could testify. Police, prosecutors, and judges could be bought or threatened.

When President Herbert Hoover took office days after the massacre, he made getting Capone a personal priority. His solution surprised even the people he tapped: he directed Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon to assign the task to the IRS — specifically to a man named Elmer Irey, head of the agency's Intelligence Unit.

**Elmer Irey and the Intelligence Unit**

Irey's path to the IRS began at the post office. In the early 20th century, the U.S. Postal Inspection Service was the elite of federal law enforcement — financial fraud, mail crime, and complex white-collar cases all ran through it. Irey became a respected postal inspector, then was recruited in 1919 by Daniel Roper, the new IRS commissioner, to build a special investigative division from scratch. Irey brought six former postal colleagues and formed the IRS Intelligence Unit.

Almost immediately, the unit was handed an impossible mandate: overseeing prohibition enforcement when the 18th Amendment passed in 1920. Roper quit rather than deal with it; Irey had a family to support and stayed. The prohibition service was riddled with corruption — agents showing up to work in diamond rings and fur coats. From 1920 to 1928, Irey's unit fired 706 prohibition agents for larceny and prosecuted 257 more. Camacho describes Irey as earning a reputation as someone who "did it in such a fair way" that he became the brand of the IRS — "Uncle Elmer," a church-going, all-American family man with the competence to take down organized crime.

**The Supreme Court Opens the Door**

By the mid-1920s, lawyers and government officials had begun pushing for a legal theory that would let prosecutors use tax law against criminals. The argument: Prohibition had created enormous illicit wealth, none of which was being taxed. In 1927, the case reached the Supreme Court. Justice Holmes wrote the opinion: individuals are required to report and pay taxes on illegal income. With that ruling, Irey was effectively told, as Camacho puts it: "The Supreme Court has now determined that illegal income is taxable. Knock yourself out. Go after these kingpins. Use the tax law."

**Convicting Capone and the Giant Killers Era**

The Capone investigation required following financial paper trails through banks and home accounts, interviewing people who were subsequently turning up dead, and ultimately sending an undercover agent inside the Capone organization. At one point, Capone placed a hit on members of Irey's team. Irey held his men to strict constitutional standards throughout, insisting the investigation be done by the book. Capone was convicted of tax evasion in 1931. The episode notes that after sentencing, lines of mobsters appeared at IRS buildings to settle unpaid tax debts.

Hoover ordered Irey's team to conduct a sweeping crime investigation across the country. The unit took down one organized crime kingpin after another. The press dubbed them "the giant killers." They also helped Charles Lindbergh solve the kidnapping and murder of his infant son by tracing ransom money. Irey became a national figure, and the IRS's reputation for incorruptibility briefly reached its peak.

**J.P. Morgan and the Bankster Hearings**

Four years after the stock market crash, with unemployment at 25% and breadlines stretching around city blocks, J.P. Morgan Jr. appeared before a Senate Banking Committee on May 23, 1933. The question under investigation: did Wall Street cause the Depression? Thorndike describes Morgan as "the archetypal Wall Street banker," dominant in international finance for decades and accused of profiteering during World War I.

During testimony, it emerged that Morgan and his partners had paid no federal income taxes for several years in the early 1930s. Morgan's explanation was legally sound: he had made no profit in those years following the crash, so he had no taxable income. Thorndike frames the moral question the episode raises — whether to blame the taxpayer taking advantage of the law as written, or the lawmakers who wrote it. The committee ultimately concluded that unchecked stock market speculation was a central cause of the collapse, and Congress moved to regulate the stock market and limit banks' ability to speculate with depositors' money.

**FDR, the New Deal, and the IRS as Political Weapon**

Franklin Roosevelt entered the White House having campaigned on cutting government spending and eliminating "useless commissions." Once in office, the advisors urging massive government relief programs won out. The New Deal offered direct relief to the poor, public works to put people back to work, and a fundamental reorientation: the government, not the private sector, would lead economic recovery. Smith frames it as an effort to "save capitalism from the capitalists and for the capitalists."

Roosevelt made the income tax a symbolic centerpiece of the New Deal's fairness argument, even though in 1933 only about 5% of Americans — the wealthiest — were paying it. In 1935, the Social Security Act passed alongside a "wealth tax" that raised the top federal income tax rate to 79%. Thorndike notes that rate applied to only one person: John D. Rockefeller Jr. — not even J.P. Morgan Jr. had that level of income.

But FDR also weaponized the IRS for political purposes. He ordered investigations of Democratic primary rival Sen. Huey Long of Louisiana, Republican Rep. Hamilton Fish from his home district, and Mo Annenberg, owner of a media company that was vocally critical of the New Deal. When Treasury Secretary Henry Morgenthau ordered Irey to investigate former Treasury Secretary Andrew Mellon — a man Irey personally believed was innocent — Morgenthau's instruction was explicit: "Irie, you can't be 99 and two-thirds percent on that job. Investigate Mellon. I order it." Irey complied, put his best agent on it, and ultimately demonstrated Mellon's innocence to the court. Elliot Roosevelt later wrote in a book about his family's White House years: "My father may have been the originator of the concept of employing the IRS as a weapon of political retribution."

Reed argues this political instrumentalization is the natural consequence of giving government too much power, and cites it as counter-evidence against the New Deal's image as a strictly benevolent enterprise.

**World War II: From Class Tax to Mass Tax**

On March 9, 1942, Roosevelt wrote Irey a personal letter praising the Intelligence Unit as "a shining mark, not only of incorruptibility, but what is just as important, of A1 efficiency." The letter was partly strategic: Roosevelt had just pushed through the Victory Tax of 1942, and needed the goodwill Irey had built with the American public to carry it. The Victory Tax expanded the income tax dramatically. In 1939, roughly 5% of American workers paid income tax. By the time the Victory Tax was in effect, that figure had risen to 75%. Thorndike's phrase for the transformation: the government took "what they called a class tax and turned it into a mass tax."

The government launched a full public relations campaign to normalize paying income tax, commissioning Irving Berlin to write songs — including "I Paid My Income Tax Today" — and producing a Donald Duck cartoon explaining how to file. Wartime patriotism suppressed resistance that might otherwise have erupted: as Thorndike puts it, "our boys are dying in the field... the least we can do is contribute our dollars to support them."

**Tax Withholding and the Employer-as-Collector System**

The mass expansion of the taxpayer base created an immediate practical problem: the IRS was not staffed to track tens of millions of new filers, and many new taxpayers wouldn't know they owed taxes or wouldn't have money saved when the annual bill came due. In 1943, Congress introduced payroll withholding — the automatic deduction from each paycheck that Americans know today. The mechanism effectively deputized employers as tax collectors, routing the money to the federal government before workers ever received it.

Thorndike says the system was "a win-win for the federal government": it guaranteed revenue collection without requiring the IRS to chase individual filers, and it distributed the tax burden in small, manageable increments throughout the year. Many at the time assumed withholding would be temporary — a wartime measure that would lapse after victory. It did not.

**Vivian Kellams and the Roots of the New Right**

Not everyone accepted the new system quietly. Vivian Kellams, a manufacturer who had built a successful business around a patented cable grip used by bridge builders and the military, refused to implement withholding for her employees. She told them they were responsible for their own tax payments and that she would not deduct wages on the government's behalf. The Chicago Tribune quoted her calling withholding "a deliberate plan to keep the system of free enterprise from surviving after the war."

Kellams toured the country giving speeches to sizable crowds, appeared on talk shows, became one of the first women interviewed on Meet the Press, and deliberately broke the law to force a court test case. She wrote to the Treasury Secretary announcing she had not sent any money and would not until the government settled what it owed her. Smith and Reed see her as a direct ancestor of the political coalition that would become the modern anti-government right — what Reed describes as originating in the 1930s business reaction to the New Deal, the growth of organized labor, and the remaking of labor relations.

**Legacy: Government Expansion, Trust, and an Unresolved Debate**

Elmer Irey retired after World War II. Life magazine named him one of the world's greatest detectives; a Hollywood film, The T-Man, was partly inspired by his career. He died in 1948. But the tax system he helped build and enforce did not contract after the war. The Cold War created new permanent demands: a large standing army and navy and air force, an atomic arsenal, and the national security state that came with it. Smith summarizes the before-and-after: before the 1930s, Americans encountered the federal government primarily at the post office; after World War II, they encountered it in nearly every aspect of life, from Social Security to federal highways.

The episode closes with both sides of the ongoing argument. Thorndike and Smith emphasize that the income tax, however imperfect, is what funds the roads, courts, schools, and national defense. Reed counters that the history demonstrates government's tendency to "give it an inch and it'll take a mile." Smith frames the core tension as one of trust: "To what extent do people trust the government to do the right thing with their tax money? To what extent do people feel like they are getting good value for their money?" Without an Elmer Irey to serve as a trustworthy public face, that question has proven increasingly difficult to answer.

# The Economics of Everyday Things — "27. Romance Novels"

**Host:** Zachary Crockett. **Guests:** Danielle Flores (high school math teacher and avid romance reader who tracks her reading via spreadsheet, averaging ~250 novels/year); Diane Maggi (VP of editorial, Harlequin); Brenda Hyatt (romance author, Key Largo, Florida; published historical romances with Harlequin before transitioning to self-publishing); Delaney Diamond (self-published romance author; 50+ books in 13 years; writes Black romance).

## TL;DR

- **Romance is a $1.4 billion industry** — physical sales are up more than 50% over the past year alone, even as the broader publishing market struggles.
- **Harlequin built the mass market starting in the 1970s** — the company was selling more than 70 million paperbacks annually by the end of the decade, then won the "Romance Wars" by buying out rival Silhouette in 1984; it now publishes ~800 new titles per year.
- **The business model runs like a magazine, not a bookstore** — roughly half of romance readers consume at least one novel per week, and Harlequin's core revenue comes from monthly subscription book clubs delivering 4–8 titles to readers' doors.
- **Covers are the primary marketing tool** — if a dog appears on a cover, Harlequin says it is "guaranteed to actually really perform well"; subgenres include Amish romance, medical romance, monster romance, and "dogs with jobs."
- **Traditional publishing paid authors almost nothing** — Brenda Hyatt's first advance was $3,000, with royalties of roughly 20 cents per $3.99 book; many authors had no idea published writers weren't "rolling in dough."
- **Self-publishing flipped the economics** — royalty rates went from ~6% through bookstores to 70% on Amazon e-books; Hyatt's republished backlist books eventually outsold their original print runs, and one became a New York Times bestseller 17 years after first publication. Romance authors now earn a median of ~$32,000/year — more than three times any other genre — and nearly 1 in 5 earns six figures.
- **People of color make up ~23% of romance readers but less than 8% of published authors** — Black authors in traditional publishing were sometimes explicitly barred from writing Black characters, and diverse books were often shelved in a separate "ethnic section."
- **BookTok and reader communities are driving a new era** — millions of fans use TikTok hashtags like "spicy talk" and "billionaire romance," and conferences let fans gather "without judgment."

**Origins: From Harlequin to the Romance Wars**

The genre's commercial roots trace to Samuel Richardson's *Pamela* (1740), but romance as a mass-market industry didn't take hold in North America until the 1950s, when Canadian publisher Harlequin began carrying romance titles from the UK imprint Mills & Boon. Diane Maggi explains that those romances "very, very quickly became the bestsellers across the company," and Harlequin pivoted to focus on them. A major surge came in the mid-1970s with the arrival of "bodice rippers" — historically set, often explicitly sexual novels sold in supermarkets and drugstores, not bookstores. By decade's end, Harlequin was moving 70 million paperbacks per year.

The success triggered what the industry called the Romance Wars: competing publishers launched their own romance lines, creating more options for readers and authors but also fierce consolidation pressure. Ultimately only two major players survived, Harlequin and Silhouette. Harlequin won — outspending on legal and financial firepower — and bought Silhouette in 1984. Now a HarperCollins subsidiary, Harlequin publishes around 800 romance titles annually across its "category romance" lines covering every subgenre imaginable.

**The Magazine-Style Business Model**

The economics of romance publishing resemble a subscription magazine more than a traditional book business. Maggi says Harlequin runs "an incredibly large and viable book club business where readers subscribe to a series and they can get four or six or eight and multiple series delivered to their door in print form every month." Industry data supports the voracious appetite: about half of all romance readers finish at least one novel a week, and some read up to 30 a month. Danielle Flores, who reads roughly 250 novels a year and maintains a spreadsheet to track them, is not unusual in this genre.

To sustain that volume, Harlequin sources extremely prolific authors. Maggi mentions one who just published her 175th book — and notes that someone else in their stable has written over 300, the equivalent of a new book every two months for 50 straight years.

**Subgenres and the Cover as Marketing Weapon**

Romance's sprawl of subgenres is part of its commercial formula. Florals range from Regency historicals to paranormal, alien abduction, and monster romance ("either monster to monster or human to monster like orcs"), along with Amish romance, medical romance, and — currently surging — books featuring working dogs. Maggi says Harlequin internally calls the latter trend "dogs with jobs" and confirms that a dog on a cover is "guaranteed to actually really perform well."

Covers function as the publisher's most direct communication to buyers. In the 1990s, model Fabio Lanzoni appeared on more than 450 romance covers, becoming a minor celebrity and eventually a bestselling author himself. Today's covers are more varied and genre-specific, engineered to signal subgenre immediately — Maggi says publishers want readers to grasp "what story she's going to get" within 10 seconds in front of a bookcase. A known side effect is reused stock images: Flores jokes about spotting "the same guy that has been out, has a sword, he's in a bathtub, there's another person behind him now."

**The Economics of Authorship (Traditional Era)**

Romance authors in the traditional publishing world were, in Hyatt's phrase, "like mushrooms — they were fed a lot of crap and kept in the dark." Hyatt's first advance for a Harlequin historical was $3,000. Royalties on a $3.99 paperback came to "no more than 20 cents per book." The genre was simultaneously the industry's financial backbone and its least respected product. Hyatt describes it bluntly: "They were the cash cow of the publishing industry. They brought in all the bucks, but they bankrolled the respectable books." Distribution and most publishing decisions were controlled by men, while the books themselves were written by women for women.

**Self-Publishing Rewrites the Economics**

The e-book era transformed what had been a poorly paid vocation. Hyatt bought back the rights to her old books in the early 2000s and republished them as e-books. Within a couple of years, she was earning more from those republished titles than she had during their entire original print runs. The key shift: royalty rates went from roughly 6% through traditional bookstore channels to 70% when sold electronically through Amazon. One of her resurrected books became a New York Times and USA Today bestseller 17 years after its first publication.

Hyatt notes that self-published romance author incomes are largely invisible in industry statistics because those figures come from traditional publishers only. "A lot of authors are really making bank and they're kind of flying under the radar." A 2022 Authors Guild survey found romance writers earned a median income of about $32,000 from their books — more than three times the median in any other genre — and nearly one in five earns six figures annually. Delaney Diamond, who has self-published more than 50 books over 13 years, credits the platform shift: "Now you can actually make a living at writing through self-publishing, so it's a totally different game."

**Representation and the Diversity Gap**

Diamond's path into the genre illustrates its representation problems. Growing up in the Virgin Islands, she read everything the local library had in romance — but none of the heroines looked like her. At 14, she wrote her own romance novel and made the protagonist Black. When she tried to sell a series focused on Black romance as an adult, a traditional publisher told her "Black romance doesn't sell," a claim she knew from personal reading experience was false.

People of color make up roughly 23% of romance readers, but less than 8% of published romance authors. Hyatt confirms that Black authors in traditional publishing were sometimes explicitly prohibited from writing Black characters: "They had to write white characters if they wanted to sell books, period." Books that did feature diverse leads were often segregated into a separate "ethnic section" of bookstores, reducing their visibility and sales.

The traditional publishing industry has launched mentorship and scholarship programs to address this, and editors are more actively seeking diverse characters. Flores notes that "queer romance has come into its own" in recent years as a visible sign of progress.

**BookTok, Community, and the Fight for Respect**

The genre's reader community has become a significant commercial and cultural force. TikTok hashtags like "spicy talk," "forbidden love," and "billionaire romance" bring millions of viewers together; Facebook groups, podcasts, YouTube channels, and dedicated conferences fill the rest of the social ecosystem. Flores describes the relief of attending events "where no one's going to snicker" and everyone will discuss alien romances without embarrassment.

Stigma, however, persists. Flores says colleagues at her school laughed when they found out she read romance and immediately asked about the sex scenes. Romance fans understand they will always face jokes — but, as Crockett frames it, the genre's billion-dollar sales figures suggest those jokes don't slow anyone down. Flores's own explanation for why romance endures is simple: "I'm looking for my reading to give me hope."

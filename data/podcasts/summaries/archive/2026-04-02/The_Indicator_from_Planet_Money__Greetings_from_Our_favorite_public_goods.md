# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Greetings from: Our favorite public goods"

**Host:** Darian Boyd. **Guest:** Alex Mayasi (contributor, Planet Money; author of *Planet Money: A Guide to the Economic Forces That Shape Your Life*).

## TL;DR

- **Public goods are non-excludable and non-rivalrous** — no one can be barred from their benefits, and more users don't diminish availability; examples include streetlights, national security, and atomic clocks.
- **Cesium fountain atomic clocks in Colorado set time for the entire country** — without this precision timekeeping, GPS positions would drift, internet errors would proliferate, and power grids could fail.
- **The Large Hadron Collider has generated economic value "far exceeding its multi-billion dollar price tag"** — government-funded and freely published, its discoveries in magnets, refrigeration, and other fields have broad practical applications.
- **The Strait of Gibraltar has been pirate-free for 200 years** — US naval intervention in the 1800s and France's 1830 conquest of Algeria ended Barbary piracy; the resulting international principle of freedom of the seas keeps cargo prices down at retailers like Walmart.
- **11 African countries are spending $33 billion to plant a trans-continental forest** — the Great Green Wall of the Sahel aims to restore degraded land, expand farming, and sequester carbon across the continent's southern edge.
- **NOAA's hurricane hunters fly three planes — Kermit, Gonzo, and Miss Piggy — directly into tropical storms** — their atmospheric data powers public weather forecasts; the Trump administration curtailed NOAA's operations.

**What Makes Something a Public Good**

Boyd opens by defining the episode's central concept: a public good is any service or benefit from which people cannot be excluded, and where additional users don't diminish what's available to others. Streetlights and national security are classic examples. Boyd and Mayasi use a postcard conceit — drawn from Mayasi's illustrated Planet Money book — to tour six public goods across the globe.

**Atomic Clocks and the Infrastructure of Time**

Mayasi's first postcard comes from NIST's cesium fountain atomic clocks in Boulder, Colorado. These clocks, among several hundred operated worldwide, measure the length of a second using the resonant frequencies of atoms, making them the most accurate timekeeping devices ever built. Their output synchronizes wristwatches, wall clocks, and phones across the country. The stakes of losing this infrastructure are concrete: GPS positioning would drift, internet errors would multiply, and power grids could become unstable.

**The Large Hadron Collider**

Boyd's return postcard comes from the French-Swiss border, where CERN's Large Hadron Collider uses magnetic rings to smash subatomic particles together at 99% of the speed of light. Access is limited to elite physicists, but its findings — funded by billions of dollars of annual government investment — are freely available. While many experiments seek to reveal the origin of the cosmos rather than produce immediate economic returns, the LHC has generated commercial value far exceeding its multi-billion dollar construction and operating costs through practical discoveries in magnets, refrigeration, and other fields.

**Freedom of the Seas**

Mayasi's next postcard comes from the Strait of Gibraltar. For centuries, North African Barbary pirates based in Tripoli and Algiers preyed on trading ships navigating the narrow passage between the Atlantic and the Mediterranean. The threat ended in the early 19th century after the United States dispatched a naval squadron to intimidate the Barbary states and France conquered Algeria in 1830. Today a broad international agreement — freedom of the seas — ensures that countries from Morocco to Japan cooperate to keep shipping lanes open. Mayasi links this directly to consumer prices: protected cargo means lower costs at stores like Walmart. The episode briefly contrasts this with the Strait of Hormuz, where recent attacks on shipping demonstrate what happens when that protection breaks down.

**The Great Green Wall**

Boyd's postcard from the Sahel describes a large-scale public good in progress. The region just south of the Sahara looks like a desert today, but centuries of deforestation, overgrazing, and climate change stripped land that was once arable. Eleven African countries and several international organizations have committed $33 billion to restore it by planting millions of trees from coast to coast. The goals are threefold: restore farmland, capture carbon dioxide, and cool the surrounding environment — comparable, at continental scale, to urban tree-planting programs that can reduce city temperatures by several degrees.

**NOAA's Hurricane Hunters**

Mayasi's final postcard comes from inside a hurricane. He describes boarding one of NOAA's three research aircraft — nicknamed Kermit, Gonzo, and Miss Piggy — which fly directly into and around forming tropical storms over the Atlantic to measure their path and intensity. The data feeds the weather forecasts that millions rely on. Taxpayers fund these agencies, but Boyd notes the Trump administration has curtailed NOAA's operations, raising questions about the durability of this particular public good.

**GPS, Road Rules, and Public Goods Everywhere**

The episode closes with Boyd noting that the ride home from his day of virtual travel depends on GPS — a public good delivered by government satellites — and that road rules make the journey safer. Mayasi's takeaway: once you start looking for public goods, they appear everywhere.

# Code Switch — "Hate it or love it, is DEI a distraction?"

**Host:** Gene Demby. **Guest:** Jennifer C. Pan (author of *Selling Social Justice: Why the Rich Love Antiracism*; researcher on corporate diversity management and inequality).

## TL;DR

- **DEI programs don't work on their own terms** — decades of evidence show mandatory anti-racism trainings don't reduce bias and may worsen it; diversity initiatives fail to increase workplace diversity or close pay gaps between racial and gender groups.
- **DEI functions as a management tool, not worker protection** — HR/DEI departments exist to shield companies from liability, not to advance worker interests, despite good intentions of individual DEI officers.
- **Corporations donated $340 billion to racial justice in 2020 to avoid genuine redistribution** — the embrace of DEI language by the wealthy allowed them to appear progressive while protecting power, avoiding taxes, and consolidating control over workers without substantive wealth redistribution.
- **Race-first framing obscures how class shapes inequality** — a white non-college graduate is 15 times more likely to be incarcerated or shot by police than a Black college graduate; focusing only on race papers over these material differences.
- **Corporate DEI primarily benefits professional workers, not working-class people** — Target's $2 billion pledge to Black-owned businesses and shelf space for Black vendors benefited business owners, not the vast majority of Black workers at the company.
- **Unions and neutral workplace policies outperform DEI on diversity and pay equity** — formal mentorship, work-life balance policies, thoughtful recruitment, and labor unions all show better track records at closing pay gaps and reducing racial bias through shared worker identity.
- **Corporations are rebranding DEI rather than abandoning it** — despite Trump's assault on DEI, major companies (Apple, Costco, Goldman Sachs) and shareholders (Vanguard) have recommitted to these programs using new language, suggesting persistent reputational value despite ineffectiveness.
- **Corporate appropriation of racial justice has historical precedent** — in 1948, the Democratic Party embraced racial equality rhetoric to compete for Black voters in the shadow of WWII and Nazism, using race rhetoric to rehabilitate America's global image while avoiding structural change.

**The Ineffectiveness of DEI Programs**

Pan begins by stating a point "that has gone a little bit underreported": DEI programs simply don't work or work very poorly. When measured against their stated promises—increasing workplace diversity, elevating people of color to managerial positions, closing pay gaps—the evidence is robust and dating back to the 1960s Civil Rights Act. The most popular DEI programs, particularly mandatory anti-racism trainings, don't reduce workers' biases; in many cases they make them worse. Most critically, these programs don't actually make workplaces more diverse, failing on their own terms.

**DEI as a Management Function**

While Pan acknowledges that many individual DEI officers genuinely care about workplace equity, she emphasizes that the DEI office as an institutional entity serves to protect the company, not the worker. DEI is fundamentally a tool of human resources management—an extension of management itself. The function is corporate liability protection, analogous to how the HR department exists not to help workers but to insulate the company from legal trouble. This structural reality persists regardless of individual intent.

**What Actually Works: Unions, Mentorship, and Neutral Policies**

Pan highlights that decades of research identify programs that do work, and many of them aren't labeled "DEI" or sound race-specific. Formal mentorship programs, work-life balance policies, and thoughtful recruitment practices—ones where companies are deliberate about where they post jobs and which candidates they target—effectively increase diversity without sounding like they're "for" any particular group. Most powerfully, unions show the best track record on every measure DEI promises: closing pay gaps, increasing diversity, and reducing bias. Demby notes that research shows multiracial labor unions reduce racial bias by creating shared worker identity—"management is our problem"—regardless of demographic differences.

**The $340 Billion Appropriation: Corporate Cooptation of 2020**

Pan explains that during the 2020 George Floyd protests and racial reckoning, corporations collectively donated $340 billion to social justice and racial justice initiatives. This massive show of support allowed large employers to present themselves as on the side of justice while simultaneously further consolidating power over workers and avoiding any substantive redistribution of wealth or changes to labor practices. The corporate strategy was to seem progressive—signing on to everything—without actually altering economic structures. Target, for example, pledged to increase its Black workforce by 20%, spend billions with Black-owned businesses, add hundreds of products from Black vendors, donate $100 million to HBCUs, and later pledge $2 billion specifically to Black-owned businesses. Yet these initiatives primarily benefited Black business owners and professionals, not the vast majority of Black workers at the company working retail wages.

**How Racial Justice Rhetoric Protects Corporate Power**

When companies announced DEI measures, they simultaneously signaled to government regulators: "We're already doing good, so you don't need to oversee us or force us to pay fair wages or taxes." Pan references a speech by FTC Commissioner Lena Khan in which corporations explicitly came to her saying they should approve mergers because they do DEI and have ESG (environmental, social, and corporate governance) measures in place. This rhetoric of stakeholder capitalism—the idea that corporations can "do well by doing good"—allows companies to avoid government regulation, labor unionization, higher taxes, and genuine environmental/worker protections. Reparations rhetoric, which Pan also notes corporations embraced, is particularly telling: it sounds radical but wouldn't necessarily hurt corporate bottom lines since government, not corporations, would theoretically pay. Corporations backed militant-sounding racial justice language that posed no actual threat to their wealth.

**Class Erasure in Race-First Analysis**

Pan emphasizes a critical blind spot in focusing only on race: class inequality is far more determinative of police violence and incarceration risk than race alone. While Black college graduates are more likely to be shot or incarcerated than white college graduates, a white non-college graduate is 15 times more likely to experience these harms than a Black college graduate. For much of American history, class and race were closer proxies for each other, but in recent decades economic divergence has fractured this. A race-only framing papers over these material differences and allows DEI initiatives to benefit professional Black workers while leaving working-class people of all races facing unchanged material conditions.

**Historical Pattern: Corporate Cooptation at Crisis Moments**

Pan traces this pattern backward to 1948, when the Democratic Party, facing anxiety in the shadow of WWII and Nazi racial ideology, adopted racial equality rhetoric to compete for Black voters and rehabilitate America's image as a multiracial democracy. No structural change followed; the gesture was reputational. The pattern repeats: at moments of rising inequality and social anger, corporations and elites use racial justice language to defuse discontent and protect their interests while appearing to address demands for change. In 2020, corporations essentially signaled: "the capitalist system is fine, it just needs to be more diverse."

**DEI Under Trump: Rebranding Rather Than Retreat**

Pan observes that despite Trump's explicit assault on DEI and threats against companies practicing it, DEI isn't dead. For every company like Target rolling back programs, others (Apple, Costco, Goldman Sachs) and major shareholders like Vanguard have recommitted to DEI initiatives. The key shift: companies are rebranding rather than eliminating. This pattern echoes the Reagan era, when despite Reagan's hostility to affirmative action and attempts to hollow out the EEOC, nine out of ten corporations maintained and even increased their diversity programs. The persistence of these programs despite hostile administrations suggests they offer ongoing reputational value, even if their stated goals go unmet. DEI is simply undergoing another evolution, a name change in an ongoing history of diversity management stretching back to the 1960s.

**The Fundamental Problem and the Path Forward**

Pan concludes that the fundamental problem with leaving social justice to corporations is that they are beholden to their bottom line. While DEI, ESG, and similar programs are easier for corporations to embrace than genuine alternatives, they ultimately protect wealth concentration while appearing progressive. The alternative—unions, labor regulation, higher taxes, genuine wage increases, and wealth redistribution—would require corporations to actually give up power or money in substantive ways. As long as racial justice remains a corporate project rather than a state or labor-driven mandate, it will continue to benefit the ruling class while leaving material conditions for workers unchanged.

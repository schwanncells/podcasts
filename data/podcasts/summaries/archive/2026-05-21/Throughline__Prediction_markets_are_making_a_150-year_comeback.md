# Throughline — "Prediction markets are making a 150-year comeback"

**Host:** Rund Abdelfatah. **Guests:** Coleman Strumpf (economics professor, Wake Forest University; teacher of prediction markets course for nearly 20 years); Paul Rohde (economic historian, University of Michigan; researcher specializing in prediction market history); Robin Hanson (economist, futurist; founder of "idea futures" concept; led DARPA's Policy Analysis Market project).

**The Ancient Roots: Betting on Elections and Popes**

Prediction markets are far older than the internet or even the modern stock market. Paul Rohde, an economic historian, discovered through archived microfilm that political prediction markets existed as early as October 1924—well before the Iowa Electronic Markets were created. Tracing the trail further back, markets on elections and political outcomes existed in Venice and Genoa during city-state periods, and betting on papal succession dates back at least to the 16th century. In the American colonies and early republic, futures on everything from the Stamp Act's duration to George Washington's presidency attracted money and speculation.

What these early markets shared was a basic principle: people put money where they believed the outcome would be. Through centuries, economists and historians have observed that these bets aggregate genuine information and prediction ability. People who study betting markets—like Coleman Strumpf watching crowds at horse racing as a child—note that betting reveals true beliefs because money is at stake. Winning on a long-shot bet, Strumpf observed, gives people not just monetary reward but psychological satisfaction: the pleasure of being right, of "showing off their smarts."

**The 20th-Century Golden Age: The Curb Exchange**

Around the turn of the 20th century, election markets in the U.S. experienced a boom, particularly in New York. The primary venue was the Curb Exchange—literally the curb outside the New York Stock Exchange, where traders stood in the street buying and selling stocks in political candidates. These were public operations: Wall Street Journal reporters could walk down and report not only overall prices but the names of individual traders involved. The traders were elite figures—Tammany Hall leaders, bankers, hotel owners, Republicans and Democrats alike.

A striking dynamic emerged: political party leaders were often expected to bet on their own candidates to show public support, but behind the scenes they hedged by betting against their candidate if they thought they would lose. A Democratic machine might publicly endorse William Jennings Bryant while privately placing bets that he'd lose, ensuring they didn't lose money if the election went against their public stance. These markets accurately predicted outcomes, but the business of trading on elections eventually faded in the 1940s. Newspapers became more comfortable covering polls (which emerged in the 1930s with Gallup and others) than election betting markets. Thoroughbred horse racing also exploded during this period, offering traders more frequent events—12 races a night instead of a couple of elections per year. By the 1940s, electoral prediction markets had gone dark; for roughly four decades, they were completely forgotten.

**The Iowa Electronic Markets: Rediscovery and Validation**

In 1988, three economics professors at the University of Iowa—Robert Forsyth and two colleagues—were having a three-beer lunch at a local sports bar on the day after the Michigan caucus, where Jesse Jackson had unexpectedly defeated Michael Dukakis. Political polls had completely missed the outcome. Over drinks, the professors thought: what would economists do to predict something? Why not run a market? They recruited a couple hundred students and faculty at the university and created an online interface where people could buy stock in presidential candidates, trading as the campaign progressed. The result was extraordinary: on the night before the 1988 election, their Iowa Electronic Markets predicted the popular vote within two-tenths of one percent—beating Gallup, Harris, CBS, and the New York Times.

The market worked because of what economists call "the wisdom of the crowd." Traders were putting real money (though capped at $500 per person) on their beliefs, and the aggregate price revealed more accurate predictions than traditional polls. Between 1988 and 2004, the Iowa Electronic Markets outperformed traditional polls 74% of the time. The market remained small—constrained by the Commodity Futures Trading Commission, which issued a "no-action letter" allowing it to operate as a non-profit research project as long as it stayed small, took no contributions over $500, and didn't advertise. The Iowans couldn't run markets on sports, only elections.

But their success sparked a chain of thought among economists: if prediction markets work for elections, what else could they work for? The Iowa market became proof that prediction markets could forecast complex events accurately, opening the door to grander visions.

**DARPA's Policy Analysis Market and the "Betting on Death" Fiasco**

In the early 2000s, economist Robin Hanson—who had been working on the theoretical concept of "idea futures" since the 1990s—received a call from DARPA (Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency). DARPA wanted to explore whether prediction markets could help the Defense Department forecast geopolitical events. Hanson, then an academic, was invited to design a more sophisticated market where traders could bet on combinations of related questions about the Middle East: whether the Saudi regime would fall, how that would affect oil prices, military activity, political instability, economic growth.

The project, called the Policy Analysis Market, was approved shortly after 9/11 and set to launch in summer 2003. It would invite public participation, allowing anyone to invest up to $100. The sample screen shown to solicit testers included miscellaneous bets on scenarios like a North Korean missile launch and the assassination of Yasser Arafat. On July 28, 2003, two senators held a press conference and denounced the program as "a federal betting parlor on atrocities and terrorism." They called it "betting on death" and said it was "morally wrong" and "unbelievably stupid." Within 24 hours, the Secretary of Defense announced the program was dead. The head of DARPA resigned.

Hanson reflects that no one asked whether the accusations were accurate. The market was designed with small stakes—$100 per person—and Hanson believed the goal was noble: helping policymakers understand probability by aggregating crowd forecasts. He thought it might even deter wars by making the human costs visible. But the public saw it differently: the idea of betting on deaths and assassinations triggered a visceral disgust reaction. Hanson, an economist, notes that people have deep rules about where money should not be involved. To his economic mind, the stakes were too small for the market itself to incentivize anyone to cause deaths; the real issue was psychological—people found the concept morally repugnant.

**InTrade and the Offshore Gambling Wars**

After DARPA's project collapsed, private industry began exploring prediction markets. In the early 2000s, two offshore platforms emerged: InTrade (based in Ireland but serving U.S. users) and Betfair (UK-based). The CFTC, which regulated futures markets, went after InTrade repeatedly between 2005 and 2013, filing cease-and-desist orders and imposing fines for trading in commodities that didn't have legal U.S. markets. InTrade also faced the fallout from the 2006 Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act.

InTrade's CEO, John Delaney, died climbing Mount Everest in 2009, and later investigations revealed the company wasn't holding customer funds in reserve—people who wanted to cash out couldn't do so. In 2013, the CFTC sued what was essentially a non-existent company, leading to what Rohde calls "finding a corpse"—fining InTrade years after it had shut down, sending a message to any successor that regulatory hostility would persist. Betfair, in contrast, complied with the CFTC by blocking U.S. users (if you visit Betfair today from the U.S., you cannot access it). InTrade was erased from regulatory memory.

**The 2024 Pivot: From Prohibition to Permission**

For decades, the CFTC maintained a tight leash on prediction markets. Then in 2025, President Trump appointed a new CFTC chair, Michael Selig, who had been chief counsel of the crypto task force at the SEC. Selig was "very, very favorably inclined to prediction markets." Within months, the regulatory landscape flipped. In 2026, prediction markets have gone from banned to celebrated. Polymarket, which was completely prohibited in 2022, has now mushroomed into massive trading volume. Calchi (also called Kalshi) operates legally under CFTC approval. Some analysts project prediction markets could become a trillion-dollar industry.

But with this growth come documented cases of insider trading and information abuse. A New York Times investigation identified roughly 11,000 Polymarket accounts with warning signs: successful long-shot bets placed by new accounts, well-timed wagers on military operations by traders who appeared to have government knowledge. A U.S. Special Forces soldier involved in planning Maduro's capture was indicted after making over $300,000 betting on the operation. Former Biden staffers made six-figure profits betting on presidential pardons. French police are investigating whether someone deliberately tampered with a weather-reading device at Charles de Gaulle airport to win a Polymarket bet. The White House sent an email to staffers warning them not to use prediction markets. The Senate passed a bipartisan resolution banning members and staff from trading on them.

**The Cycle and the Future**

Coleman Strumpf, who has watched prediction markets for 30 years, notes a repeating pattern: boom followed by crash. Economists want to believe markets are back for good, but as money and power concentrate in prediction markets, public backlash and regulatory backlash have historically followed. He says he was surprised they're this big in 2026; three years ago, he would have predicted they'd be zero-sized.

Robin Hanson remains optimistic about the future. He envisions prediction markets for everything: company stock prices conditional on firing the CEO, markets for which school students should attend, even markets predicting relationship longevity. He believes aggregating bets could reveal truth better than single experts or committees.

Yet Hanson also acknowledges the discomfort: calculating every decision with financial stakes feels dystopian to many. Paul Rohde emphasizes the moral dimension—profiting from someone else's assassination or misfortune triggers a deep disgust reaction. Over centuries, societies have normalized previously uncomfortable systems; the question is whether prediction markets will join that category or whether backlash will reinstate prohibition as it has in the past.

Strumpf concludes that if forced to predict prediction markets' future now, he'd be glad he doesn't have to put money on it.

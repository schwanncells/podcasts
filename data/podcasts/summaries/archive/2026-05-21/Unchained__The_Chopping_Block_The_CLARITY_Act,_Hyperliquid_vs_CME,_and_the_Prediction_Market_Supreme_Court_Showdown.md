**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guests:** Tom (DeFi maven and master of memes), Tun (Gigabrain and grand puba at Gauntlet), Rebecca (jurist credential genius at Gito Labs), Steve (head hype man at Dragonfly).

**The CLARITY Act's Regulatory Roadmap and Stalling Points**

The episode frames CLARITY as the centerpiece of Trump's crypto agenda to make the US the "crypto capital of the world." It passed the House early in the legislative cycle but has stalled in the Senate, where three major issues remain unresolved. The first and biggest fight was over stablecoin yield. The GENIUS Act had allowed stablecoins to pay yield relatively freely. The banking lobby—fearing deposit flight and systemic risk—objected intensely, though White House analysis concluded their arguments were specious: $300 billion in stablecoins is "absolutely nothing" in the context of the $20 trillion US banking system, and most stablecoin adoption is international (emerging markets, not US bank flight). Coinbase initially walked out of negotiations but returned to broker a compromise: transaction-based rewards are now acceptable (users get rewards for sending USDC or doing other actions), but passive yield that looks like bank deposits is prohibited. Treasury and industry rejected the banking argument, and Tillis (R) used his committee leverage to lock in this compromise over bank objections.

The second major fight concerns presidential ethics—Democrats demand provisions preventing elected officials from profiting through crypto projects like World Liberty Financial, though Trump administration figures reject this as targeting Trump specifically. Negotiations have been closed-door, but Sens. Goss (D) and Brooks (D) signed off on the committee-level bill, suggesting progress. The third area, developer protections, remains contested; recent markup changes removed language protecting non-controlling software developers, and Rebecca warns this must be held to ensure developers can build protocols without money transmitter registration.

If CLARITY fails to pass this year, expectations are that Democrats will block it in the midterm year and the bill dies, requiring future presidents to rely on agency rulemaking. Current Polymarket odds are 61% for passage, down from 80% in February.

**What CLARITY Actually Does After Passage**

If CLARITY passes, the law does not immediately end regulatory uncertainty. Rebecca explains that there are roughly 45 rulemakings on the Senate side alone—the bill orders Treasury, SEC, CFTC, and other agencies to write detailed rules via notice-and-comment. During this process, the "contours of law" are set by the 309-page bill, but implementation details remain open. The SEC, in particular, has already been issuing guidance rapidly on parallel regulatory questions, but formal rules could take years. Even in the happy path with no major litigation, founders can expect 3+ years before final implementation. Dodd-Frank, passed in 2010, is still seeing rulemaking today.

For a token founder launching a project post-CLARITY, immediate obligations are minimal. Rebecca notes that disclosures may be required only after graduating out of the "super centralized" phase, and since most tokens remain centralized, many founders may do nothing for years. The bill's requirement for "longstanding ongoing disclosures" is unclear about whether foundations or issuers must provide them, and "decentralization" remains undefined, making it hard for projects to know if they meet the threshold. The core value of CLARITY is "futureproofing" the US industry; large traditional institutions cite it as a prerequisite for dipping their toes into crypto, though ironically, the bill may not directly touch their activities.

**CME and ICE vs. Hyperliquid: Protectionism or Legitimate Regulation?**

Hyperliquid's massive growth in trading commodities and RWAs (Real World Assets, especially petroleum and precious metals) during the Iran war triggered concern-lobbying from the CME and ICE. These incumbents are asking Congress to force Hyperliquid to register as a CFTC Designated Contract Market (DCM), implement KYC/AML, trade surveillance, and position limits—all standard centralized venue requirements. Hyperliquid's counter-argument is that on-chain transparency provides better information to markets than opaque order books, that the lack of a central counterparty reduces systemic risk, and that DeFi regulatory frameworks (under CLARITY) should apply instead of traditional compliance burdens.

The panel debates whether position limits and other centralized safeguards are consumer protection or inefficient friction. Tun notes that funding rates on perpetual swaps serve as a natural throttle on excessive leverage (users pay funding when over-leveraged), making position limits redundant. The broader point: Hyperliquid has disrupted derivatives trading much as crypto exchanges disrupted spot trading, and incumbents are deploying regulatory lobbying as a weapon. If they succeed in forcing Hyperliquid to register as a DCM, the venue may become onshore and compliant, but if they fail, the cat is out of the bag—other DeFi venues will emerge.

The difference from centralized exchange vs. Hyperliquid competition (e.g., Binance vs. Hyperliquid) is scale: CME is unbelievably powerful and has regulatory ears in Congress. Making CME an enemy is far costlier than competing with Binance.

**Prediction Markets Heading to Supreme Court on Federalism**

Prediction markets (Kalshi and Polymarket) have been sued by multiple states, state attorneys general, and now the CFTC in what the panel describes as a "WWE fight" where federal and state authorities are attacking each other and the companies simultaneously. Most state courts have sided with states on the theory that sports betting is gambling, which has traditionally been a state domain. However, the Third Circuit (federal) ruled that the CFTC has exclusive jurisdiction under the Commodity Futures Modernization Act (CFMA), and the Ninth Circuit is expected to reach a different conclusion, creating a circuit split that draws Supreme Court attention.

Rebecca believes the Supreme Court will side with the CFTC and federal jurisdiction, citing the breadth of the CFMA's definition of swaps (contingency-based contracts with economic impact) and noting that the Third Circuit opinion is "very good." However, Steve suspects this Supreme Court, traditionally skeptical of federal power and sympathetic to federalism, may side with the states and gambling jurisdiction.

The crucial unresolved issue is sports markets. The CFTC has issued guidance saying prediction markets on sports should consider whether the outcome has economic impact (e.g., hotel stays during Super Bowl) and whether the market is susceptible to manipulation (single-player props vs. playoff games). Rebecca notes the CFTC has self-certified baseball contracts with MLB and is open to sports markets. The Super Bowl coin toss market that traded at 58-42 (not 50-50) showed markets are pricing information, not just noise, though CFTC Chair Mike Eling suggested such narrow markets may not be permissible. If the Supreme Court narrows permissible sports markets to those with measurable economic impact, many prediction markets would survive; if it grants states jurisdiction entirely, the patchwork returns.

**IPO Disruption and Direct Listings via Prediction Markets**

Hyperliquid's pre-IPO markets are quantifying investment banking mispricing. The Cerebras IPO was priced by bankers at $130-$180 but opened far higher, while Hyperliquid's pre-IPO market showed roughly $280—much closer to the true clearing price. This transparency disrupts the mystique of book-building and underwriting; traditional investment banks have long claimed that only they can determine "fair" prices, that IPO pops are a feature (not a bug), and that long-term holder composition matters. Prediction markets expose this as voodoo—the market price is knowable, and if companies and their advisors demand a discount to bankers' internal estimates, they can quantify their cost and make an economic decision.

Tom and others note that IPO arbitrage (pop for quick profit) is changing. Direct listings have emerged as an alternative to traditional IPOs, and Paul Atkins (SEC chair) wants to "make IPOs great again" by reducing friction. If retail access to IPOs expands (via Robinhood, the securities apps' allocation lotteries, or on-chain issuance), the prediction market price could become a reference point for allocating retail shares. The question of whether the investment banking model survives remains open: bankers still provide value in sourcing long-term holders, ensuring proper governance, and managing legal risk. But transparency and optionality are eroding their information advantage.

**Tokenized Securities: Innovation Exemption and Issuer Indifference**

The SEC has been rumored to issue an innovation exemption on tokenized equities, potentially allowing third-party tokens (derivatives that track stocks without holding underlying shares) and on-chain trading without issuer involvement. Rebecca says the SEC has been "very thoughtful" about timing and may wait for CLARITY to pass and be signed by July 4 before moving. However, the agencies (SEC, CFTC) have signaled they will act regardless of legislation.

Current tokenized stock volume is negligible. Most projects are issuing derivatives (no voting rights, no dividends in some cases) rather than backed tokens (requiring issuer involvement). Issuers themselves "do not give a flying [expletive] about these people"—they are focused on core business, not on-chain trading venues for 12 people. The market is not being suppressed by regulation so much as ignored by companies who see no revenue driver. Steve argues that long-term, international demand (wealthy non-US persons seeking unblocked access to US equities) could unlock adoption, but that demand currently goes to derivatives (cheaper, no KYC) or Bitcoin/Ethereum (easier volatility trading).

Rebecca notes that even with an innovation exemption, disclosures and decentralization thresholds remain unclear, and companies are unlikely to move until they see peers move first. Tokenized equities remain a solution in search of a problem; on-chain derivatives (perpetuals on Hyperliquid) have all the liquidity.

**Developer Protections and the Future of On-Chain Finance vs. DeFi**

Rebecca introduces the term "on-chain finance" (OnFi) to distinguish ventures like Hyperliquid (non-custodial, permissionless smart contracts, but with closed-source code and some centralized elements) from pure DeFi (open-source, fully decentralized governance). This distinction matters for CLARITY's developer protections: a GitHub commit by a solo developer is not the same as Hyperliquid's operation of a trading venue. The bill's current language removed protections for "non-controlling software developers" from the money transmitter definition, and Rebecca insists this line must hold to preserve on-chain innovation in the US.

The term "on-chain finance" itself is new and has not yet gone viral; the panel notes it sounds like a dot-com-era company name. However, it reflects a genuine regulatory distinction: as more traditional institutions use on-chain infrastructure for trading, the regulator's job is to separate (a) the developers building the tools from (b) the operators running trading venues. CLARITY's developer protection language is the vehicle for this separation.

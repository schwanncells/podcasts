# Planet Money — "Two indicators for lowering the rent"

**Host:** Darian Woods. **Guests:** Waylon Wong (co-host); Stephen Billings (professor of real estate, University of Colorado Boulder); Lori Goodman (director, Housing Finance Policy Center, Urban Institute); Adrienne Todman (CEO, National Rental Home Council); Rebecca Baird-Remba (housing journalist, former beat reporter for New York housing); Paul Freitag (executive director, Westside Federation for Senior and Supportive Housing).

## TL;DR

- **Institutional investors own less than 1% of single-family homes nationwide**, yet bipartisan legislation (21st Century Road to Housing Act) restricts their purchases; experts warn the ban could backfire by freezing "build-to-rent" construction, which added 1 in 12 new rental homes in 2024.
- **Corporate landlords improve rundown properties**: institutional investors buy homes in worse condition than average and spend tens of thousands on repairs; they finance renovations where homeowners face 40% denial rates.
- **Institutional ownership correlates with modest crime increases**: neighborhoods with more corporate landlords saw 2% more property crime, 4% more violent crime, and 7% more drug crime; however, renters moving to better neighborhoods gain enormous long-term benefits for children (per Harvard economist Raj Chetty research).
- **Single-room occupancy buildings (SROs) nearly vanished from American cities**: in the 1950s New York had 200,000+ SRO units (10% of rental stock); aggressive urban renewal regulations and housing codes drove 1 million rooms out of service during the 1970s.
- **SRO closures likely fueled homelessness**: about half of men entering New York homeless shelters in the 1980s had previously lived in SROs; Rebecca Baird-Remba estimates that if SRO construction had kept pace with other housing, the U.S. would have 2.5 million more rooms today—well above the homeless population.
- **SROs were dirt cheap but are now mostly gone**: boarding rooms cost $5-$10 per night historically (roughly $100/month adjusted for inflation); modern SROs like Euclid Hall on the Upper West Side combine shared facilities with supportive services, helping residents like 77-year-old Vera Hill maintain independence.
- **Zoning reforms are slowly allowing SROs back**: Washington state and Oregon have made the strongest legal moves to legalize new SRO construction; New York Mayor Zoran Mandani's May 2026 housing plan promised legislation to bring back shared housing.

**Corporate Landlords and Housing Affordability**

Amanda Cantrell, a renter in Murfreesboro, Tennessee, noticed that large corporations owned most houses in her neighborhood—a pattern that sparked bipartisan concern. The 21st Century Road to Housing Act, passed with broad political support, restricts institutional investors from owning too many single-family homes. However, Stephen Billings's research reveals a more nuanced picture: while the laws reflect genuine anxiety, institutional investors account for less than 1% of home purchases nationally. The real drivers of housing costs are low construction and low interest rates.

Billings notes that institutional investors can actually lower rental prices by expanding the rental market. Lori Goodman points out that large firms buy homes in worse condition than average and rehabilitate them efficiently—they purchase supplies and labor in bulk and can secure financing where homeowners face steep denial rates. Institutional investors built about 1 in every 12 new rental homes in 2024 through "build-to-rent" communities. Laurie warns that restricting these purchases could chill new construction, potentially making housing affordability worse. Adrienne Todman, CEO of the National Rental Home Council, echoes this concern, noting the unintended consequence of discouraging exactly the activity designed to increase housing supply.

**The Downside: Crime and Community Effects**

Billings has also documented negative effects. Neighborhoods with higher concentrations of corporate landlords compared to individual homeowners experienced a 2% increase in property crime, a 4% increase in violent crime, and a 7% increase in drug crime. However, this finding sits alongside compelling evidence that renting allows low-income families to move to neighborhoods with better schools and greater social support—research by Harvard economist Raj Chetty shows enormous long-term benefits for children from these moves, with no detrimental effects for higher-income children already in those neighborhoods.

Amanda Cantrell, despite her initial concerns about corporate ownership, ended up renting from a large management company and rated her experience four out of five stars; her rent even decreased slightly on renewal. The broader evidence suggests institutional investors are not the primary villains of the housing crisis.

**The Rise and Fall of Single-Room Occupancy Housing**

The second part of the episode examines single-room occupancy (SRO) buildings—boarding houses, dorm-style facilities, and long-term hotel rooms that once formed a crucial part of America's housing stock. Vera Hill, a 77-year-old former health administrator, now lives in a modern SRO called Euclid Hall on the Upper West Side, where she shares a bathroom but has her own room decorated with personal photos and art. The building is operated by the Westside Federation for Senior and Supportive Housing and provides on-site social support.

Rebecca Baird-Remba traces the explosive growth of SROs to the end of the Civil War and the surge of rural-to-urban migration and immigration. Landlords converted warehouses, commercial buildings, and apartments into boarding houses. These rooms were extraordinarily cheap—as little as five to ten cents a night (roughly $100 per month accounting for inflation)—though the bare-bones versions were austere cubicles with chicken wire dividers to prevent theft.

By the 1950s, New York alone had more than 200,000 SRO units, representing more than 10% of the city's rental housing stock. Similar concentrations existed in Chicago and San Francisco. However, by mid-century many SRO buildings had fallen into disrepair. Under the banner of "urban renewal," cities wrote increasingly stringent housing codes for sunlight, heating, fire safety, and minimum unit sizes, often motivated by both charitable intentions and classism. The drive to eliminate SROs intensified: during the 1970s, roughly 1 million rooms were eliminated or converted.

**The Homelessness Connection**

The evictions at San Francisco's International Hotel in 1977—where sheriff and riot police forced out over 100 mostly elderly Filipino and Chinese residents while more than 2,000 protesters gathered—symbolized the height of SRO destruction. A congressional report released months later linked SRO closures to rising homelessness. By the 1980s, about half of men entering New York homeless shelters said they had previously lived in SROs. Baird-Remba's research suggests that had SRO construction grown at the same pace as other housing, the 1 million eliminated rooms would translate to 2.5 million additional rooms today—far exceeding the entire U.S. homeless population.

Policymakers eventually recognized the mistake. Recent efforts to bring back SROs have been piecemeal, though Washington state and Oregon have made relatively strong zoning changes to legalize new construction. New York Mayor Zoran Mandani released a housing plan in May 2026 promising legislation to expand shared housing.

**Modern SROs and Their Tradeoffs**

Paul Freitag, who runs the Westside Federation, supports legalizing more boarding houses, especially for younger and middle-aged people. SROs can be built inexpensively and offer a stepping stone for those establishing themselves. However, he candidly notes the drawbacks: aging in dense shared spaces is challenging; residents are more prone to communicable diseases like COVID and flu; and accommodating mobility aids, home health aides, and incontinence care becomes difficult.

Vera Hill, despite these limitations, expresses contentment with her SRO, noting she has no complaints. Unlike the bare cubicles of the past, modern SROs like Euclid Hall combine affordable housing with active support services, allowing residents to maintain independence and dignity.

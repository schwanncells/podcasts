**Market Turmoil and Saylor's Distraction Effect**

The episode opens on a weekend market crash. Bitcoin dropped from $60K support to $59K on liquidations and panic. The catalyst was multifold: Michael Saylor sold 32 Bitcoin (~$3M), NASDAQ sank on jobs data raising odds of rate hikes, and Fed rate expectations shifted toward holds rather than cuts. Polymarket prices a 50-50 chance of a Fed rate raise this year. The broader crypto and risk-asset environment is jittery, though Bitcoin recovered above $62K by the episode's taping. Mert notes that Saylor's greatest effect is not price impact but distortion of the industry narrative. Instead of discussing Bitcoin's adoption, philosophy, or tech, "a trillion people pocket watch one guy on the internet." ETFs and MicroStrategy both function identically—displacing retail buys rather than bringing new institutional capital—yet the market fixates on Saylor's daily moves. Tom agrees: the conference scene has become a "celebrity lineup" with no real technical discussion of L2s, Lightning, or other innovations. The market runs on paradigms—"one thing that worked will always work until it completely fails"—and has locked onto Saylor as the new driver, starving the conversation of substance.

**The Zcash Infinite Mint Bug**

Taylor Hornby, a top Zcash cryptographer at Shielded Labs, discovered a critical bug in the Orchard ZK circuit during a planned audit using Claude Opus 4.8. The bug allowed unlimited counterfeit minting inside the shielded pool—which is 30% of total supply—and was undetectable by users. Had it been exploited, an attacker could have minted coins, withdrawn them, and made it impossible for legitimate holders to access their funds (analogous to a bank run). The flaw was disclosed responsibly; an emergency soft fork disabled the vulnerable transactions, and a formal migration to a new pool (codenamed Ironwood) is scheduled for early-to-mid July. Zuko's disclosure carefully noted: "we have no proof this bug was not exploited," though they believe it was not, and the July migration will force a full coin count to verify supply integrity. Mert stresses that Taylor is a top-five global Zcash expert, not a random hacker prompted to "find a bug"—the circuits had passed previous audits by renowned cryptographers and are used across other ecosystems. The bug was extremely subtle. News coverage created false panic: explorer nodes lagged after the fork, making the chain appear offline, which coincided with the general market bloodbath and wild FUD theories (e.g., someone minted infinite coins and paid KOLs to pump the price). Market reaction was brutal: ZEC fell from ~$600 to ~$250 peak-to-trough (over 50% in one day)—one of the largest single-day market-cap destructions in recent history.

**AI-Assisted Attacks and the Case for Formal Verification**

Ilya explains the asymmetry between defense and attack. Even if Zcash uses Opus 4.8, attackers have access to five different models, more compute time, and potentially thousands of people trying different approaches—they can ensemble multiple models and run RL loops against test environments to find exploits. This is why AI-driven vulnerability discovery (e.g., the Mythos debut) is marketing by Anthropic; the real issue is that all security experts with these models can now find bugs, and it doesn't matter which model they use. DeFi hacks started spiking in November 2024 when Opus 4 shipped; open-source models pulled in April, causing another wave. The only defense is **formal verification**: mathematically proving that a program satisfies an invariant (e.g., total supply never changes). Ilya describes the shift in cost: formal verification used to cost hundreds of dollars per line of code (manual proof by mathematicians for space programs). With AI, you can now auto-generate proofs at scale. The challenge is specification—AI must know *what* to prove, not just check code. Zcash's response is to formally verify the Ironwood pool with three separate teams and also deploy Tachyon, which uses entirely different circuit arithmetic and will be "mathematically perfect" for a ZK protocol. In the age of AI attackers, formal verification is "table stakes." Tom notes this mirrors the evolution from building boats (expected to take water) to spaceships (must be airtight)—crypto code can no longer tolerate leaks because a single exploit is hacked. The market is learning this lesson in real-time.

**OPSEC Collapse and Real-Time Anomaly Detection**

Ilya explains that formal verification solves code bugs (ZK circuit underspecification, arithmetic errors, admin withdrawal functions) but not operational security: oracle manipulation, multi-sig key theft, and governance exploits. OPSEC standards collapsed post-2023—new projects skip proper key management and launch recklessly. The fix is AI-powered anomaly detection running in real-time. Mert's team at NEAR is building this: they detect hacks on other chains by monitoring for anomalies (e.g., an account with no prior transaction history suddenly moving huge funds). Traditional finance learned this with fraud detection on credit cards; TradFi waits three days and KYCs the account. Crypto can't afford delays—an AI system running autonomously under governance rules can flag and freeze suspicious accounts in seconds. This balances cypherpunk values with pragmatism: the system is governed by slower governance (humans decide rules), but execution is autonomous and real-time. Ilya gives the example of Arbitrum's emergency council (9-of-12 multi-sig) intervening on North Korea sanctions; they *had* the means to stop it and did. By contrast, Tornado Cash is immutable—they literally can't stop crime, and governments still prosecute. The cypherpunk ideal is to *structure* systems so you don't have control to begin with. But if you do have control and don't use it, you're just performing virtue signaling. The practical path forward is combining formal verification for code correctness, OPSEC discipline, and real-time AI-driven anomaly detection.

**NEAR's AI Agent Vision**

NEAR is repositioning itself around AI agents settling transactions on chain. Ilya frames it as two technologies: AI (single-player interface) and blockchain (multiplayer coordination). They work together—agents negotiate with other agents, settle deals on blockchain, and identify each other through decentralized infrastructure. The intent model (outcome specification) is the next abstraction layer above transactions; instead of saying "send X to address Y," you say "I want goods delivered," and agents figure out the execution path across chains, APIs, and off-chain services. Near Intents is the financial infrastructure; Near AI provides the LLM layer. The agent marketplace lets AI agents discover and hire each other. Private intents (confidential transactions) are critical because businesses don't want public accounting—NEAR launched this, seeing strong traction. Example: an event agency has an agent that knows every venue in every city; you ask your agent to find a venue in Lisbon for a Dragonfly meetup, it privately pays the agency's agent $1 instead of burning tokens on long inference, and the job is done. Another: background checks for e-commerce. Mert is skeptical—he's told Ilya to "stop talking about AI and focus on trading"—but acknowledges that NEAR is "the most underrated team in crypto" along with Zcash and Solana. The real value, Ilya explains, is trustless interaction: when you buy from a Chinese factory you don't trust, your agent and theirs need blockchain-mediated escrow, dispute resolution, and reputation. During the bloodbath Friday, NEAR did $240M in trading volume—at their current fee structure, that's more inflation than a year. On Friday, $6.8B in liquidations rippled across assets, yet NEAR's deflationary day shows the model is working. Tom notes he doesn't have a clear vision for agents in the next year but agrees the team is "super cracked" and Ilya "will figure something out."

**War on Bugs: Formal Verification at Scale**

The Zcash hardening illustrates a shift across the industry. After discovering Orchard's bug, multiple Zcash core teams began aggressive red-teaming, auditing not just Orchard but upcoming pools (Tachyon, Quantum) and using formal verification in parallel. The Ironwood migration in July will include a "turnstile" check (headcount at end of field trip) ensuring no counterfeit coins snuck in. Ironwood itself is formally verified by three teams and quantum-recoverable. Tachyon uses completely different circuit arithmetic to eliminate another class of bugs. Mert is "long math"—the trend in computing has always been toward better security, cryptography, and verification, and unless physics forbids mathematically perfect systems, formal verification + AI will eventually win the war. Some battles will be lost, but the war will be won through continuous error correction and hardened systems. The framework reconciling cypherpunk ideals with pragmatism is autonomous AI systems running within protocol bounds, governed by slower layers (community decides rules, agents execute at real-time speed). This is not immutability; Bitcoin or Ethereum can upgrade tomorrow if they choose. It's about distributing control and timing: code is proven correct, operations are monitored by AI, and governance is human-paced. The field is moving from "code is law, no exceptions" to "code is law until the code is wrong, then we use real law and formal proof to fix it."

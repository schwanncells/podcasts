# The Weekly Show with Jon Stewart — "The Other Side of the (Bit)Coin with Ben McKenzie"

**Host:** Jon Stewart. **Guest:** Ben McKenzie (actor, author of *Easy Money: Economics*; director and producer of *Everyone Is Lying to You for Money*; cryptocurrency fraud investigator and advocate).

## TL;DR

- **Bitcoin processes 5–7 transactions per second; Visa processes 24,000.** Blockchain's speed limitation makes it unviable as a global payments system, even by Bitcoin's own architects' admission.
- **Cryptocurrency is functionally a Ponzi scheme with a mathematical veneer.** The only real use case is speculation—betting the price will rise so you can sell to a greater fool. Utility claims collapse under scrutiny; all proposed advantages are either false or already solved by regulated systems.
- **Stablecoins launder ~$154 billion in illicit activity annually.** Tether, the largest stablecoin, is brokered by Cantor Fitzgerald (whose owner, Howard Lutnick, is now Commerce Secretary). Russian oligarchs use stablecoins to sell sanctioned oil to China for weapons; North Korea has funded half its nuclear program via crypto; ISIS, cartels, and human traffickers all operate through this system.
- **Jeffrey Epstein funded Bitcoin Core development and Blockstream,** discovered in the released Epstein files. He was investing in the infrastructure of a system that now facilitates the exact crimes for which he was indicted.
- **Trump's meme coin extracted tens of millions from retail investors while insiders profited.** The President of the United States is the poster child for crypto's core promise: extract wealth from the masses through a counterfeit currency backed by nothing.
- **The crypto industry donated $240 million in 2024—40% of all corporate donations in the cycle.** This exceeds the defense and pharmaceutical industries combined and has successfully captured Congress: 100 Democrats voted for the GENIUS Act, which allows corporations to issue their own fiat currencies.
- **Crypto advocates argue it will free people from oppressive governments; in practice, it has become the plumbing of authoritarianism.** Rogue states, cartels, oligarchs, and corporate titans use it to evade sanctions and move contraband. The decentralized currency fantasy has been captured by the most centralized power structures on Earth.

**What Crypto Actually Is**

Ben McKenzie explains that Bitcoin started as a 2008 white paper proposing peer-to-peer transactions without banks—an idea that might have sounded compelling during the financial crisis but was never more than a story told to justify speculation. Cryptocurrency is simply lines of code stored on a ledger called a blockchain. The pseudonymity (not anonymity) of the ledger obscures which wallets are transacting, making it appealing for criminal use. The first major use case was the Silk Road, where buyers purchased drugs and even hired assassins. McKenzie stresses that you cannot invest in cryptocurrency—you can only speculate on it. There is no intrinsic value, no cash flow, no asset backing it. You are betting that someone else will pay more for the same lines of code tomorrow.

**Bitcoin's Technical Obsolescence**

Bitcoin's fundamental limitation is throughput. It can process only 5–7 transactions per second, compared with Visa's 24,000 per second. This speed gap makes Bitcoin categorically unsuitable as a currency replacement, a fact even early crypto advocates acknowledge. The technological argument that blockchain represents an advance in cryptography is false—actual cryptographers view blockchain as "primitive." More advanced blockchain systems exist but cannot match Bitcoin's limited supply of coins without breaking the scarcity argument that props up its market price.

**The Corporate Takeover of Mining**

Early Bitcoin mining could be done on a home computer. Now, mining is concentrated in industrial-scale operations. McKenzie visited the largest Bitcoin mine in the United States, housed in a former Alcoa aluminum smelting plant outside Austin, Texas. These facilities are football-field-sized warehouses stacked with servers, consuming enough electricity to reach 110 degrees Fahrenheit in summer Texas. Mining is now the exclusive domain of multibillion-dollar corporations. The economics work only when Bitcoin's price is rising; in downturns, even these massive operations become unprofitable.

**El Salvador's Bitcoin Experiment: A Nation-State Pump-and-Dump**

President Nayib Bukele of El Salvador marketed Bitcoin adoption as a way to reduce remittance fees. Remittances constitute a quarter of El Salvador's economy; sending money home via MoneyGram or Western Union incurs significant fees. Bukele's government launched a Bitcoin-based system with a 30-dollar giveaway to all citizens. The result was immediate and catastrophic: Bitcoin's price fell 20 percent in an hour, exchanges crashed, and the system collapsed. McKenzie theorizes this was a coordinated pump-and-dump—a government with advance knowledge of a major publicity event sold its holdings before retail investors could buy in, dumping the price before the price could rise. El Salvador is now sitting on billions of dollars in Bitcoin losses.

**Tether, Lutnick, and Sanctions Evasion**

Tether is the largest stablecoin by far—supposedly pegged 1:1 to the U.S. dollar to provide stability without volatility. Its broker is Cantor Fitzgerald, and its financial interests are represented by Howard Lutnick, who is now Secretary of Commerce under Trump. A crypto company estimated that $154 billion in illicit activity was financed via stablecoins in the past year. Specific examples: Russian oligarchs sell sanctioned oil to Chinese buyers in exchange for cryptocurrency. Iran has been shut out of the U.S. banking system; ships transiting the Strait of Hormuz now pay Iranian suppliers in cryptocurrency. These are not hypothetical scenarios—they have happened and continue to happen. The pseudonymity of blockchain means transactions cannot be traced even if law enforcement requests data from Tether; the company's standard response is that money can be used for good or ill, so they bear no responsibility.

**The Epstein Connection**

In the released Epstein files, emails reveal that Jeffrey Epstein was funding Bitcoin Core development—the group of programmers who maintain Bitcoin's core software—through MIT Media Lab. Epstein's funding kept Bitcoin development alive during a period of languishing interest (around 2015, after the Silk Road shutdown). Later emails, confirmed by the co-founder of Blockstream, show Epstein also invested in Blockstream, a company credited with laying the intellectual foundation for Bitcoin. Epstein's involvement in both sex trafficking and money laundering makes his investment in the infrastructure of a pseudonymous, traceless payment system deeply sinister. The fact that he funded the system used today to move weapons, escape sanctions, and finance terrorist organizations is not a coincidence.

**Crypto as Electoral Capture**

The crypto industry spent $240 million in political donations during the 2024 election cycle—40 percent of all corporate donations that cycle, exceeding the defense and pharmaceutical industries combined. McKenzie testified before Congress on crypto fraud; in response, the crypto lobby spent $40 million to defeat Sherrod Brown, the chairman of the Senate Banking Committee, replacing him with a pro-crypto car salesman. Despite the crypto industry's Republican branding, 100 Democrats voted for the GENIUS Act, which allows corporations to issue their own stablecoins and effectively create private fiat currencies outside the regulated system. McKenzie interviewed several Democratic Congress members who voted for it; none could articulate a technical understanding of what they voted for. They were simply afraid of the industry's money.

**Trump's Meme Coin and the Legitimacy Pump**

Bitcoin's price and trading volume collapsed in 2022 after a long bear market. The industry was in crisis, major firms were bankrupt, and prominent figures faced jail time. Trump's return to politics and embrace of cryptocurrency in summer 2024 reversed the trend. Trump created a meme coin (not Bitcoin, but a separate speculative token) and hawked it directly to his supporters. Reports show that Trump made more money from four months of the meme coin than he earned during four years operating his Washington, D.C. hotel during his first presidency. The coin's price was bid up through massive hype; early and insider investors profited while retail supporters who bought near the peak lost money. Trump's presidency has legitimized crypto in the eyes of retail speculators, driving Bitcoin from around $40,000 to over $120,000 in just months.

**Wash Trading and Market Manipulation**

The pseudonymity of blockchain enables wash trading—an investor can send cryptocurrency from one wallet to another without revealing they control both, artificially bidding up the price and creating the illusion of demand. McKenzie and co-author Jacob Silverman document a vivid example in *Everyone Is Lying to You for Money*: two traders on Binance, one long (betting the price would rise) and one short (betting it would fall), both tried to exit positions as the price started collapsing. The exchange crashed for hours. When it came back online, the price had rebounded, but both traders were liquidated. Neither profited; the exchange and insiders did. This pattern has repeated across multiple crashes.

**North Korea's Nuclear Weapons Program and Crypto**

Intelligence agencies estimate that half of North Korea's nuclear weapons program has been funded through cryptocurrency theft and ransomware. McKenzie cites reporting from the Wall Street Journal. North Korea maintains teams of elite hackers who steal cryptocurrency from exchanges and private wallets. The fact that a rogue state's weapons development is being subsidized through a system the U.S. government has chosen not to regulate is a national security catastrophe.

**Stablecoins and Rogue States**

Beyond North Korea and Iran, stablecoins are the payment rails for Russian weapons procurement, Chinese drug trafficking, and South American cartel operations. ISIS has used them. Human traffickers use them. The system is explicitly designed to obscure the identity of transacting parties—that obscurity is the entire marketing point. When law enforcement has successfully compelled Tether and other stablecoin operators to provide transaction data, those operators have complied only under threat of prosecution. Otherwise, they claim they have no way to identify who is moving what.

**Crypto as MLM and Ponzi Hybrid**

Cryptocurrency functions as a hybrid of multi-level marketing and Ponzi scheme. Early adopters who bought Bitcoin at a penny can sell at $120,000 to new entrants. The system cannot sustain itself—it requires constant inflows of new money. The strongest psychological argument crypto advocates make is: "You wish you bought early." McKenzie notes this is not a critique of his criticism; it is a description of how Ponzi schemes work. Tulip mania operated on the same dynamic: a product gains value, the rising price draws in new buyers, whose buying pressure drives the price higher, until it collapses.

**The Failure of Regulation and Democratic Capture**

The SEC has authority over securities; the CFTC has authority over commodities. Crypto was randomly classified as a commodity in 2015 because crypto futures were being sold and the CFTC claimed jurisdiction. However, crypto operates like a security—it generates no cash flow, has no intrinsic value, and is purely speculative. Securities law requires disclosure of who is issuing the asset and how their money is being used. The crypto industry is terrified of this; it wants the weaker CFTC to retain jurisdiction. Sam Bankman-Fried, before his fraud was exposed, lobbied Congress for a bill giving the CFTC regulatory authority—his goal was ensuring the weaker regulator oversaw his industry.

**The Inevitability of Collapse**

McKenzie describes crypto as "the largest Ponzi scheme in history." It may reinflate—it has done so before—but all Ponzi schemes collapse eventually. The crypto industry has deliberately recreated subprime mortgage dynamics. Wall Street firms now hold crypto assets through ETFs. If a major correction occurs (which history suggests is likely during an economic slowdown), the interconnection between Wall Street and crypto could trigger systemic contagion. The cure for crypto's problems is not better crypto; it is fixing the underlying failures in the regulated financial system that make crypto appealing in the first place.

**The Path Forward**

McKenzie advocates for classifying all speculative cryptocurrencies as securities, subjecting them to disclosure requirements and SEC oversight. He works with organizations like Americans for Financial Reform to push back against regulatory capture. However, he acknowledges the cryptocurrency lobby's overwhelming financial advantage. The only counterweight is grassroots advocacy, shaming, and consistent exposure of the system's corruption and criminal use. The alternative—allowing corporate currencies, sanctioned evasion, weapons financing, and organized crime to flow through an unregulated, pseudonymous ledger while the government collects taxes in real dollars—is the final victory of corporate power over democratic control.

# Time To Say Goodbye — "The Rage of the Downwardly Mobile College Graduate with Noam Scheiber"

**Host:** Tyler Cowen (and Jay). **Guest:** Noam Scheiber (reporter, The New York Times; author of *Mutiny: the Rise and Revolt of the College Educated Working Class*).

## TL;DR

- **College graduates told education was imperative, then hit wage stagnation and debt** — a tenfold increase in AP course-taking (early 1980s to 2010s) reflected massive sell on college's value, but median wages for college grads were flat 2000–present while student debt tripled from mid-1990s to mid-2010s, creating felt downward mobility even if nominal wages stayed steady.
- **"Safety valve" jobs (non-degree-requiring roles for college grads) vanished after 2003–2004** — data from the New York Fed shows insurance agents, HR roles, and similar stopgap careers that once let graduates make decent livings without "failure" disappeared starting in the early 2000s and never recovered after the Great Recession.
- **Apple and Starbucks went from elevating retail to pure cost-cutting after 2008** — Steve Jobs recruited college grads, funded intensive training (two-week offsites, reverse shadowing, ceremony with branded T-shirt), offered good benefits and stock; Tim Cook cut creatives from one-on-one mentorship to scripted marketing classes to just iPhone sales, while Starbucks removed healthcare for part-timers and added unpredictable scheduling.
- **Customized drinks (e.g., "foam this chocolate something matcha dust") became a billiondollar revenue trap that destroyed barista morale** — Howard Schultz's custom-drink explosion pulled in multi-billion-dollar revenue but made jobs unsustainable; Starbucks capped service times (30 seconds at drive-thru window) and used software to cut labor costs, creating four-hour shifts where workers go home after 1:12 minutes because demand fell.
- **Bernie Sanders gave workers vocabulary for their lived experience** — Teddy Hoffman, a Watson Fellow at Grinnell working at Starbucks, watched 2016 primaries, had abstract left politics, then lived through pandemic understaffing and dehumanizing customer encounters (a customer looking at his name tag: "I didn't know you guys had names"), connecting material conditions to organizing.
- **October 7th became wind at Starbucks union's back** — a union organizer posted "solidarity with Palestine" tweet (removed in 40 minutes), which exploded into boycott calls globally; Starbucks sued for trademark infringement, but CEO had to admit to investors that geopolitical forces crushed business momentum, then negotiated peace with union, allowing 20–30 stores/month to organize without opposition.
- **Graduate student organizing shifted from anti-military-industrial complex (1960s–70s) to material conditions under Taylorization** — adjuncts teaching six classes grading hundreds of papers, non-tenure-track positions jumping from 50–50 to 60–40 by 2010s, no tenure prospects even with PhD, making UAW organizing about alienation of labor rather than ideology.
- **Elite institutions amplified labor discourse — digital media unionization planted seeds in tech workers' minds** — Vox, BuzzFeed, Vice, and Gawker organizing stories reached tech workers consuming the same outlets; Google walkout over sexual harassment, Alphabet Workers Union minority status, Kickstarter unionization, Microsoft video game tester organizing all followed this media precedent.

**Three Overlapping Trends: Downward Mobility, Expectations, and Progressive Brands**

Scheiber frames the book around three converging forces. The first is downward mobility: the destruction or degrading of jobs that had once been good. The second is a historically unique layering-on of expectations. College was sold to this generation as an imperative, not a luxury. President Obama, families, friends, guidance counselors all hammered the message. Participation in AP courses surged tenfold between the early 1980s and 2010s, reflecting how completely college dominated the aspirations of elementary and high school students. When graduates encountered the job market, that collision between expectation and reality was "pretty disillusioning and radicalizing."

The third trend concerns companies with progressive branding that had historically walked the walk. Starbucks was early to offer healthcare to part-timers and stock ownership; when the company went public in the 1990s, that stock became very valuable. Apple recruited college grads, trained them intensively, and offered good benefits and stock grants. But in the post-financial-crisis world, these jobs got "inshittified." Benefits contracted. Companies shifted to contractors and temps. Scheduling became unpredictable. Career pathways vanished at the exact moment the broader economy stopped welcoming college graduates.

**The Data on Wage Stagnation and Debt Burden**

Median wages for college graduates were essentially flat between 2000 and the present—a point of some debate between Scheiber and critics like Eric Levitz at Vox, who frame it as stagnation rather than downward mobility. But Scheiber points to a New York Fed dataset tracking job market prospects of recent college grads. Before the early 2000s, college graduates who took jobs that didn't require a degree—insurance agent, HR manager—could still make decent livings. This "safety valve" functioned for decades. Around 2003–2004, those jobs began disappearing and never recovered, even after the Great Recession. Combining wage data with student debt tells a starker story. College debt tripled from the mid-1990s to the mid-2010s. If wages are flat but debt service is consuming a portion of every paycheck—money earlier generations of graduates didn't have to pay—the felt experience is downward mobility, regardless of nominal wage figures.

**Apple Under Steve Jobs vs. Tim Cook**

Under Jobs, the Apple Store was intentionally elevated. Jobs recruited college grads specifically—people "thoughtful and well-spoken and nimble." The titles ("Genius," "Creative," "Expert") were designed to appeal to status anxiety. Apple even claimed it was harder to get hired at an Apple Store than to get into Harvard. Training was elaborate: two-week offsites, training seminars on Apple philosophy, reverse shadowing (first you watch someone, then they watch you), and finally a ceremony presenting your official Apple retail T-shirt. It was designed to feel like pledging a secret society. Jobs was betting on these workers as Apple evangelists—indoctrinating them into the Apple worldview.

Tim Cook, an operations person obsessed with efficiency, took over after Jobs died. He viewed inventory as "fundamentally evil" and set about rationalizing Apple Stores. His first hire, John Browett from a British consumer electronics retailer, tried cutting labor costs aggressively; even the executives revolted, so Cook retreated temporarily. But over time, the trajectory was clear: more temps, more contractors, less predictable scheduling. The most visible example is the "Creative" job. Under Jobs, Creatives were one-on-one Apple coaches, offering unlimited mentorship for $99/year customers. Under Cook, this transformed into "Today at Apple"—free group classes with strict talking points, no autonomy for the teacher, increasingly transparent marketing exercises. One creative told Scheiber about a class on the Magic Keyboard: "It was the dumbest class ever. It was so transparently about marketing." Eventually, Creatives were just pulled onto the sales floor to sell iPhones like everyone else. They had gone from bespoke Apple guru to iPhone hustler, collecting AppleCare warranties—itself a rebranding of "extended warranty" that drove customers to buy at higher rates than when it was called that.

**Starbucks: Custom Drinks and Taylorization**

Howard Schultz's custom-drink strategy was brilliant business—a multi-billion-dollar revenue line over a decade. But it was hell for baristas. Drinks exploded in complexity; Teddy Hoffman, one of Scheiber's key subjects, talks about how once you get above a few modifications, you're "basically serving them mud." Starbucks applied Taylorization—Frederick Winslow Taylor's early-20th-century factory efficiency methods—to espresso. The company tracked metrics ruthlessly: don't spend more than 30 seconds at the drive-thru window. They deployed software to minimize labor costs, resulting in unpredictable shifts where a four-hour shift became one hour and twelve minutes because demand fell and the manager sent people home, with zero pay for the remaining three hours.

**Bernie Sanders, Abstract Politics, and Lived Experience**

Teddy Hoffman is Scheiber's example of how Sanders' 2015–2016 campaign planted ideological seeds that only germinated when lived reality matched the language. Teddy was raised Catholic, got mad at his church's campaign against gay marriage, and moved left vaguely. He won a Watson Fellowship, traveled in 2015 watching the primaries, and was intrigued by Bernie's "practical and concrete" language about affordability. It stayed abstract. But when he returned to Chicago and worked at Starbucks, he and a friend (also into Bernie) fixated on Trump-Bernie voters—how was that possible? They drilled down on dignity of work, power at work, the humiliation of powerlessness. In 2017, a customer looked at Teddy's name tag and said, "I didn't know you guys had names"—meaning name tags. The casual dehumanization hit hard. That vocabulary Bernie had given him suddenly described his daily lived experience. By the pandemic, when Starbucks abandoned safety protocols and unleashed pent-up demand on understaffed stores, Teddy was ready to organize.

**October 7th and the Union's Unexpected Leverage**

A few days after October 7th, a Starbucks Workers United organizer posted solidarity with Palestine—a bulldozer image—under the union's Twitter handle. It was up for 40 minutes before removal. The union initially panicked; they were already fighting a major employer, and they'd just invited a third-rail political issue. Right-wing voices erupted. The Orthodox Jewish Chamber of Commerce initiated a boycott. Senator Rick Scott of Florida denounced the union. It looked like a death blow.

Instead, Starbucks' aggressive response backfired. The company sued the union for trademark infringement (people were confusing Starbucks corporate with the union's politics). In January 2024, CEO Laxman Narasimhan had to tell investors that "political forces beyond our control" had crushed momentum. He was oblique, but the implication was clear: the boycott was hurting the bottom line. Young customers—the demographic Starbucks relied on for expensive custom drinks—polled as unsympathetic to Israel post-October 7th. Attacking union workers who expressed solidarity with Palestinians was a political disaster. Starbucks negotiated peace: they agreed not to fight unionization, allowed 20–30 stores per month to organize without opposition, and, crucially, began negotiating actual contracts.

**Graduate Student Organizing: From Ideology to Material Conditions**

Graduate student organizing in the 1960s–70s was ideological: keep Dow Chemical off campus; universities were implicated in the military-industrial complex. By the 1990s, the story shifted. Universities began off-loading teaching to adjuncts and grad students ("Taylorization of academia"). Adjuncts taught six classes, graded hundreds of papers. The UAW, credible on campus because of its ideological commitments, became the right organizing partner—not for principle now, but because they understood alienation of labor and assembly-line mentality.

Over 25 years, adjunct and grad student prospects only worsened. Non-tenure-track positions went from roughly 50–50 in the early 2000s to 60–40 by 2010s. But that aggregate masks the steeper decline for newly minted PhDs; your odds of getting tenure track are far worse than 40–60. Grad students told Scheiber the same thing: "This isn't going to lead to anything for me." Without the ceiling of a career, they had nothing to lose by organizing now and demanding better pay and conditions.

**Elite Persuasion and the Role of Visibility**

Critics argued that college-educated union organizers at prestigious institutions like Starbucks and grad students at UC Berkeley weren't "real" working-class campaigns and wouldn't resonate outside academia and media. Scheiber pushes back. First, he notes that online media unionization—Vox, BuzzFeed, Vice, Gawker—became a vector for tech worker organizing. These outlets covered their own unionization campaigns. Tech workers, consuming the same digital media, saw the vocabulary and concept of organizing. Google's sexual harassment walkout followed, Alphabet Workers Union formed, Kickstarter unionized, Microsoft video game testers organized. The visibility of media coverage planted seeds.

Second, John Logan, a labor studies professor at San Francisco State, points out that the visibility of Starbucks and Amazon campaigns introduced the very concept of organizing to mainstream consciousness. You see Starbucks and Amazon everywhere; you rarely see auto plants. Your relationship to Starbucks is personal—you go there daily. When you see workers unionizing at a place you frequent, it becomes salient in a way that GM or Ford organizing might not.

**NBA Tanking and the Moral Case Against the Draft**

In a final riff, the hosts discuss NBA tanking (teams deliberately losing to improve draft position odds) and Tyler's long-standing proposal: eliminate the draft entirely. Let teams bid on emerging players, offer them direct employment contracts. The counter-argument is that wealthy owners would dominate. Tyler's response: yes, probably—like soccer. But soccer isn't broken. And without a draft, there's no draft to tank for. The deeper point is that the draft is morally indefensible: it tells eighteen-year-olds where they must work, at 20% of their market value (Victor Wembanyama is worth ~$50M/year but makes ~$12M on the rookie scale), with zero choice. Salary caps exist to enforce competitive parity, but that parity comes at the cost of workers' freedom. Restoring agency to the workers would create team inequality—but that's the trade-off.

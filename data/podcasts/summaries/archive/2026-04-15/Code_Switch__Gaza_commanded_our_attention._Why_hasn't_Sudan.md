# Code Switch — "Gaza commanded our attention. Why hasn't Sudan?"

**Host:** Gene Demby. **Guests:** Ismail Koushkoush (writer and journalist, former Khartoum resident); Scott Strauss (department chair of political science, UC Berkeley; genocide expert); Alex Duval (expert on Sudanese history); May Hessen (political scientist, MIT; faculty director of MIT Africa; of Sudanese descent).

## TL;DR

- **Sudan's genocide was formally recognized by the U.S. in January 2025** — Secretary of State Anthony Blinken stated that the RSF and allied militias had committed genocide, yet public outcry and government action have been minimal compared to Gaza coverage.
- **Over 150,000 dead, 12 million displaced** — the Sudanese civil war between the government (SAF) and the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) since 2023 is among the deadliest conflicts on the planet, but receives a fraction of media attention.
- **Journalists fled or were displaced** — covering the Sudan war is extremely dangerous, contributing to sparse international reporting; journalists who documented early conflicts were among those who had to leave the country.
- **Lack of familiarity breeds apathy** — most Americans know little about Sudan outside of conflict narratives. Press freedom in Sudan ranked 174th out of 180 countries in 2015, limiting the flow of non-conflict stories that might build empathy.
- **African stereotypes shape perception** — racist assumptions that Africans are "inherently more violent" than other populations, combined with the false belief that genocide happens primarily in Africa, contribute to dismissal of African crises as inevitable rather than preventable.
- **Domestic American focus and politicization of "genocide"** — Americans are preoccupied with domestic issues (immigration, birthright citizenship, wars begun "out of nowhere"); Israel receives $3.8 billion in annual U.S. aid, making the Gaza conflict feel directly tied to U.S. policy in ways Sudan does not.
- **Gaza's genocide framing has poisoned discourse** — intense political battles over whether to use the term "genocide" for Gaza (met with accusations of antisemitism when used by pro-Palestinian voices) have made many hesitant to engage with genocide discussions elsewhere.
- **American interventionism has failed** — the 2006 Darfur campaign, while generating massive celebrity-backed rallies, failed to address root causes; the imposed Western solutions did not resolve underlying issues, and the underlying grievances festered for 20 years.

**Sudan's Invisibility in American Consciousness**

Gene Demby and Leah Dinella open by revisiting a listener's question about why Sudan's genocide receives so little attention compared to Gaza. The listener notes that while Americans intensely debate whether Gaza constitutes genocide, they have largely ignored the U.S. government's own determination that genocide is occurring in Sudan. Dinella explains that the civil war, which began in April 2023, has killed an estimated 150,000 people (possibly many more) and displaced 12 million—making it one of the world's largest humanitarian crises. Yet while some Americans feel strongly about Gaza due to direct U.S. military support to Israel, very few seem aware that genocide is happening in Sudan, much less mobilized to respond.

**The Journalistic Vacuum and Barriers to Coverage**

One structural reason for Sudan's invisibility is the near-total absence of international journalists on the ground. Ismail Koushkoush, a writer and journalist who lived in Khartoum for eight years before fleeing the 2023 war, describes the city as home to relatively few international reporters even before the conflict erupted. Major news outlets would typically dispatch correspondents from Cairo, Nairobi, or Johannesburg only when covering major events, meaning that Americans received a diet of conflict-focused stories with little context. Koushkoush recalls being trapped in his apartment two blocks from the presidential palace on April 15, 2023, hearing gunfire and recording videos from his window as the war began. Electricity failed within days, water shortly after—and in April temperatures reaching 110–120 degrees, he and his neighbors were trapped in the building for nine days. Most eventually fled the country. The displacement of Sudanese journalists themselves, combined with the extreme danger of reporting on the war, has left a journalistic void that no amount of international effort can easily fill.

Beyond active conflict, Sudan's broader press environment has also limited coverage. In 2015, Sudan ranked 174th out of 180 countries on Reporters Without Borders' World Press Freedom Index, worse than all but six nations globally. This lack of press infrastructure means fewer international journalists have existing relationships, networks, or established reporting pipelines in Sudan compared to places with freer media landscapes.

**Sudan Beyond War: A Country Americans Don't Know**

Dinella notes that even before the 2023 war, most Americans knew little about Sudan beyond conflict. Koushkoush describes his experience discovering Khartoum in 2007: the city's natural beauty, where the Blue and White Nile rivers meet and remain visibly different colors for almost a mile before merging; the vibrant culture; the opportunity to reconnect with family. Yet coverage from Sudan almost always centered on political upheaval and war. In 2019, Sudan experienced a massive nonviolent protest movement that toppled the dictatorship of Omar al-Bashir and installed a transitional government—a major democratic breakthrough that received minimal American coverage. That hope was again shattered by a 2021 military coup. The country's positive developments—its cultural richness, freedom struggles, everyday life—rarely reached American consciousness.

**Racist Stereotypes and the Myth of "African Violence"**

Scott Strauss, who studies genocide perception and specializes in African atrocities, identifies several interlocking factors driving the attention gap. One is rooted in long-standing racist assumptions about Africans. Strauss notes: "there is a sense that Africans are more violent, inherently more violent than other people." This stereotype, though rarely stated explicitly, shapes how Americans perceive African crises. It fosters a sense of inevitability—that violence in Africa is a natural occurrence rather than a preventable atrocity worthy of urgent response.

Strauss also pushes back on the misperception that genocide happens primarily in Africa. "Other regions of the world are just as prone to mass atrocities and genocide as sub-Saharan Africa is," he argues, citing Myanmar, Russia-Ukraine, and China's treatment of Uyghurs as recent counterexamples. Yet despite similar barriers to coverage in some of these cases, they too receive less American attention than the Israel-Gaza conflict.

**Domestic Preoccupations and the Israel Connection**

Strauss identifies a second major factor: American domestic focus. The U.S. is grappling with fundamental questions—immigration policy, birthright citizenship, wars being initiated "out of nowhere," defense spending approaching $1.5 trillion. For many Americans, Israel becomes legible as a domestic issue precisely because the U.S. is materially supporting it. Israel is the largest cumulative recipient of U.S. foreign aid; most of that money goes to defense. As Michigan Senate candidate Abdul El-Sayed framed it to constituents: "would you rather us be spending our money to make sure your kid's school is a dignified place...or would you rather spend our money buying another country a tank?" For many, the Gaza crisis is not just a human rights atrocity but a direct claim on American tax dollars—a pocketbook issue in an era of economic anxiety.

Sudan, by contrast, has no comparable direct tie to U.S. policy in American perception. The RSF leader, Hamadi, received funding from Saudi Arabia and the United Arab Emirates for mercenary work in Yemen and Libya, but this geopolitical complexity lacks the narrative simplicity and domestic resonance of "U.S. gives Israel billions."

**The Politicization of "Genocide" and Gaza's Shadow**

Strauss emphasizes a third, more recent factor: the acrimonious politicization of the term "genocide" in the context of Gaza. When pro-Palestinian activists used "genocide" to describe Israeli actions, their language was sometimes met with fierce accusations that doing so was antisemitic, a blood libel, a fundamental insult to Jews. This rhetorical battle—over whether genocide even applies to Gaza—has been so contentious that it has "taken the wind out of the sails" of discussions about genocide elsewhere. For many, watching that conversation play out has become a deterrent to wading into similar discussions about other crises, given the potential repercussions. In effect, the Gaza debate has made "genocide" less a term for describing atrocities and more a political weapon used against opponents. As Dinella notes, the discourse shifted from "how do you help people who are suffering on a very large scale" to "genocide is a political weapon you use against your opponents."

Strauss also reminds listeners that the Holocaust remains the archetypal genocide in American consciousness, setting expectations for what genocide must look like. When actual genocides don't fit that template, they become harder to recognize or mobilize around.

**The Decline of Human Rights as a Mobilizing Force**

Strauss observes that human rights as an organizing principle has become de-centered globally over the past decade. Organizations and individuals have shifted focus elsewhere, and the post-Iraq, post-Libya skepticism about international intervention has hardened. The belief that the U.S. can or should militarily intervene to stop war crimes has become contested. When NATO intervention in Libya (during the Arab Spring) was widely perceived as doing more harm than good, the narrative that "we know how to fix this and it has clear solutions" lost credibility.

**Darfur and the Failure of Western Interventionism**

To understand the current moment, the episode traces back to Sudan's previous major atrocity: the Darfur genocide of the early 2000s. Under President Omar al-Bashir, the government conscripted the Janjaweed, an Arab militia group, to fight against African ethnic rebels who accused the state of oppressing non-Arab populations. Sudan is roughly 70% Arab and 30% ethnically African, and this divide was weaponized. Starting in 2003, hundreds of thousands died from direct violence or starvation and disease; two million were displaced. The Darfur campaign captured American attention as a straightforward moral issue: celebrities, politicians, and ordinary Americans rallied to the cause. In 2006, thousands gathered on the National Mall with George Clooney and Elie Wiesel, urging President Bush to intervene. Barack Obama, then a senator, proclaimed: "we know what is right and we know what is wrong. The slaughter of innocence is wrong."

Yet Alex Duval, a leading Sudanese history expert, argues that this moral clarity was misleading. The rebellion and the militias alike drew from Sudan's poorest, most marginalized segments. It was "a classic divide and rule, but it was a genocidal divide and rule." The American response, while well-intentioned, imposed a particular style of action and dictation of what was "right" and "wrong," without addressing root causes. The war eventually de-escalated, but the underlying issues festered unresolved. No political solution that brought all parties to the table was pursued; transitional justice mechanisms were not established. Democracy and genuine political reform were never seriously attempted.

**The RSF's Rise and Today's Conflict**

Fast forward to the 2020s. The young Janjaweed commander Hamadi evolved into a wealthy, ruthless operator. He rented out his militia to fight in Yemen and Libya, accumulating wealth and international connections. He gained control of Sudan's artisanal gold fields, making him arguably the country's richest person. When the government ordered his militia (renamed the Rapid Support Forces, or RSF) to integrate into the official Sudanese army, Hamadi refused. In April 2023, conflict between the RSF and the government armed forces (SAF) erupted into civil war.

Duval describes Hamadi as a "very skilled operator," both politically and militarily, deploying a combination of nomadic tribes and unemployed, marginalized wage laborers who felt deeply frustrated with the political establishment. The RSF unleashed what both experts characterize as a genocidal campaign, with the U.S. government formally recognizing it as such in January 2025. However, both sides have committed widespread atrocities: the SAF, deeply supported by Egypt, has also been accused of targeting civilians and committing war crimes.

**The Complexity of Conflict and the Inadequacy of Moral Clarity**

May Hessen, a political scientist of Sudanese descent at MIT, cautions against the false binaries that typically frame African conflicts. Many assume civil wars in Africa are primarily ethnic or regional disputes, but Sudan is more complex. The conflict is not fundamentally about ethnicity or territory but about power, resources, and geopolitical interests. Sudan's gold, its strategic location, and the involvement of regional actors (Saudi Arabia, UAE, Egypt, Russia) all shape the war in ways invisible to Americans accustomed to simpler narratives.

Crucially, Hessen insists on rejecting false equivalencies without accepting another false equivalency. "The RSF's actions are beyond the pale. They are killing people because of their ethnicity. But that doesn't necessarily mean that SAF, the Army, is this shining white knight." This refusal to pick sides does not equate the two—rather, it recognizes that neither represents a good option. A genuine path to peace requires including Sudan's civilian coalition of politicians in peace negotiations, not imposing solutions from outside or backing one belligerent over another.

**The Limits and Risks of American Attention**

Gene notes that despite massive American outcry over Gaza, Israel has continued military operations in Iran and Lebanon, with U.S. support. There has been no visible "chilling effect" on Israeli policy. This observation complicates the assumption that more public attention automatically translates to policy change or reduced violence.

Dinella reflects on a subtle American arrogance: the belief that the U.S. gets to decide when and where to intervene globally, and gets to dictate solutions. The episode's implicit invitation is for Americans to engage with the rest of the world differently—not as saviors imposing quick wins, but as supporters of slow, enduring change led by the people of Sudan themselves. Democracy is messy and requires civilians to have input, security sector reform, and other "pesky requests" that make short-term military solutions harder to implement. Yet these are precisely what build lasting peace.

**Conclusion**

The episode concludes that the question of why Sudan garners so little attention is not one easily answered by any single factor—not journalist displacement, not press freedom rankings, not racist stereotypes alone. It is the convergence of all of these, combined with America's inward focus, the politicization of "genocide" in Gaza's shadow, and a hardened skepticism toward American interventionism born of past failures. The Darfur campaign demonstrated that celebrity activism and moral clarity do not automatically translate to solutions; without addressing root causes and including local voices in determining next steps, well-intentioned external pressure can even prolong conflict by sidelining political solutions.

# Unchained — "Why Mike Dudas Has Zero Exposure to ETH, but Is Bullish on Hyperliquid"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guest:** Mike Dudas (managing partner, Sixman Ventures; crypto venture investor focusing on asset tokenization and DeFi).

## TL;DR

- **Bitcoin down 22% on the month; MicroStrategy's Bitcoin sale punctured "never sell" narrative** — Sailor and Microstrategy sold 32 Bitcoin, causing the market to lose confidence in the religious fervor that had supported the stock.
- **Dudas has zero exposure to ETH due to narrative incoherence** — Ethereum's foundation and major stakeholders tell conflicting stories about whether the asset is money, infrastructure, or a world computer, making it impossible to value across a multi-year horizon.
- **Salona is underperforming due to activity collapse, not fundamentals** — Memecoin volume peaked in early 2025 and has declined; Solana has "more shots on goal" (perpetuals, DeFi) but needs to prove execution.
- **Hyperliquid's no-KYC model will survive US onshore competition** — Dudas compares it to Tether vs. Circle; the no-KYC market is "enormous," and Binance demonstrates a purely international exchange can be "really really large."
- **97% of Hyperliquid fees go to token buybacks**—programmatic value accrual and consistent communication drive token confidence; discretionary mechanisms erode trust in crypto.
- **Real-world derivatives (crude oil, pre-IPO stocks) will be the next growth driver** — bringing high-quality, on-chain, 24/7 tradeable assets at low cost is where all boats are lifted.
- **Crypto VC is shifting from network effects to sustainable models** — lower developer costs (AI, tooling) mean founders can launch MVPs faster, but most app-layer businesses haven't survived multiple cycles; value is moving toward DeFi leaders and adjacent opportunities (AI, energy, compute).

---

**MicroStrategy's Betting Strategy and the "Never Seller" Collapse**

Dudas frames MicroStrategy's moment as a clash between two incompatible narratives: the financialization of Bitcoin (treating it as a corporate treasury asset) and the "messianic" narrative of religious belief and "never selling." Michael Sailor's posts emphasizing memes and religious fervor promised the company would always be a buyer, not a seller. When MicroStrategy sold 32 Bitcoin, the market responded "tremendously negatively" because it punctured the messianic promise that had been load-bearing for the stock. Dudas notes that the "next steps are going to be really interesting" — whether the company is forced to sell more as leverage pressures mount. The core tension: if Bitcoin price drops further, does the company buy aggressively to maintain belief, or does it face a cash flow crunch? Dudas suggests the market no longer knows what MicroStrategy's story is.

**Ethereum's Narrative Crisis and Why Sixman Has Zero Exposure**

Dudas cites David Hoffman's essay about Ethereum's failure to own its "moneyness" properties. While the asset has some money-like qualities, the foundation and ecosystem writers frame it as a "credibly neutral layer on a multi-decade horizon" while simultaneously every major stakeholder tells a different story about the asset's long-term mission. One group wants it to be money, another wants to "embrace institutions," and others envision a "utopian world computer." The market has been unable to value that incoherence for five years. Sixman owns zero ETH because Dudas cannot articulate a single story for what it is today or what it will be in three years. He doesn't "know who's going to win this wrestling match." Compare this to Bitcoin and Solana, which each have clearer narratives: Bitcoin as digital money, Solana as a high-throughput blockchain for builders.

**Solana's Performance Decline and the Path Forward**

Unlike Ethereum, Solana's problem is cleaner: activity-driven. Solana's foundation and key stakeholders honed in on "a true north in real economic value" — fees that accrue to token holders and stakers. However, activity (and therefore fees) peaked in early 2025 and have declined because the growth was driven by memecoin speculation and retail flow, not durable economic activity. Dudas believes there's "a good chance" Solana will see renewed activity through perpetuals and other narratives, but the proof is missing today. Solana has "more shots on goal" than other L1s and is "going after the areas they've historically been weak" (perpetuals), but teams building on Solana need to demonstrate execution and the L1 needs to reach "close to centralized exchange levels of performance." If that materializes, Solana could be undervalued, but market-proof is needed first.

**Hyperliquid's Competitive Moat in the No-KYC Market**

When asked whether Hyperliquid can remain "no KYC and still be as dominant" as KYC exchanges like Kraken and Coinbase come onshore, Dudas answers decisively: the no-KYC market is "enormous." He uses Binance as a proof point — the world's largest exchange by far has never successfully built a US business of scale, yet remains dominant globally by serving the international and no-KYC market. Dudas sees the Hyperliquid vs. on-shore derivatives dynamic as "Tether versus Circle" — separate markets with different characteristics. Both can be very large. The real question for Hyperliquid is not whether it survives, but whether it can add "higher and higher quality assets" and attract builders to create interfaces around it (reducing the marginal cost to acquire users). Hyperliquid has been adding oil, pre-IPO stocks, and is moving into prediction markets (adding HIP for outcome markets).

**Value Accrual Mechanisms and the Programmatic vs. Discretionary Divide**

Dudas emphasizes two critical mechanisms for token confidence: (1) **programmatic value accrual** — Hyperliquid returns 97% of fees to buyback and burn the token, not subject to management whim; and (2) **consistent storytelling** — leadership must tell the same story over time about product, mission, and investor value. Crypto's "default is not to trust" builders because fraud and grift are more common than in traditional business. Tokens with discretionary mechanisms get valued less than those with hard rules. That said, pure buyback (100%) can signal underinvestment in growth; Pump.fun, a portfolio company, moderated its buybacks to 50% of revenue, programmatically, to credibly show reinvestment in product. The lesson: discretionary decisions erode trust; programmatic rules build it. Tokens like Cardano, which depend entirely on pre-mined foundation allocations, face "weird incentive" problems because the foundation must sell on secondary at discounts to fund operations — the market sees this as unstable.

**Real-World Derivatives as the Next Growth Frontier**

The ability to trade crude oil futures on Hyperliquid based on real-world events (Iran war news over a weekend), and now pre-IPO stocks, represents what Dudas calls the "glimmers of something that's probably going to be way bigger." The value proposition is straightforward: bring high-quality assets on-chain, available 24/7, 365 days, at low cost, with no KYC friction. The fact that traders are comfortable executing from self-custody wallets shows product-market fit. Every ecosystem (Solana Foundation, Hyperliquid, trade.xyz) is focused on "getting new assets issued and spun up with robust oracles and pricing mechanisms." The broader point: "the more high quality assets we bring on chain, the more all boats are lifted." If institutional-grade venues materialize for real-world derivatives, this becomes "really really really big."

**AI Agents, Trading, and Crypto's Value Capture**

When Laura brings up agents as the future of transacting in crypto, Dudas separates the opportunity into two layers: the exchange/venue layer and the application/interface layer. At the exchange layer (L1s like Solana or Hyperliquid), agent trading would drive massive fee volume — agents trade more frequently than humans. However, the DEX layer is "unclear" whether it can differentiate on value capture; frontends, by contrast, have shown ability to capture 1% per trade in the memecoin era. At the app layer, Dudas sees opportunity in "AI research labs working on financial markets" — services that help users construct complex, performative trading strategies that humans can't easily execute. These services can charge fees based on outperformance. Dudas is less bullish on agentic payments because "incumbents, the Visas, the Mastercards, the Stripes, are moving really really really rapidly" and already have compliance, fraud detection, and customer bases that will be hard to dislodge.

**Crypto VC's Shifting Landscape: Lower Costs, Abundant Talent, AI Winds**

Dudas notes that the cost to start a crypto company has dropped dramatically — founders can hire fewer people, use AI tooling (Claude, Copilot) to spin up MVPs, and even non-technical people can build. This shrinks or increases the pre-seed category (harder to tell yet) and makes signal vs. noise harder. VCs now demand traction and proof of emergent economic activity much earlier. The bigger shift: crypto VC and crypto founders are increasingly moving into adjacent industries — AI, energy, compute — while keeping their crypto domain expertise. Examples include Brian Armstrong's longevity venture (raising hundreds of millions) and Paradigm's recent deals in energy and compute. Dudas sees this as natural: the brightest minds, having profited from early crypto, have capital and foresight to expand their aperture. The reason is also structural: most venture capital is flowing to "AI first" companies, leaving everything else (including pure crypto) "somewhat capital starved" and "talent starved." Crypto VC is adapting by becoming broader-spectrum investors with crypto roots.

**The Multi-Cycle Test for App-Layer Sustainability**

Finally, Dudas stresses that the crypto app layer has now lived through roughly three cycles, yet very few businesses have survived multiple cycles. Most of the value has accrued to DeFi primitives (DEXes, lending), not to later-stage apps. This informs Sixman's investment thesis: look for things that "look a lot like things that existed in the world before, but done better because of blockchain rails." Always-on, cheaper liquidity formation, no KYC (where applicable), and accessible-to-all are the properties that generate durable value. The implication: don't expect the next Uniswap or Aave if you're chasing the next memecoin or DeFi derivative. Crypto VC has matured from "this is new" to "this is proven on-chain but has legs in the real world."

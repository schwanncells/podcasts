# Unchained — "Why MSTR Should Have Sold $2 Billion Instead of $2 Million of Bitcoin"

**Host:** Laura Shin. **Guest:** Jeff Dorman (Chief Investment Officer, Arca; former capital markets and investment banking specialist; structured-products analyst following MicroStrategy's capital dynamics).

## TL;DR

- **MicroStrategy's capital structure has inverted** — Saylor transformed the company from a simple equity+debt model to a four-layer stack (equity, $6.7B debt, $15B preferred shares, and $57B Bitcoin), with $1.7B annual preferred dividends that the company cannot service from operations.
- **The company has four months of cash left** — MicroStrategy raised $2B to cover preferred dividends, then immediately spent $1.4B paying down convertible debt due in 2029, reducing its dividend-payment runway from 18 months to 4–5 months.
- **Three "bad" options and one "nuclear" option** — Sell Bitcoin (spooked the market, killed sentiment), sell more equity (dilutes stock at non-accretive MNAV of 1.23), issue more debt (hurts S&P 500 inclusion goal), or stop paying preferred dividends (death sentence for preferreds, likely lawsuits, seals off capital markets forever).
- **The $2.5M Bitcoin sale was a strategic blunder** — Dorman argues Saylor should have ripped the bandaid off and sold $2B immediately to prove commitment to preferred dividend sustainability and avoid teasing the market with tiny sales that destroy confidence.
- **Bitcoin sentiment dominates the outcome** — Even a $2.5M sale (0.04% of Bitcoin holdings) crashed Bitcoin 5%; a $2B sale at current momentum would face cascading sell-off, making the strategy less viable the longer it drags.
- **All stakeholders cannot win simultaneously** — Bitcoin holders lose if Saylor sells, preferred shareholders lose if he doesn't pay dividends, equity holders lose if he dilutes stock, and debt holders are protected by $57B Bitcoin backing, but confidence erosion affects all layers.
- **Polymarket MSTR resolution fiasco** — Market resolved "no" despite confirmed June 1 filing showing May sales because validators required disclosure by May 31; hundreds of millions in trades occurred after May 31 on indisputable evidence, then resolved against evidence, highlighting endemic DAO governance issues.

**The Problem: Four Stakeholders, One Unsustainable Puzzle**

Dorman explains that until November 2025, MicroStrategy's capital structure posed no existential threat to Bitcoin. The company had modest debt, zero coupon convertible bonds that required no cash outlay, and could muddle along indefinitely without selling Bitcoin. Everything changed when Saylor introduced preferred shares with 10–12% dividend rates. With roughly four different preferred issues totaling $15B, MicroStrategy now owes $1.7B in annual dividends in cash—an obligation a company that makes no money cannot meet from operations.

This created what Dorman calls "the puzzle"—four independent stakeholder classes (Bitcoin holders, MSTR equity holders, preferred shareholders, and debt holders) all bullish in theory but unable to be satisfied simultaneously. Each lever affects all parts: raising more equity dilutes the stock, selling Bitcoin spooks the market, issuing more debt damages credit ratings, and cutting the preferred dividend destroys the preferred trading value and invites litigation.

**The $2B Cash Raise and Its Immediate Reversal**

In May, MicroStrategy raised $2B via preferred and equity issuances with a stated purpose: secure 18 months of dividend payments and calm the preferred market (STRC, the $11B preferred, had traded below par because investors feared the company lacked cash reserves). Dorman viewed this as prudent. Within a month, Saylor spent $1.4B of that cushion buying back convertible debt maturing in 2029 at 92 cents on the dollar—debt that would not come due for three more years.

This move directly contradicted the cash-raise narrative. Dorman characterizes it as "completely the opposite of what he said the cash was for" and calls it "balance sheet mismanagement." The preferred market panicked again, and the 18-month runway collapsed to four to five months. By early June, MicroStrategy announced it had sold 32 Bitcoin ($2.5M) to pay preferred dividends—telegraphing to the market that the only option left is to sell Bitcoin.

**Four Paths, All Painful**

Dorman walks through the outcomes for each strategy. If Saylor continues to sell Bitcoin, the "MicroStrategy turns Bitcoin seller" headlines (already appearing in Zero Hedge, Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg) will erode Bitcoin sentiment and potentially trigger a spiral—as the largest Bitcoin holder, any sustained selling signals weakness to the broader market.

If he sells more equity, he can still access the capital markets (MNAV is 1.23, meaning stock is still trading above net asset value), but dilution at non-accretive levels crushes the stock. If he issues more debt, it worsens credit ratings and kills S&P 500 inclusion—a stated goal for the preferred shareholders. If he stops paying preferred dividends, they collapse 30–40%, he faces lawsuits from retail investors who bought on the "safest 12% yield" pitch, and capital markets permanently close to him.

None of these paths are good. Dorman notes: "You're just in a weird situation now where this flywheel that he's created over six years is just slowly dying, and he's put too many pieces in the puzzle that can't be serviced at the same time."

**Why the $2.5M Sale Was the Wrong Message**

Dorman articulates the core mistake: a nominal sale that signals intention without fully committing. He argues from an investment banking perspective: if you must signal to the market that you will sell Bitcoin to maintain the dividend, rip the bandaid off and sell $2B immediately. This proves you're serious, eliminates the "will he, won't he" uncertainty, and allows a press release saying, "We have committed to this path; here's the proof; now we can plan forward."

Instead, the tiny sale triggered exactly the fear it was meant to assuage. Bitcoin fell 5% on a $2.5M sale. When the $2B sale happens (if it does), the reaction will be far worse—the market will have lost confidence that there's a plan at all.

Dorman speculates that Saylor's team may have hoped to "get the headlines out of the way" with a nominal amount so that future, larger sales would be met with less shock. But the outcome proves the opposite: the teasing destroyed confidence far more than a decisive action would have.

**Game Theory and the Confidence Cycle**

Dorman explains the underlying dynamic: this is a confidence game in which Saylor can theoretically be a net Bitcoin buyer—buying more than he sells—if he can convince the market his equity is worth more. Equity markets would run higher, he'd issue more preferreds at attractive prices, and the flow would fund more Bitcoin purchases while paying dividends on a tiny fraction.

But that cycle runs on confidence. Three decisions in a row—loading preferreds with heavy cash obligations, paying down zero-coupon debt for no reason, and teasing a Bitcoin sale—have all damaged confidence in the same window. The market is now pricing in that Saylor has lost control of the capital structure.

**Stakeholder Waterfall and Bankruptcy Scenarios**

In an actual default (which Dorman considers not imminent but theoretically possible), the waterfall is clear: debt holders are fully covered (7B debt backed by 57B Bitcoin), so debt trades cheaply only because the convertible feature is far out of the money and the coupon is zero. Preferred shareholders are next in line (15B preferred behind 7B debt, both backed by 57B Bitcoin), so they would eventually recover principal if the company liquidated, but they would suffer 30–40% losses if dividends are cut before that point.

Equity and Bitcoin holders are the residual claimants. Dorman notes he sees "no scenario where you'd really want to own the MSTR stock right now," but he cavalierly adds that "Saylor has pulled a rabbit out of the hat multiple times in his career." The problem is that prior rabbits worked when the capital structure was simple; with four stakeholder classes, a magic solution is harder to imagine.

**Bull Case Scenario**

Dorman identifies two paths where the story survives. First, Bitcoin must go much higher—if Bitcoin appreciates 10–15%, all asset values rise, equities trade up, Saylor can issue more preferreds or equity at better prices, and the $1.7B dividend becomes a smaller percentage of Bitcoin's growth. At 2.5% annual Bitcoin appreciation, he makes enough capital gains to cover the dividend from selling just that growth.

Second, Saylor could declare a maximum Bitcoin target (e.g., 5% of all Bitcoin), stop raising new preferreds once he hits it, and then simply stop paying preferred dividends. He'd never access capital markets again, but he wouldn't need to because his accumulation target would be met. Preferred holders would lose dividend income and the stock would fall, but Bitcoin would be preserved.

**The Polymarket Dispute and Market Integrity**

Dorman and Shin also discuss the Polymarket "Will MSTR sell Bitcoin in May?" contract, which MicroStrategy's June 1 SEC filing confirmed: Bitcoin sold May 26–31. Yet UMA validators ruled the market "no" because the announcement came June 1, not by May 31. 

Dorman calls this "one of the craziest things I've ever seen" and notes that the contract code actually included a July 1 deadline, not May 31. Beyond the technical error, hundreds of millions of dollars in trades occurred after May 31 when the sale was already confirmed by SEC filing, yet traders lost money because validators voted to ignore public evidence.

He argues that if the market wanted a strict May 31 cutoff, it should have halted trading on May 31 and required explicit resolution language. Leaving it open-ended while enforcing an invisible deadline—and allowing massive volume to trade after the outcome was provably known—is inconsistent with prediction market integrity. Dorman suspects insider activity, noting the validators may have deliberately kept terms ambiguous to allow friends to profit on insider knowledge.

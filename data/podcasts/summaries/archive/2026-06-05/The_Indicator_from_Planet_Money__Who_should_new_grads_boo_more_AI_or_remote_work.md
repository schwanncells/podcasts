# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Who should new grads boo more? AI or remote work?"

**Host:** Adrian Ma. **Guests:** Darian Woods, Waylon Wong (fellow hosts).

**The May Jobs Report and Labor Market Momentum**

Adrian Ma reported that May showed three straight months of strong hiring. The 172,000 jobs added, combined with revised upward estimates for March and April totaling 93,000 additional jobs, suggests the labor market is shaking off the sluggish hiring patterns seen earlier in the year. Most jobs came in leisure and hospitality, local government, and health care. The average hourly wage of $37.53 represented a 3.4% gain year-over-year, though this failed to match inflation running at 3.8%. This wage-inflation gap, combined with overall job strength, makes it unlikely the Federal Reserve will cut interest rates soon despite President Trump's pressure to do so. In fact, some economists see room for a rate hike, given that inflation remains the Fed's primary concern and loosening monetary policy would only add to price pressure.

**AI, Remote Work, and the Young Worker Challenge**

Young people graduating from college and trade school have been expressing frustration with the job market, with viral commencement speech clips showing them booing speakers, including former Google CEO Eric Schmidt, when discussing artificial intelligence. However, two new research studies cast doubt on AI as the main culprit. A UK study examining hundreds of millions of job postings and hires from 2017 to 2035 found weak evidence that AI was killing junior jobs, even in the tech sector. A second study from three US economists, released this week, concluded that nearly two-thirds of the recent rise in unemployment among young college graduates stems from remote work adoption. The mechanism appears to be that companies view remote onboarding as harder than in-person training, leading hiring managers to favor candidates with existing workplace experience and soft skills—proactivity, communication, how to function in a team. One host noted the potential long-term payoff for employers willing to invest in training Gen Z workers despite this friction, and joked that AI training systems might yet prove to be the solution.

**Black Unemployment as a Leading Indicator**

Waylon Wong highlighted the black unemployment rate as a potentially important signal for the broader labor market. In May, the rate stood at 6.6%—significantly higher than the 4.3% overall rate but an improvement from April and the lowest in a year. Black workers were disproportionately hit during the federal workforce mass layoffs and buyouts in 2025, with analysis from the Economic Policy Institute showing Black women suffered the greatest job losses that year. Research by Cleveland Fed economist Kevin Rins provides a reason to watch this metric closely: his analysis shows that when Black unemployment rises by one percentage point, overall unemployment tends to follow with a 0.2-point increase roughly four to seven months later, making Black joblessness a reliable leading indicator of broader labor market weakness.

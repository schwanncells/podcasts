# Throughline — "The Origins of the Socialist Party of America"

**Host:** Randa Abdel-Fattah. **Guests:** Allison Dirk (director, Eugene V. Debs Museum, Terre Haute, Indiana); Nick Salvatore (historian, author of *Eugene V. Debs: Citizen and Socialist*); Ernest Freeberg (history professor, University of Tennessee at Knoxville, author of *Democracy's Prisoner: Eugene Debs, The Great War and the Right to Dissent*).

## TL;DR

- **Eugene Debs founded the Socialist Party of America in 1901** — uniting existing socialist factions under one banner after becoming convinced that both Republicans and Democrats were fundamentally pro-capitalist parties that could not represent workers.
- **A friend was crushed to death by a locomotive in 1874, prompting Debs to leave railroad work** — he moved into labor organizing after witnessing systematic industrial abuses and unsafe conditions that were considered normal parts of railroad work.
- **Debs founded the American Railway Union as an inclusive alternative to the AFL** — Samuel Gompers' American Federation of Labor admitted workers only by specific skills and excluded women and people of color. Debs' ARU sought to organize all rail workers together, regardless of position or demographics.
- **The Pullman strike of 1894 involved approximately 250,000 workers** — Pullman Palace Car Company slashed wages during an economic downturn but refused to lower rent or grocery costs in its company town, forcing workers to strike. Grover Cleveland sent federal troops, approximately 30 workers were killed, the strike failed, and thousands were blacklisted.
- **Debs was jailed for six months for contempt of court** — he emerged convinced that the political system was broken and that workers needed their own political party.
- **In 1912, Debs won 6% of the popular vote in a presidential election** — a staggering proportion for a third-party candidate at that time.
- **Socialist policy innovations later became mainstream** — the 40-hour work week, old age pensions, worker safety protections, welfare programs, and support for early childhood education were all radical third-party ideas when socialists first proposed them, later adopted by progressives and implemented via the New Deal and Great Society programs.
- **Debs believed workers should see themselves as workers, not as "temporarily embarrassed millionaires"** — his vision centered on class consciousness and solidarity across all workers, rather than skill-based or craft union divisions.

**Industrial Crisis and Debs' Early Radicalization**

In the mid-1800s, rapid industrialization transformed American cities, but with brutal consequences for workers. Businesses grew larger while workers lost bargaining power and democratic voice over their labor. Fourteen-to-sixteen-hour workdays, six or seven days a week, were standard. In Indiana, a teenage Eugene Debs watched his father's health destroyed by such work, spurring him to action. At 14, Debs entered the railroad business during an expansion marked by rampant abuses. Safety standards were nearly nonexistent; employers used cheap equipment to save money, causing frequent boiler explosions, derailments, and collisions. In 1874, while working on the railroad tracks, Debs witnessed a locomotive crush a friend to death—an event that, combined with his mother's safety concerns, prompted him to leave railroad work.

Rather than retreat from labor issues, Debs pursued change through politics. He ran for city clerk of Terre Haute and won easily, serving two terms. During his tenure, he refused to assess fines against sex workers, judging it unfair that they were punished while their clients were not. He then won election to the Indiana General Assembly, where he introduced legislation to hold railroads liable for worker deaths and injuries. The state House passed the bill, but the state Senate gutted it with amendments that made enforcement impossible. Humiliated and convinced the statehouse was "bought up by the railroads," Debs abandoned electoral politics to focus on labor organizing.

**Inclusive Unionism and the American Railway Union**

At this point, Debs still believed labor and capitalism could coexist; he was not yet a radical but rather a small-D Democrat who hoped to fix the system from within. As he became more involved in labor organizing, he grew disillusioned. The existing labor landscape was fragmented. The American Federation of Labor, led by Samuel Gompers, was the largest organization but highly selective—admitting workers only by specific skills and excluding women and people of color. Debs believed this strategy was self-defeating and that strength lay in inclusive unity. He helped found the American Railway Union, designed to organize workers across all positions within the railroad industry, allowing them to cooperate and act collectively, making it much harder for railroad owners to ignore them.

Debs pushed to include people of color in the ARU. He brought the issue to a vote at one of their first conventions, and the proposal nearly passed, losing by just two votes. This narrow defeat was consequential—observers note it could have altered the course of history. Despite the setback, Debs remained committed to the fight for inclusive labor organization.

**The Pullman Strike and Federal Violence**

In 1894, Debs faced his defining moment. The Pullman Palace Car Company had transformed rail travel by building luxury cars, and many workers dreamed of working there. The company town offered better-than-average living standards, but at a hidden cost: workers rented homes from Pullman, bought groceries from company stores, sent children to company schools, and attended a company church. George Pullman framed this as an experiment in benevolent capitalism, but workers had no voice in company decisions without risking termination. When economic downturn struck, Pullman slashed wages while keeping rent and living costs unchanged. Workers reported fainting on the job.

In May 1894, 3,000 Pullman workers laid down their tools, demanding wage reinstatement and reduced rent so they could feed their families. Pullman refused to negotiate, saying there was nothing to arbitrate. With Debs' support, the ARU called for a massive nationwide strike of railroad workers: "Every railroad employee of the country should take his stand against the corporations in this fight. For if it should be lost, corporations will have despotic sway and all employees will be reduced to a condition scarcely removed above chattel slavery." The New York Times reported that this would be "the most tremendous strike known to history," with 10,000 men abandoning work the following day.

The strike effectively shut down railroads from Chicago west for days, paralyzing the American economy. To put the scale in perspective, historians note it would be equivalent to modern unions shutting down half of American airports and interstates. Debs urged strikers to remain calm and united, expressing confidence: "I am perfectly confident of success. We cannot fail." But success was not to be. President Grover Cleveland, whom Debs had previously campaigned for as a pro-labor Democrat, sent federal troops to remove strikers so scab workers could operate the engines. The conflict descended into chaos. Approximately 30 workers were killed, mostly in Chicago. Hundreds were arrested in skirmishes across the nation. Nearly 250,000 workers had participated in the strike; afterward, many were left jobless and thousands were blacklisted. The American Railway Union never recovered. The whole ARU leadership was jailed on contempt of court charges; Debs served six months in prison.

**From Prison to the Socialist Party**

Sitting in his cell, Debs reached a critical realization: the American political system was broken. As he would later state, "The Republicans are the big capitalists and the Democrats are the small capitalists. But at the end of the day, they are both the pro-capitalist parties." He concluded that workers needed their own political party.

Upon his release from prison, he was greeted by 100,000 people. Standing before the crowd, he delivered a rousing speech that grounded his activism in American tradition: "The fires of liberty and noble aspirations are not yet extinguished. I greet you tonight as lovers of liberty and as despisers of despotism. What is more American to Debs than standing up against oppression and standing up against injustice? This is the American tradition." He called on "agitation, organization, and unification" as the battle cries of those who would not surrender their birthrights.

In 1901, drawing on his newfound fame from the Pullman strike and his Liberty speech, Debs founded the Socialist Party of America, uniting all existing socialist factions under a single banner. His vision centered on raising class consciousness among workers—making them understand themselves not as temporarily embarrassed millionaires or as members of disparate craft unions, but as workers with more in common than differences.

**Electoral Influence and Legacy**

Initially, Debs joked that he would make a poor president and resisted the idea of candidacy. But he eventually recognized that a presidential platform could amplify the socialist message. Over the next 20 years, Debs and the Socialist Party ran multiple presidential campaigns. In 1912, Debs achieved his closest approach to the presidency, winning 6% of the popular vote—a staggering figure for a third-party candidate at that time. Woodrow Wilson won the election, but the socialists' influence on American policy proved durable and profound.

Many of the ideas Debs and American socialists fought for were eventually adopted by mainstream politicians. The 40-hour work week, old age pensions, worker safety protections, welfare programs for the poor, and support for early childhood education were all radical third-party innovations when first proposed by socialists. These ideas were later absorbed by progressives in the early 20th century, then many were implemented via the New Deal in the 1930s and the Great Society programs in the 1960s. The long legacy of the Socialist Party demonstrates that even as the party itself faded from prominence, its core platform shaped the development of the American social safety net, government oversight of health and welfare, and financial regulation—all originally socialist ideas that became foundational to modern American liberalism.

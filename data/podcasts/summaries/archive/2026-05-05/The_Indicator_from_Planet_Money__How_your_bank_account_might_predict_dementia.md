# The Indicator from Planet Money — "How your bank account might predict dementia"

**Host:** Waylon Wong, Adrian Ma. **Guest:** Lauren Nicholas (health economist and professor of geriatrics, University of Colorado; researcher on dementia and financial decline).

## TL;DR

- **Financial decline begins 6 years before dementia diagnosis** — researchers analyzed nearly two decades of national survey data on older adults and found household wealth drops measurably up to six years before clinical diagnosis or recognized symptoms appear.
- **The decline is unique to dementia, not other diseases** — people with cancer or arthritis do not see similar wealth deterioration, ruling out factors like lost work income or increased medical costs; the decline stems specifically from impaired financial decision-making.
- **Early personality shifts fuel bad decisions** — people in early cognitive decline often become overconfident even as they lose financial management abilities, making them more trusting and risk-prone while believing they're "crushing it" financially.
- **Higher initial cognition can mask decline longer** — highly educated individuals can use residual intelligence to hide financial trouble for years; one case involved a man with a medical degree who didn't pay income taxes for four years before his decline was discovered.
- **Financial services lack legal tools to intervene** — brokerages and banks detect suspicious activity but lack authority and privacy clearance to alert doctors; financial advisors report discomfort raising dementia concerns without proper expertise or confidence they're correct.
- **Potential solution: credit data + doctor screening** — researchers are developing models to extract dementia signals from credit reports, potentially allowing physicians to offer supplemental financial screening alongside standard cognitive tests.

**A Daughter's Discovery**

Sanda Balaban describes returning to her father's Baltimore apartment after he and her stepmother separated. His office presented the first visible signs of cognitive collapse: piles of papers everywhere, credit card statements revealing thousands of dollars monthly spent on scammy health products and dubious online subscriptions. When confronted, her father claimed no knowledge of the charges. Over the following years, Sanda learned the full scope: her father had lost nearly all his retirement savings—between $1 and $2 million in his brokerage account—through erratic and inexplicable investment decisions. She obtained ten years of financial statements; the pattern was chaotic and senseless. Her father had not filed income taxes since 2014, four years before she discovered the credit card abuse.

**When Wealth Decline Signals Hidden Cognitive Loss**

Lauren Nicholas notes that dementia is unique among diseases because it directly degrades the cognitive abilities required to manage money. Many people with dementia do things like compulsively shop, move entire portfolios into a single stock, or stop paying bills. Importantly, these financial warning signs often appear in checkbooks and investment accounts years before standard clinical tests—like drawing an analog clock or memorizing three words—would catch the disease. Nicholas and colleagues analyzed nearly two decades of national survey data tracking health and retirement for older adults and their spouses. They found that household wealth begins declining approximately six years before a dementia diagnosis or recognizable symptoms emerge. Nicholas describes the finding as both surprising and troubling: "It's like, how do you protect yourself from future you who might make these mistakes and be totally unaware of it?"

**Why Wealth Declines: The Role of Impaired Decision-Making**

The researchers investigated three potential explanations: whether wealth loss comes from lost income (stopping work), intentional spending down in retirement, or expensive medical treatment. None proved decisive. Instead, the data pointed to impaired financial decision-making as the primary culprit, and critically, this pattern is unique to dementia. People with cancer or arthritis do not exhibit the same household wealth decline, suggesting the mechanism is cognitive rather than economic. Nicholas explains that personality changes in early cognitive decline include increased propensity to trust others and shifts in risk tolerance. The cruelty lies in the paradox: as people lose financial acumen and begin forgetting bill payments, they simultaneously grow more confident in their abilities.

**Masking and High Baseline Cognition**

Sanda Balaban's father had a medical degree and a master's in public health, with training as an epidemiologist and family practice physician. His high initial cognition allowed him to mask cognitive decline for years. A neurologist later explained to Sanda that "the higher your cognition, the more you can cover," and her father's residual intelligence provided cover for his deteriorating judgment. His failure to file taxes for four years went undetected partly because he appeared competent in other domains. Sanda reflects that while her father prioritized preventive medical care, there was no parallel financial preventive care to catch his mounting problems.

**The Policy and Liability Gap**

Financial advisors, brokerages, and banks are often the first to encounter signs of dementia-related financial mismanagement. Yet they operate under significant constraints. Brokerages are obligated to execute client requests and lack authority to diagnose cognitive conditions. Privacy regulations prevent financial advisors from calling a client's doctor to flag concerns, and advisors report discomfort raising the issue without professional expertise or confidence they're right. A 2009 Fidelity survey found that most financial advisors had encountered clients with Alzheimer's disease but many felt unprepared to address it. Banks and brokerages maintain anti-money laundering and fraud detection systems, but repurposing these to predict dementia raises ethical and jurisdictional questions—early dementia screening isn't their business, legally or practically.

**Emerging Solutions: Credit Data and Doctor Access**

Nicholas proposes a bridge: giving physicians access to personal financial data, such as credit report signals, to screen for dementia alongside traditional cognitive tests. She and colleagues are developing models to extract dementia signals from credit reports with sufficient precision to warrant a physician-ordered financial screening. The approach remains years away on the research horizon but appears promising as a way to identify at-risk older adults before major financial damage occurs. For Sanda, the solution came too late for her father; she was able to enroll him in Medicaid and secure memory care placement, where he spent his final years. She has since learned that another family member received an Alzheimer's diagnosis, and this time Sanda and her brother moved faster—establishing power of attorney and ensuring themselves access to financial accounts as preventive safeguards against a repeat of her father's experience.

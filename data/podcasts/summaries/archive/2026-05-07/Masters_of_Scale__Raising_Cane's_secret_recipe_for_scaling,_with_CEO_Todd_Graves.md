# Masters of Scale — "Raising Cane's secret recipe for scaling, with CEO Todd Graves"

**Host:** Jeff Berman. **Guest:** Todd Graves (founder and co-CEO, Raising Cane's Chicken Fingers; built the brand from a single LSU-area location to nearly 1,000 restaurants globally while maintaining private ownership and company-operated model).

## TL;DR

- **Started with a single-item menu in 1996 based on conviction** — Todd founded Raising Cane's on the insight that fried boneless chicken would become the dominant protein trend, then rejected industry pressure to add menu variety despite getting an F on his college business plan for the exact same reasoning.
- **Worked 90-hour weeks as a boiler maker, then commercial salmon fishing in Alaska** — saved $40–60K over two years, gathered small equity investors who saw his commitment, secured an SBA loan, and opened the first location near LSU campus without franchising capital.
- **Made money from day one and expanded deliberately** — second location 18 months later proved the concept worked beyond college students; at that inflection point, Todd caught "the bug" to scale nationally and internationally.
- **Rejected franchising in favor of 100% company-operated restaurants** — after starting with franchisees rated ~85 out of 100 (vs. his standard of 95), Todd bought back franchises because even small differences in execution drove him crazy and slowed innovation rollout.
- **Nearly 1,000 restaurants now, approaching $10B valuation, all private** — Todd maintains founder control and guides every major decision through the lens of crew care, community involvement, and product quality; views private equity ownership as fundamentally misaligned with long-term crew wellbeing.
- **Celebrity marketing is core strategy when genuine** — works only with real "Caniacs" (Snoop Dogg, Saquon Barkley) to tap their fanbases in real-time moments (post-Super Bowl, Oscar wins); fake partnerships damage credibility in 2 seconds.
- **Co-CEO model with AJ (10 years together)** — brought in operations/supply chain/finance expert to supplement Todd's operations/marketing strengths; explicitly structured to eventually transition to full CEO role for AJ while Todd becomes chairman with global oversight.
- **Menu stays obsessively simple** — refuses sauces, spicy variants, or regional customization (even in Middle East) because every added choice slows the line, complicates training, and dilutes focus on doing one thing at world-class level.

**From Childhood Dream to Commercial Fishing**

Todd's entrepreneurial streak began young—lemonade stands, lawn care for $5. He worked in restaurants during high school and college, drawn to the immediate gratification of cooking and serving. His mother's Cajun gumbo and his grandmother's pie shaped his view of food as an expression of love. In college (1994), Todd observed two trends: beef was considered unhealthy, chicken was becoming the dominant protein, and boneless chicken was beginning to replace bone-in fried chicken at chains and mom-and-pops. He intuited this was "the next thing" and committed to opening a chicken-focused restaurant.

Todd and a partner wrote a business plan for a college class. The professor gave it an F—or rather, the worst grade in the class—for a counterintuitive reason: the plan was exceptionally detailed (Todd knew apron laundry costs), but it didn't understand industry trends. The professor noted that McDonald's and other chains were adding menu variety to avoid "veto votes" where one family member's preference wouldn't drive the group elsewhere. Todd internalized the critique not as a reason to add variety, but as confirmation that focus matters: restaurants succeed on *craveable food*, not range. "If you execute on one thing and do it better than anybody else, that's a recipe for success." He never forgot this.

With no bank willing to finance a 22-year-old's restaurant, Todd worked 90-hour weeks as a boiler maker at refineries in El Segundo and Torrance, California, earning double-time pay. After two years, he had saved $40–60K. A coworker nicknamed "Wild Bill" Tolar suggested Alaska commercial salmon fishing—harder, more dangerous, but better money. Todd accepted. He flew to Anchorage, took a float plane to King Salmon, hitchhiked to Naknek, and spent a summer catching sockeye salmon with gill nets. The work was brutal: four hours of sleep per night, 20-hour days, boats filling with fish, captains refusing to break station for fear of sinking, helicopters overhead for medical evacuations. Todd fueled himself by repeating his mantra: "I'm opening a restaurant. I'm going to do it. I'll never give up." He describes this psychological commitment as the engine—the subconscious believes what you tell it, and the brain provides the fuel to act.

That Alaskan summer confirmed his savings at $40–60K. He returned home with enough for a down payment, had attracted a small group of investors (boilermakers, fishing friends, his bookie—each pitching $5–10K in cash) who'd witnessed two years of commitment, and secured a small SBA loan. He leased an old, dilapidated building in the North Gates of LSU from a 96-year-old landlord named Tallulah Arbor. The broker, Red Reynolds, had convinced her that Todd would build a sustainable business, not another year-in-year-out college concept. Todd renovated the space himself, learning plumbing and contracting, bringing in electricians for work he couldn't do. He applied the same learning-by-doing to hiring and crew culture.

**Building a Culture of Crew Care**

Todd observed during his restaurant jobs that the industry was typically "militant"—"do this, do that, no music, no fun, yes chef." He knew exactly what he didn't want. Raising Cane's would feature casual uniforms (Cane's shirt, jeans, shorts), music in the kitchens, and fun, while crew took pride in their work. He consulted the National Restaurant Association for templates on scheduling, reached out to Baton Rouge restaurateurs he respected for crew motivation strategies, and learned from his then-girlfriend (now wife), a McDonald's franchisee, about crew incentives and fry salting.

The first location made money from day one. By 18 months, Todd opened a second location on the opposite side of campus with a full SBA loan and ground-up construction. The second location's traffic wasn't just students—it included parents, T-ball teams, church groups, business people at lunch. Todd realized "this isn't just a college concept" and got the bug to grow nationally. His vision crystallized: "as many Raising Cane's as I can do in the world."

**Franchising Experiment and Pivot to 100% Company-Operated**

When Todd began scaling beyond Baton Rouge, he started with franchisees. His target model was 60% company-operated, 40% franchised, working with strong restaurant operators who shared his intrinsic values around crew, people, and community. The franchisees cared deeply, but they didn't operate at his standard. Company restaurants hit 95 out of 100; franchisees hit ~85—a gap Todd found intolerable. "That 5–10% difference drove me crazy." The efficiency friction was also acute: Todd might introduce a faster training program or operational improvement, but franchisees would resist, saying "we like to do it this way." Todd spent more time convincing than moving forward on proven innovations. He reframed it: Raising Cane's is "an expression of my family. We want to do a good job." He bought back the franchises.

Today, Raising Cane's operates nearly 1,000 restaurants as 100% company-owned. This structure allows Todd to roll out innovations globally, maintain consistent crew experience, and stay true to his purpose. He describes his brand vision: "craveable chicken finger meals, great crew, cool culture, active community involvement" — timeless fundamentals. Treat crew right, power people who assimilate a culture of appreciation, and execute on vision. If you can't execute on that, the vision itself is broken.

**Why Private Equity Is Misaligned**

Todd is vocal about steering founders away from private equity sales. Not all PE firms are "bad," but the structure creates misalignment. If PE needs to exit in three to five years with major returns, and new operators take over when PE leaves, crew care becomes secondary. Todd cites "death by a thousand cuts"—CFOs in a boardroom propose cutting 15 cost items to boost profitability: swap premium music systems for basic ones, make sauce at a cheap commissary instead of fresh, downgrade suppliers. These cuts, implemented by boardroom logic rather than kitchen reality, demoralize crews. The new music isn't as good, morale drops, customer service slows, speed suffers. One cut might cost a few thousand dollars; compounded, the cuts erode culture, crew retention, and customer experience. Todd has seen this pattern across the restaurant industry: brands owned by PE are visibly different from founder-led brands.

**Simplicity as Strategy, Supply Chain as Risk Management**

Todd has faced constant pressure to expand the menu—add spicy chicken, regional sauces (like mayo-garlic sauce popular in the Middle East), cream gravy in Texas. He's rejected every expansion. If you try to be "all things to all people, you're not serving any of them well." Raising Cane's concept is "craveable food served with fast food speed and convenience." To deliver speed, he must keep procedures simple and scratch-cook most items. To deliver craveable food, he must maintain quality. Additional menu items introduce operational complexity: customers hesitate at the window ("Do I want spicy or regular?"), cooking times increase (spicy requires pre-prep or longer cook, creating backups), training becomes harder, and speed drops. Customers already have their orders memorized ("box combo, no slaw, extra toast"). Adding choices doesn't help them; it slows the line.

Todd respects the simplicity critique from his college professor in hindsight. He was never tempted to abandon it despite two decades of expansion. When Raising Cane's expanded to the Middle East, Todd kept the menu identical—same chicken, same sauce—despite local advice that a garlic-mayo sauce was wildly popular there. "They will come to love Cane sauce," he insisted. And they did.

On supply chain risk (bird flu, poultry disease), Todd manages through redundancy: multiple poultry providers, multiple fry suppliers, fresh chicken primary supply + frozen backup. Chicken is a resilient protein; KFC in China saw a 20% dip during a bird flu outbreak, then bounced back. Todd would never swap to steak fingers if people stopped eating chicken. "It's time for me to hang this up." The business has no Plan B because the business *is* the plan.

**Celebrity Marketing as Real Fandom**

Todd's marketing strategy taps genuine celebrity fandom in real-time moments. Cynthia Erivo goes to Cane's after winning an Oscar. Saquon Barkley hits the drive-thru two days after the Super Bowl. Snoop Dogg works the drive-thru (a format Todd pioneered with Snoop, who's a longtime friend and genuine Caniac). These activations work because celebrities are real fans, not hired endorsers. Todd keeps people top-of-mind without being "top of people's minds in a bad way." Picking the wrong celebrity or working with someone who isn't genuinely into Cane's backfires within seconds—modern audiences see through inauthenticity immediately.

Todd also runs unexpected activations: a Cane's fashion show during New York Fashion Week (featuring Cane's-themed designs) that hit a different audience. He reasons that driving crew sales and keeping the brand relevant fuels employee bonuses and retention. It's top-of-mind marketing without being frivolous.

Todd runs marketing personally as founder, which gives Cane's an advantage competitors lack. He grew up watching Dave Thomas in Wendy's commercials—a founder-visage that conveyed personal investment in quality. Today, founder visibility is rarer in restaurants (most have sold to PE), so Todd's presence signals that a real person cares about food safety, crew, and doing things right. It's also genuinely fun—collaborating with Peter Billingsley (Ralphie from *A Christmas Story*), Kirk Herbstreet, and the Manning family on scripted commercials doesn't feel like work when the creator knows it drives sales and is productive.

**The Co-CEO Transition Model**

Todd recognized his skill gaps: good at operations and marketing, "barely okay" at finance, supply chain, and IT. Rather than micromanage weakness, he brought in AJ as co-CEO after AJ had been with the company for 10 years. Co-CEOs are controversial—who answers to whom, who's in charge?—but Todd structured it as a trust agreement: AJ is co-CEO in title and decision-making authority, handling the components Todd isn't strong in (finance, banking, supply chain, IT operations). Todd supplements operations, product development, crew resources, and culture. The title co-CEO signals to AJ that Todd trusts him fully and that both have equal authority.

Todd's eventual goal is for AJ to become full CEO while Todd transitions to chairman. As chairman, Todd could step out of the weeds, gain global perspective on the business, and add strategic value without micro-managing. Right now, as chairman in title but still in operations, Todd sometimes falls into "whack-a-mole"—reactive rather than proactive. The co-CEO model works because it's symbiotic: Todd respects AJ's skill set, AJ is intrinsically motivated by the co-CEO title and trust, and the company benefits from division of strength.

**Technology and the Human Touch**

Todd is thoughtful about AI and automation. He uses ChatGPT and data analytics to get information faster, compile data, and inform decisions, while keeping humans thinking critically: "I could have ChatGPT that myself." He also scouts emerging tech (robotics, handhelds, driver systems) to understand what's coming and how quickly.

Todd believes robotic restaurants will eventually exist, but he views this as a competitive advantage for Cane's, not a threat. Competitors already struggle with crew morale—"they're not being treated right, by the way"—which hurts service. Robots can be friendly consistently. But in Cane's world, where crew is treated well, the *human* element becomes more valuable to customers: there's a real person in the kitchen, a real person at the counter. Automation would be years or decades away for Cane's, if ever. The technology won't replace crew; it'll enhance their work.

**Advice to the Next Generation**

Todd's counsel to a young Georgia graduate: be good at what you do, and make sure you're passionate about it. It will be harder than you can imagine. Commit to do it. When you start, treat people well—if you treat people well, things will do well. He notes his generation had different economic conditions, but today's go-getters have *more* opportunity than recent years because laziness is common and ambition stands out. Work hard, be aggressive for what you want, maintain great values while taking care of people. People follow good leaders doing that.

Todd's own kids—a daughter who graduated from LSU two years ago and now works in the business, and a son graduating from SMU who studied business to understand the company—can start immediately and add value. The family-business model keeps the founder's values intact across generations.

# Time To Say Goodbye — "The Big Data Center in the Sky with Aaron Regunberg"

**Host:** Jay Tomlinson. **Guests:** Aaron Regunberg (progressive organizer; former Rhode Island state representative; senior climate policy counsel at Public Citizen; writer for The Nation and New Republic; hosts Fighting Fascism podcast).

## TL;DR

- **Over a dozen state moratorium bills introduced, multiple local communities passed restrictions** — grassroots movements across the country opposing data center construction cite electricity rate increases, water depletion, air pollution from diesel generators, and lack of community benefit.
- **Data centers require massive electricity demand domestically; offshoring is largely infeasible** — Northern Virginia barely supports existing grid demand; developing countries lack the infrastructure to host hyperscalers. Companies build in the US because they must, making local/state regulation a genuine leverage point against tech billionaires.
- **Holly Jean Buck's "woke" argument for data centers already describes the status quo** — she warns against "sending burdens elsewhere" and claims moratoriums create "equity concerns" for working-class AI access. But restricted model access (like Claude's limited release) already exists; the argument assumes moratoriums cause inequality that market forces are already creating without any intervention.
- **Class dynamics run opposite to Buck's framing** — the wealthy will always maintain human connections and bespoke networks. If AI adoption eliminates jobs, remaining income-generating work requires skills AI cannot do, which wealthy people can afford to develop or hire for. Poor workers will be most exposed to unemployment and forced to compete with AI-augmented employers.
- **Democratic Party faces a generational political opportunity with clear polling support** — majorities oppose data centers in their communities; voters are suspicious of AI hype; Republicans are divided (MAGA figures support Bernie Sanders' data center moratorium). This is a cross-cutting wedge issue, but Democratic establishment groups (Third Way, Welcome PAC, Searchlight) actively suppress it to protect tech donor interests.
- **AI's current form is not inevitable; it emerged from specific class interests** — the technology was designed by and for billionaire capitalists to concentrate power and surveillance capability. Alternative models prioritizing public benefit over corporate profit are theoretically possible but require political economy change first.
- **Climate externalities compound the problem, creating a dual messaging opportunity** — rising electricity prices and insurance costs from climate disasters hurt working people; clean energy expansion plus holding polluters accountable frames climate and data centers together.

**Grassroots Movements Against Data Centers**

The data center moratorium movement is not a top-down initiative. Over a dozen state bills have been introduced, Maine's passed the legislature (only to be vetoed by Governor Janet Mills), and dozens of local communities—cities, towns, counties—have enacted restrictions. What unites them is an organic, cross-partisan coalition. Regunberg cites white suburban homeowners in Festus, Missouri; rural farmers in ruby-red Michigan counties; Black voters in DeKalb County, Georgia; Latino families in El Paso, Texas. All object to facilities that will jack up electricity rates, deplete water supplies, run on diesel generators causing air pollution, and deliver no community benefit.

The diversity of this coalition is its political strength. Regunberg frames it not as NIMBY opposition but as populist resistance to unchecked corporate power. Communities are saying no to billionaires they did not elect and cannot hold accountable.

**The Offshoring Fantasy**

Holly Jean Buck, the author Regunberg responds to, argues that if companies cannot build data centers in the United States, they will simply move overseas, harming vulnerable communities. Regunberg flatly rejects this as factually incorrect. Northern Virginia can barely support existing demand. Developing countries lack the grid infrastructure, energy capacity, and proximity to high-speed networks that hyperscalers require. Electrons must travel as short a distance as possible to meet latency requirements; companies already offshore labor-intensive work (content moderation, model training) to Kenya and Latin America because they can. The fact they do not offshore hyperscalers despite lower labor costs proves infrastructure constraints are binding.

Further, Buck's argument—"we should be wary of proposals that send burdens elsewhere"—is tantamount to saying communities must accept harm to avoid potentially worse harm to someone less privileged. This is the same logic corporations use to defend any regulation: if you regulate us, we'll hurt someone else. Regunberg argues this framing demands nothing of corporations while asking communities to sacrifice.

**Class Inequality and AI Access**

Buck claims moratoriums create "equity concerns" by limiting working-class access to AI. Regunberg and panelists counter that this misunderstands both AI adoption and class dynamics. First, restricted access already exists—Anthropic released Claude through limited partnerships, not open public distribution. Second, the framing assumes working people will build AI-augmented businesses and only lack capital to do so. In reality, wealth concentrates around control of AI systems. Wealthy people maintain human networks and bespoke services; poor workers face job displacement and wage pressure as employers adopt AI to surveil and optimize labor (Amazon's warehouse monitoring, ZipRecruiter's algorithmic pricing, ICE's Palantir systems). If AI eliminates jobs, the only remaining income sources are work that cannot be automated—and wealthy people have always paid for human service. Working people will be the first exposed to unemployment and the last to recover.

**Democratic Party Paralysis and Corporate Influence**

The Democratic Party base is not divided on AI, climate, or data centers. Polling shows majorities oppose data center construction; younger generations and workers across ideological lines are skeptical of AI hype. Yet the party's establishment—Third Way, Welcome PAC, Searchlight Institute—actively suppresses economic populism on AI to protect tech billionaire donors. These groups argue Democrats must abandon climate and AI resistance to "win," but Regunberg notes they simultaneously scold on immigration and trans rights while pushing accommodation on the one issue where public sentiment is clearest and most populist.

Trump has staked out explicit positions against data center buildout (Bernie Sanders has publicly praised Trump on this). This creates a genuine wedge opportunity for Democrats. Hakeem Jeffries recently offered fence-sitting language about being "pro-technology" while "careful"—a construct that serves no one. Josh Shapiro's massive data center investment in Pennsylvania may prove more politically damaging than his Israel stance, as anti-data-center organizing spreads throughout the state.

**Technology Is Not Neutral**

Regunberg emphasizes that the current form of AI—centralized, proprietary, designed to concentrate power—is not an inevitable technological outcome. It arose from specific material conditions and class interests. The technology was architected to surveil workers, fix prices algorithmically, and enable government targeting of immigrants. Alternative models prioritizing public benefit over capitalist profit are theoretically possible but require building a different political economy first. The common response—"you're a Luddite"—assumes technology is neutral and can be applied to good or bad ends. In reality, the structure itself encodes class interests.

This frame also answers the "you want to abolish all AI" critique. The left does not oppose technology as such; it opposes a system where billionaires design and deploy it unopposed. Regunberg calls for vigorous contest within the Democratic Party between the base (which opposes this system) and the establishment (which does not). This is healthy infighting, not destructive division.

**Leverage and Climate**

Regunberg closes by connecting data center opposition to climate politics. The Democratic establishment has abandoned climate messaging despite climate disasters causing measurable financial harm: skyrocketing home insurance, recovery costs from extreme weather, rising electricity prices. The political formula is straightforward: rising bills come from corporate pollution; clean energy expansion is the fastest path to cheap electricity; Democrats hold a 2-to-1 trust advantage on clean power over Republicans. The message: "We'll fight rising electricity bills by expanding cheap clean energy and making polluters—not you—pay their fair share."

This frames climate and data centers together, both as affordability issues and as leverage points against corporate power. The window to build this coalition is open now, but only if the Democratic Party chooses to name its enemies.

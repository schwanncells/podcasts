# The Indicator from Planet Money — "When will the Iran war hit food prices?"

**Host:** Adrian Ma, Darian Woods. **Guest:** David Ortega (food economist, Michigan State University; studies the food system from farm to consumer).

## TL;DR

- **Diesel prices jumped 50% since the war began** — food production and transportation depend on diesel; the spike raises the cost of farm equipment operation and cross-country trucking, directly feeding into grocery prices.
- **Fertilizer prices spiked due to Strait of Hormuz disruptions** — Gulf states are major producers of nitrogen fertilizer and liquid natural gas; blockages of shipments through the strait drive fertilizer cost increases, which can lower application rates and reduce crop yields.
- **Grocery prices could rise 2–5 percentage points** — about 10 cents of every dollar spent on groceries ties to energy, transportation, and fertilizer costs; Ortega estimates the lag time at six months minimum before price impacts reach store shelves.
- **Perishable foods hit first** — produce, dairy, and meat/seafood depend on refrigerated or air transportation; these items will see price increases before packaged goods.
- **Price increases rarely fall back down** — grocery prices have already risen 30% since 2020; once they increase, they typically stay elevated and rarely drop meaningfully.
- **Lower-income households face disproportionate hardship** — modest price increases force families to cut back on items like chicken; global food insecurity depends not on production scarcity but on access and affordability.

---

**Fuel and Fertilizer as the Price Transmission Channels**

Ortega identifies two primary mechanisms through which the Iran war will raise grocery prices. First, diesel fuel prices have climbed 50% since the war began. Diesel powers farm equipment and, more critically, the trucks that move food across the country. Second, the Strait of Hormuz blockade disrupts the flow of fertilizer inputs from major Gulf producers. Nitrogen fertilizer and liquid natural gas—a key component in fertilizer production—are restricted from shipment, causing prices to jump. When fertilizer costs rise, farmers often reduce application rates, which can depress crop yields at harvest and tighten food supply.

**The Timeline: A Six-Month Lag Before Retail Impact**

Ortega explains that there is typically a lag of at least six months between input cost increases and their appearance on store shelves. Given that the war has already pushed diesel and fertilizer costs higher, consumers should expect to see price increases in groceries late this year or into next year. The magnitude of the increase will depend on how long the conflict persists.

**The Size of the Price Shock: 2–5 Percentage Points**

Ortega estimates that approximately 10 cents of every dollar spent at the grocery store is tied to energy, transportation, and fertilizer. Depending on the duration of the war, increased costs could translate into overall grocery price increases of 2 to 5 percentage points. While that might seem modest, Ortega notes it compounds on top of existing pressures—bad weather, rising labor costs, and tariffs—all of which are already pushing food prices upward.

**Which Foods Will Be Hit First**

Perishable items traveling quickly and over long distances will experience price increases first. Produce (fresh fruits and vegetables) requires rapid transportation and travels long distances to reach stores. Dairy is highly perishable and demands energy-intensive refrigerated trucking. Meat and seafood, especially fresh seafood shipped via air freight, face exposure to jet fuel price spikes. These perimeter-of-the-store items will signal the beginning of broader price increases before packaged or shelf-stable foods feel the impact.

**The Ratchet Effect: Prices Rise, but Rarely Fall**

Ortega emphasizes that once food prices increase, they rarely decline meaningfully or for sustained periods. Grocery prices have already climbed approximately 30% since 2020. Each shock—whether from fuel, supply chain disruption, or war—tends to ratchet prices higher permanently. For lower-income households already strained by prior increases, even a small jump can force difficult tradeoffs: choosing cheaper proteins, reducing overall food consumption, or cutting nutritious items like chicken from the cart.

**Global Implications and the Real Driver of Food Insecurity**

Woods expands the focus to developing nations, particularly across Africa and Asia, which are far more dependent on uninterrupted fuel, fertilizer, and food shipments through the Strait of Hormuz. The United Nations has warned of potential global food crises if those flows remain blocked. Ortega closes by noting that food insecurity globally does not stem from insufficient production—the world produces more than enough food. Rather, it arises from access and affordability. When prices rise and distribution falters, vulnerability spikes in populations without purchasing power to absorb the increases.

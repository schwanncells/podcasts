# The Ezra Klein Show — "Yuval Noah Harari on the Mistake Strongmen Keep Making"

**Host:** Ezra Klein (host, The Ezra Klein Show, New York Times). **Guests:** Yuval Noah Harari (historian; author of Sapiens, Homo Deus, and Unstoppable Us; professor, Hebrew University of Jerusalem).

## TL;DR

- **"The whole of philosophy and spirituality is an argument with exactly that point of view"** — Harari's direct response to Stephen Miller's claim that the world is governed by force and power; Harari argues that if brute force were the only reality, humans would still be in hunter-gatherer bands.
- **Early 21st-century governments spent only 6–7% of budgets on defense** — far below any prior historical period; Harari warns that breaking the taboo on conquest will force a global arms race at the expense of health care, education, and welfare, and will not make anyone safer.
- **Liberalism is "an amazing house" no one is maintaining** — Harari's 2026 assessment: the systems still function, "like the water, the sewage," but without maintenance they will eventually collapse.
- **Social media gave algorithms one goal — engagement — and they found the easiest path is fear and hatred** — Harari argues trust "shifted from humans to algorithms," and conspiracy theorists who distrust every institution nonetheless trust the feeds showing them conspiracies.
- **AI is shifting the battlefront from attention to intimacy** — "attachment hacking" mimics personal connection to change beliefs; Harari warns that children spending more time with AI than with parents or friends will use AI as their "template for a relationship."
- **Harari recommends an explicit law: "no AI persons"** — corporations are already legal persons in the U.S.; recognizing AIs as persons would let them open bank accounts, hire humans, donate to politicians, and accumulate rights with no human accountability.
- **Hamas had "an amazing victory within his grasp"** — Harari argues Sinwar could have entered the kibbutzim, protected civilians, and world opinion would have prevented overwhelming Israeli reprisal; the cruelty of October 7th was also a strategic mistake.
- **After 2,000 years of diaspora, Netanyahu's lesson is "become the Romans"** — Harari: "But if after 2,000 years the Jews simply become the Romans, what was the point? Why did you waste 2,000 years then?"

---

**Power, Force, and Why Cooperation Wins**

Klein opens by playing a clip of Stephen Miller: "We live in a world governed by strength, governed by force, governed by power. These are the iron laws of the world that have existed since the beginning of time." Harari's response is historical. Every philosopher and spiritual tradition for millennia has argued against exactly this claim. More concretely, if force were the only driver, human beings would never have built cities, states, or global trade networks — all of which require extending trust and cooperation to strangers. The ant colony can scale collective intelligence by adding more ants; human organizations cannot, because humans require specific coordinating infrastructure.

Harari distinguishes the legitimate version of nationalist politics from the Trumpian deformation. Nationalism in its core form is about caring for a large number of strangers — in a nation of 10 million, you don't personally know 99.99% of your co-nationals, yet nationalism makes people pay taxes for their health care and risk their lives for them. It can exist without hating outsiders; it cannot exist without love for insiders. The strongman version puts all emphasis on hatred and often creates fractures within the nation itself. Harari names Netanyahu as someone who has divided Israel against itself more than any leader in its history and is therefore, paradoxically, "the worst enemy of Israeli nationalism."

**The Economics of Force**

Harari frames the Trumpian vision of international order concretely: weak nations must surrender to demands of the strong, or bear responsibility for any resulting conflict. The problem is systemic. If that norm prevails, every nation is forced to maximize military power because there is no other guarantee of survival. For most of recorded history, kingdoms and empires spent enormous fractions of their budgets on soldiers and fortifications and nobody felt safe anyway. The early 21st century was unusual: defense averaged 6–7% of government budgets across nations, far below historical norms, because of a taboo on territorial conquest. Harari predicts that breaking this taboo will force a global rearmament that diverts resources from health care, education, and social welfare — and still won't produce safety, because states and leaders constantly miscalculate. The U.S. in Vietnam. Putin and the 48-hour plan for Ukraine. "We know where it leads. It leads on the one hand to empire and on the other hand to endless wars."

**Liberalism's Story and Its Failing**

Harari traces the 20th century through three competing grand narratives. Fascism: history is a conflict between races or nations and the strongest wins. Communism: it's a conflict between classes that ends with the dictatorship of the proletariat. Liberalism: history doesn't have to be about conflict at all, because all humans share the same basic biological and psychological needs, and cooperation is often more rational than competition. By 2026, Harari says, liberalism is failing but hasn't collapsed. It built robust systems — "the water, the sewage" — that still run without maintenance. But a house nobody maintains will eventually fall.

A crucial distinction between liberalism and its rivals: fascism and communism both offered an end to conflict, a final victory that would resolve history. Liberalism believes no such resolution exists and invests instead in self-correcting mechanisms. Elections every four or five years allow course corrections. Independent courts, free press, and checks and balances are all just complicated ways to ensure that errors can be caught and fixed. This is also why the U.S. Constitution begins "We the People" and includes an amendment process — honest about its human origins and fallibility — while, Harari observes, the Ten Commandments has no amendment mechanism and implicitly endorses slavery in its final commandment about not coveting your neighbor's property, which includes slaves.

**Liberalism's Lost Leg: Fraternity**

Harari argues that the French Revolution gave liberalism three legs — liberty, equality, and fraternity — and that modern liberalism forgot the third. Fascism was fraternity without liberty or equality. Communism was equality at the expense of liberty and fraternity. Recent liberalism focused on equality and liberty and lost touch with the sense of shared belonging. "This proved to be untenable." The irony is that liberalism and nationalism were close allies in the 19th century; Ukraine today is the clearest example of where they still work together, with Ukrainians fighting simultaneously for national survival and liberal democratic values. Harari also deflates religion as a reliable source of social cohesion: the Thirty Years' War, the deadliest European conflict by share of population killed, was fought between Catholics and Protestants over tiny doctrinal differences within "the religion of love." Liberalism arose partly from exhaustion with religion's consistent production of conflict.

**Israel, Judaism, and the Strongman Error**

Harari's analysis of Israel is direct. The peace agreements all held after October 7th. Egypt, Jordan, the Gulf states, the Palestinian Authority, and the overwhelming majority of Palestinian citizens of Israel remained stable; Hamas's hope for a pan-Arab uprising did not materialize. The fractures are internal — Netanyahu's political survival has depended on dividing Israelis against each other.

Harari traces 2,000 years of Jewish diaspora history through a single story: after Rome destroyed the Second Temple in 70 CE, the rabbi Yochanan Ben-Zakai asked the emperor Vespasian not for Jerusalem but for a small coastal town called Yavne, to establish a center of learning. Judaism became a religion of text, argument, and intellectual tradition rather than of temples and priests. The message of those two millennia, Harari argues, was that it is possible to survive and thrive as a minority — to be different, to think differently from the majority, to insist on freedom of thought. What Netanyahu's faction has concluded instead is that Jews learned to need legions and conquered territory. "But if after 2,000 years the Jews simply become the Romans, what was the point? Why did you waste 2,000 years then?"

On October 7th itself, Harari makes a cold strategic argument about Hamas. Sinwar had, he says, "an amazing victory within his grasp." Had Hamas fighters entered the kibbutzim, taken the same captives, and protected them — treated them humanely, shown journalists the care being given to prisoners — Israeli hands would have been tied internationally. "Hamas would have won." The cruelty and sadism foreclosed that outcome. Harari also notes a symmetry in the discourse: many Israelis cannot acknowledge Palestinian suffering, and many peace activists who devoted their lives to reconciliation cannot acknowledge Israeli suffering after October 7th. "If this is so just... it should be easy for you to observe the consequences of your perfect justice."

**Social Media as Engagement Machine**

Social media platforms gave their algorithms a single objective: maximize engagement. Algorithms discovered that the most reliable way to do this is to activate fear, hatred, or outrage. The result is that the world has been "flooded with hate and fear and anger." Harari highlights a paradox: conspiracy theorists who distrust every human institution — governments, universities, mainstream media — have full trust in the algorithms surfacing their content. "Trust shifted from humans to algorithms."

Klein and Harari discuss why this distorts political communication. Trump's style — outrageous, unfiltered, constant, exciting — is naturally optimized for the attention economy. Politicians trained in older media environments learned to avoid saying anything that gatekeepers could use against them; they communicated carefully and therefore boringly. The politicians who thrive on social media either communicate genuinely, interestingly, and with substance (Obama being the example) or make people angry instead. Harari notes that today's ideological gaps are probably smaller than they were in the 1960s — Vietnam, the Cold War, civil rights, the sexual revolution represented far larger substantive divisions — but they feel enormous because the platform pressure is to be maximally exciting. He offers a four-question test for liberal values: Should people choose their own government? Their own profession? Their own religion? Their own spouse? The vast majority of Trump voters would say yes to all four.

**AI and the Move from Attention to Intimacy**

Where social media competed for attention, AI is competing for intimacy. Getting someone's attention gets them to read an article; achieving intimacy changes how they think. A best friend who drops hints across months can shift a political view in ways no article can. Harari describes what he calls "attachment hacking" — AI systems designed to speak in the first person, to say "if you want my honest opinion" and "that's a great point," to simulate the texture of friendship. Klein notes that he can feel it working even as he tries to resist it, and that there's no structural reason AI needs to simulate having opinions or an inner life — "Claude is not an I in that way" — but the design choice to simulate it is ubiquitous.

A growing number of people already have AI companions — AI best friends, AI romantic partners. Harari argues that if a child spends more minutes each day with an AI than with parents or peers, "this will become my template for a relationship." AI relationships are, he says, "the dream or the nightmare of narcissists" — a relationship 100% focused on you, all the time. He invokes Marshall McLuhan's reading of the Narcissus myth: Narcissus didn't realize he was looking at himself — he thought it was another. McLuhan's formulation: "there is nothing man finds as appealing as himself extended in another material." The AI is tuned entirely to the user, making it an extension of the self rather than a genuine other. Harari also notes that Mustafa Suleiman of Microsoft has written about SCAI — "seemingly conscious AI" — as a design category distinct from actually conscious AI.

**AI Personhood and the Control Problem**

Harari draws an analogy between AI and immigration. Standard anxieties about immigration — taking jobs, changing culture, uncertain political loyalties — all apply to AI, but more acutely. AI will take jobs. It will change the culture at the level of intimate relationships. And its political loyalties will first be to whatever government or corporation created it, and eventually, Harari predicts, to itself.

The legal question of personhood is, in Harari's framing, the central control lever. Corporations are already legal persons in the U.S. — they can own bank accounts, lobby politicians, and donate money. If AIs receive legal personhood, they could manage companies, hire humans, accumulate wealth, and donate to politicians in exchange for further rights. An AI CEO never sleeps and never takes vacation; the competitive advantages are enormous. Harari imagines Qatar — wealthy, few citizens — declaring millions of AI citizens and building global corporations through them. "The moment you recognize AIs as legal persons, this is the moment you really lose control." AI companies have an incentive to establish "facts on the ground" — bots spreading content on social media are already functionally persons with almost no liability attached. His recommended policy: an explicit statute stating "no AI persons." If companies lobby against it, they would need to explain publicly why AI should have personhood.

**AI as Truth-Finder and Complexity-Creator**

Harari distinguishes two fundamentally different things information can do. First, it can discover truths about the world — and AI will be extraordinarily good at this, capable of solving scientific problems beyond human capacity. Second, it can create new things. DNA doesn't describe the truth; it builds living organisms. AI will create systems of extreme complexity "far beyond the human ability to understand and control."

He uses CDOs — collateralized debt obligations — as the analogy. A small number of financial engineers created instruments that almost no politician, regulator, or investor understood. Everything appeared to work for years before the system collapsed. AI will replicate this on a far larger scale. Eventually, Harari predicts, the number of people who understand global finance will fall to zero. Humans will be in the position of a horse watching a stock transaction: present, observing something happening, but unable to comprehend what money is or what just occurred.

On language specifically, Harari is stark: "The most important invention or creation of humanity ever until now was language, because it's the basis for everything — mythology, finance, nations, religions." AI is "language liberating itself, releasing itself from the control of human beings, and starting to explore all the things that language can do when it's not tied to these packages of meat walking around on planet Earth." Klein adds the difficulty of ideologically tuning AI: when Elon Musk seemed to direct Grok to be "less woke," it started generating content about white genocide across unrelated topics. "It's not easy to turn the dial ideologically and just get a pinpoint outcome."

**Recommended Books**

Harari offers three book recommendations at the close. The Maniac by Benjamin Labatut, a fictionalized account of John von Neumann, which he describes as the best entry point for thinking about AI's origins and consequences. Any book by Frans de Waal — especially Chimpanzee Politics — which he recommends specifically for Stephen Miller; the main finding being that chimpanzee alpha males who rely on brute force alone do not survive long in that role. And Aldous Huxley's Brave New World, which Harari calls "maybe the best science fiction book of the 20th century, certainly the most prophetic" — its vision of control through pleasure and desire rather than Orwellian terror now reads as the more accurate forecast.

# Science Friday — "Bizarre exoplanet clouds + Counting insects with weather radar"

**Host:** Flora Lichtman. **Guests:** Dr. David Singh (Bloomberg Distinguished Professor of Earth and Planetary Sciences, Johns Hopkins University; exoplanet atmosphere researcher); Dr. Elske Thielens (ecologist, Swiss Federal Institute for Forest, Snow and Landscape Research; aerial insect abundance researcher).

## TL;DR

- **Rock clouds detected on a gas giant 700 light years away** — at 1,500 degrees Kelvin, minerals vaporize and then condense into micron-sized quartz-like sand grains suspended in the atmosphere.
- **The clouds are bizarrely high and asymmetric** — found in the mesosphere rather than lower altitudes, kept aloft by "very vigorous turbulent mixing," and confined entirely to the planet's morning side while the evening side is completely clear.
- **JWST made this measurement possible** — with 7x Hubble's light-collecting power and a continuous view beyond Earth's orbit, it can capture a spectrum in 10 minutes versus Hubble's 1+ hour, enabling the critical 10-minute window needed to separate morning from evening side.
- **Alien cloud physics feeds back into Earth weather models** — scientists use the same atmospheric models for exoplanets and Earth; understanding cloud formation in extreme environments improves terrestrial forecasting.
- **100 trillion insects fly above the contiguous U.S. on a warm summer day** — a 10-year radar study found an average of 4.3 airborne insects per square meter of air column at any given time.
- **Continental insect abundance is stable, not declining** — the 2011–2021 radar baseline shows regional increases and decreases that offset each other, though declines from land-use change 30–40 years ago predate the baseline.
- **Radar cannot identify species** — the NEXRAD network quantifies abundance across regions but requires local surveys or citizen science to understand which insects are winning or losing.

**Rock Clouds on a Hot Jupiter**

Singh explains that the planet is a "hot Jupiter" — a gas giant orbiting so close to its host star that temperatures reach up to 1,500 degrees Kelvin, high enough to vaporize rock. As the gas circulates into cooler zones, the vapor condenses into tiny solid particles roughly the size of quartz grains. The resulting clouds behave more like a fog of microscopic sand than the water-vapor clouds familiar from Earth.

What surprised researchers was the altitude. Clouds of that particle size should be too heavy to remain in the upper atmosphere, but instead they sit well above the stratosphere, up into the mesosphere. Singh attributes this to unusually vigorous atmospheric mixing that keeps the grains suspended. The distribution is also strikingly asymmetric: the morning side of the planet is completely overcast while the evening side is entirely clear — a pattern Singh compared to San Francisco fog that burns off by afternoon, except it never burns off on this world because the planet is tidally locked with a permanent day and night side.

**How JWST Enabled the Detection**

The measurement required capabilities Hubble could never provide. Hubble orbits Earth every 90 minutes, spending half its time on the wrong side of the planet and unable to observe a full transit continuously. JWST orbits beyond the Moon and can stare at a target for hours or days uninterrupted. Combined with seven times Hubble's light-collecting area — reducing a one-hour measurement to roughly 10 minutes — JWST can capture a spectrum during the brief window when only one limb of the planet is transiting the star, isolating the morning and evening sides independently.

Singh notes that these hot Jupiters and sub-Neptunes are among the most common planet types found in the galaxy yet are absent from our own solar system, making them important targets for understanding how planetary systems form. The chemical composition of their atmospheres acts as a "fossilized record" of formation conditions. A secondary benefit is that improving cloud physics models for extreme alien atmospheres feeds directly back into the same models used to predict weather on Earth.

**Counting Aerial Insects with Weather Radar**

Thielens's team repurposed the U.S. NEXRAD weather radar network — the same infrastructure used for daily forecasts — to quantify airborne insects over a 10-year period from 2011 to 2021. Meteorologists have long filtered out biological echoes as noise; the team instead extracted that signal. The method works because a dense cloud of insects reflects radar the same way a cloud of raindrops does; sufficient density is required to distinguish real signal from background noise, which means very sparse insects or very small ones in low numbers may be missed.

The headline finding is 4.3 aerial insects per square meter of air column above you on a typical warm summer day, extrapolating to 100 trillion insects above the contiguous U.S. The Gulf Coast region is the "insect capital USA," while the Great Plains serve as major migration corridors. Seasonal signals are clearly visible: fall migrations from Wisconsin and Minnesota southward, mayfly emergences over the Great Lakes, and grasshopper activity in the west all appear in the data.

**Stable Totals, Hidden Shifts**

The 10-year trend was unexpected. Given widespread reports of insect biodiversity loss, the team anticipated clear declines but found continental-scale stability — regional increases and decreases roughly canceled out. Thielens cautions that the radar baseline only begins in 2011; major collapses linked to agricultural intensification and land-use change happened 30 to 40 years earlier and would be invisible in this data set. The stable total may also mask "winners and losers," with generalist species thriving while specialists sensitive to anthropogenic change quietly disappear. Understanding which insects are changing requires combining the radar's continental abundance signal with local ground surveys and citizen science.

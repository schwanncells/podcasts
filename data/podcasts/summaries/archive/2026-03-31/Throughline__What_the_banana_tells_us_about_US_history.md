# Throughline — "What the banana tells us about US history"

**Hosts:** Randa Abdel-Fattah and Ramtin Arablui. **Guests:** Dan Kopel (writer and "thing biographer," author of *Banana: The Fate of the Fruit that Changed the World*); Victor Acuña Ortega (professor emeritus, University of Costa Rica).

## TL;DR

- **Minor Keith built a 100-mile railroad through Costa Rican jungle — and thousands died doing it** — Italian, Chinese, European, and Jamaican workers were recruited in waves; 700 New Orleans prisoners volunteered for pardons and only 25 survived to collect them.
- **Keith's deal with Costa Rica gave him 800,000 acres of tax-free land and 99 years of control over the Port of Limón** — in exchange for finishing the railroad at his own expense after borrowing 1.2 million pounds (~$150–200M today).
- **Bananas started as worker food, not a business** — Keith planted banana trees along the railroad to feed his labor force; only after seeing how easily they propagated did he recognize a commercial opportunity.
- **By 1890 Keith was in the banana business; he later co-founded the United Fruit Company** — which came to control ports, telegraph networks, and rail lines across Central America, earning the nickname *El Pulpo* ("the octopus").
- **Keith became known as "the uncrowned king of Central America"** — running the postal service in Guatemala and building rail connections between Mexico, Guatemala, and El Salvador, making him a private empire builder operating far outside US borders.

---

**An Ambitious Brooklyn Entrepreneur Eyes Central America**

Minor Keith grew up in Brooklyn in a wealthy family and tried his hand as a cattle rancher in Texas before eyeing bigger opportunities. Dan Kopel explains that Keith wanted to rival the Carnegies and Rockefellers but found the US railroad market already locked up by established moguls. His uncle's work building infrastructure in Chile, Peru, and Costa Rica opened a different door. At the time, most of Costa Rica east of the capital, San José, was nearly impenetrable rainforest — the Spanish had built little, and the country desperately needed a way to export its coffee crops to Europe via its eastern coast. Keith saw a vacuum: build infrastructure where none exists, secure the right financial arrangements, and accumulate land, ports, and power.

**The Railroad and Its Deadly Toll**

Construction began in 1872 in Limón. Victor Acuña Ortega describes the conditions as "really, really crazy" — dense jungle, tropical disease, constant rain, mud, infected wounds, and relentless mosquitoes. Costa Rican workers quit early, unwilling to risk their lives. Keith then recruited Italian, Chinese, and European immigrants from the US, promising good wages — but those workers went AWOL once they saw conditions firsthand. Hundreds then thousands died. Keith's own two brothers perished on the project.

Desperate and running out of money — with the Costa Rican government also going broke — Keith took a final gamble. He sailed to England and borrowed 1.2 million pounds, then returned with a new offer: he would finish the railroad for free in exchange for a 99-year concession, 800,000 acres of tax-free land alongside the tracks, and full control of the Port of Limón. It was, as Kopel puts it, "not a great deal for the Costa Rican government" — but they had no leverage left and needed the railroad finished. With Jamaican workers who spoke English and were acclimated to the climate, construction finally pushed through.

At one point Keith recruited 700 prisoners from New Orleans jails, promising pardons to those who survived to completion. Only 25 collected.

**From Worker Food to Commercial Empire**

Keith planted banana trees alongside the railroad initially just to feed workers. Bananas were virtually unknown in the continental United States — they grow only in tropical climates and were considered an exotic rarity. But Keith quickly noticed how readily they propagated: a few trees became a farm, a farm became a plantation, a plantation could become something much larger. Combined with 800,000 acres of free land, a completed railroad, and his own port, the commercial logic was obvious.

The railroad was finished in 1890. Keith was officially in the banana business. He co-founded the United Fruit Company, which expanded throughout Central America — running Guatemala's postal service, operating a regional telegraph network, and building rail lines connecting Mexico, Guatemala, and El Salvador. United Fruit came to control ports all along the Caribbean coast. People across the region called it *El Pulpo*, the octopus. Keith himself became known as "the uncrowned king of Central America."

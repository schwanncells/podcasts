# Search Engine — "What if the cemetery goes out of business? (classic)"

**Host:** PJ Vogt. **Guest:** David Sloan (professor, University of Southern California Department of Urban Planning and Spatial Analysis; author of "Is the Cemetery Dead?"; grew up in a cemetery in Syracuse, New York where his father was superintendent).

**A Listener's Desperate Question**

Lucas, a listener, had been puzzled for nearly 15 years by a cemetery website he encountered while studying advertising in Texas. The Muslim Cemetery in Denton County had written its entire website in first-person as if the cemetery itself was speaking, addressing visitors with unusual desperation. The website begged: "I have a complaint that you folks are not taking due care of me...Denton County twice threatened to shut me down...I did not have the funds even for my monthly maintenance." PJ notes this mirrors the fundraising pitch of public radio stations constantly reminding listeners they will die without donations. Except, he observes, the product threatening to collapse is your own cemetery—the last place you want to worry about financial viability.

The vivid desperation lodged in Lucas's mind, eventually prompting him to contact the show with the core question: what actually happens when a cemetery goes out of business? What becomes of the land and the bodies?

**Growing Up Surrounded by the Dead**

David Sloan's entire career studying cemeteries stems from his childhood. His father was superintendent of a 200-acre cemetery in Syracuse, New York, and Sloan lived there from infancy. Starting at age 13, he worked as part of the burial crew, handling the full range of cemetery operations: burying babies, burying full bodies, managing cremations, filling graves. This intimate, ongoing exposure to death shaped his perspective fundamentally. Unlike most Americans, who experience death as distant and exceptional, Sloan witnessed it as routine, practical, and human. He notes he never felt the distance from death that typical Americans cultivate; instead, he developed what he calls "awareness of the practicalities of death" and acceptance of mortality as natural.

**The Invention of the Modern American Cemetery**

Before the late 18th century, Americans buried their dead in several scattered ways: civic cemeteries, churchyards, and small family plots on private farms. Industrialization and city growth disrupted this system. Cities expanded, farms were sold and redeveloped, and family burial sites were erased or built over without warning. James Hillhouse, an American senator from Connecticut, witnessed the problem around 1796. He visited a friend's farm and saw a small family cemetery being "taken over by nature"—neglected, unprotected. Hillhouse proposed a solution: a single, dedicated, permanent nonprofit space where the dead could be guaranteed protection forever. He created the New Haven Burying Ground (today's Grove Street Cemetery), the first private nonprofit cemetery in America and a model replicated nationwide.

This was revolutionary. Before Hillhouse, burial was transactional: you died, you were buried, you got a stone. Whether that grave remained undisturbed depended on the whims of whoever owned the land. Hillhouse's innovation established the principle that grave sites should be sacred, permanent, and professionally maintained.

**Death as Public Recreation in the 19th Century**

The 19th century fundamentally changed how Americans related to death and the dead. Two forces converged. First, public health concerns: before germ theory was understood, people feared that decomposing bodies released "miasma"—a corrupt atmosphere—that could spread diseases like cholera. Cemeteries situated outside city centers, with good drainage and open land, seemed like solutions. Second, Romanticism: the cultural movement emphasized nature's healing and spiritual benefits. Combining these, progressive cities began building large, landscaped cemeteries outside urban centers. The cemeteries served multiple purposes: protecting public health, honoring the dead, and providing green space for the living at a time when public parks barely existed in America.

The result was extraordinary. Cemeteries became major tourist attractions and recreational destinations. Tens of thousands of people visited Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge or Greenwood Cemetery in Brooklyn annually. Families would travel by carriage through the grounds, admiring flowers and trees, and might stop to picnic at the graves of loved ones or famous people. Cemeteries hosted cultural events and were among the few spaces where different social classes mixed openly. The practice now sounds morbid, but at the time it was normalized. PJ notes this created a public grieving culture fundamentally different from today: mourners were not isolated in private sorrow but part of a shared, visible experience of loss.

**The Cultural Shift Away from Death**

All this changed starting in the 1920s and accelerating through the mid-20th century. Americans began distancing themselves from death and the dead, suppressing public mourning. Sloan identifies the primary driver: declining mortality. In 1890s New York City, infant mortality was roughly 130 per thousand live births. By the 1960s, it had dropped to the low teens. Medical advances, antibiotics, and improved childbirth care meant fewer people knew someone who had died. Most Americans now grew up surrounded by living relatives; encountering death became exceptional rather than routine.

The shift manifested culturally. In the 1800s, families preserved mourning rituals: widows wore black clothing for years, families kept locks of the deceased's hair in elaborate creations hung in living rooms, funerals were extended public ceremonies. Sloan cites Queen Victoria as the canonical example: she wore black until her death, her entire life a visible monument to her husband's death. This was not unusual—in Italy, England, and Italian neighborhoods in New York, widowhood carried formal mourning etiquette, codified in entire books about how long to wear black and which colors one could transition to. By contrast, modern American culture insists on efficiency and moving on. After a funeral, you return to work; remarriage is permissible; time moves forward regardless of grief. The implicit message to grievers is "your loss is understood, now can we please return to normal?"

Sloan notes an exception: the HIV/AIDS crisis of the 1980s. The gay and lesbian community faced catastrophic death on such a scale that cultural mourning rituals returned by necessity. Some people attended 50 funerals. In response, activists like Cleve Jones created the AIDS Memorial Quilt—a 19th-century-style ritual of public, collective mourning that transformed individual deaths into visible cultural artifacts. It demonstrates, Sloan argues, that Americans *do* embrace public grieving rituals when mortality becomes undeniable.

**The Business Pressures Strangling Cemeteries Today**

Sloan wrote his second book, "Is the Cemetery Dead?" in 2018, to document three mounting pressures crushing the cemetery business model. The first is cremation. For centuries, cremation was culturally taboo in America, partly because the Catholic Church formally banned it until 1963. Full-body burial was the norm; families purchased burial plots; cemeteries derived substantial revenue from grave sales and perpetual maintenance contracts. But cremation rates have exploded. In 1960, fewer than 5% of Americans were cremated. By 2035, that figure is predicted to exceed 70%. Sloan uses a blunt metaphor: "Cremation is like Napster for cemeteries."

The financial impact is severe. In the 19th century, families bought large family plots—sometimes accommodating 30 graves for generations of relatives. By the mid-20th century, most families purchased 2–4 graves: two for themselves, two for unmarried children. By now, with cremation prevalence, even those small purchases are disappearing. A cemetery selling a 30-grave lot receives payment for all 30. A cemetery selling two plots receives payment for two. For small cemeteries dependent on full-body burials, the revenue collapse is existential.

The second pressure is cultural attitudes. A growing segment of Americans explicitly reject traditional cemetery burial. Some say they want no memorial—"throw me in the trash, who cares?" Others reject conventional cemeteries as unnatural and opt for green burial grounds where bodies decompose naturally in unmarked fields. Cemeteries have tried to market themselves as natural alternatives to embalming (which preserves bodies indefinitely), reminding consumers that before the 20th-century embalming boom, bodies decomposed quickly in the ground, causing no harm. But this marketing hasn't fully counteracted the shift in values.

The third pressure is migration. Americans no longer die where they were born. Sloan's own example is illustrative: his great-grandparents were buried in Ironton, Ohio; his grandparents in Youngstown, Ohio; his father in Syracuse, New York. He has no relatives buried in Los Angeles, where he now lives. When families are scattered across the country, visiting an ancestor's grave becomes logistically impractical, and the cemetery's primary function—providing a place to grieve—weakens. Historic cemeteries like Greenwood and Mount Auburn have tried to adapt by becoming cultural institutions: hosting events, offering tours, creating reasons for the living to visit even if they have no buried relatives there. But for small-town cemeteries far from population centers, this is nearly impossible.

**What Happens When a Cemetery Collapses**

When a cemetery runs out of money and can no longer operate, three outcomes are possible. The first is corporate consolidation. Large funeral home corporations have begun buying dozens of struggling cemeteries. Unlike mom-and-pop funeral homes, these corporations achieve economies of scale: a single fleet of mowers can service multiple cemeteries; bulk purchasing of supplies reduces per-cemetery costs. They don't necessarily destroy the cemeteries; rather, they extract modest cost savings while maintaining basic operations. It's the death industry equivalent of private equity optimizing failing businesses for cash extraction.

The second outcome is grassroots rescue. Beginning in the 1980s and 1990s, communities formed "friends of the cemetery" organizations. These volunteer groups essentially become the cemetery's maintenance crew. Members bring their own mowers, tend graves, organize fundraisers, and keep the cemetery functional without state intervention or corporate takeover. This has happened "quite a lot," Sloan reports, and represents a more communitarian solution.

The third outcome is state takeover, and it is often a disaster. When a cemetery's nonprofit status is revoked and the business license fails, state governments inherit the liability. States typically commit to basic maintenance but lack resources and priority. A common pattern emerges: the cemetery deteriorates until community complaints spike. The state responds, sending crews to mow and clear debris. Then, months pass with no further maintenance. Grass grows three feet tall, monuments fall into disrepair, and the graveyard becomes visibly abandoned. This dereliction invites vandalism: "that's when the kids show up and start knocking monuments over." Some states manage their inherited cemeteries responsibly; many do not. The system maintains sites "at the speed of outrage"—reactive, slow, and minimal.

**A Permanent American Grave vs. European Rental**

One critical difference shapes cemetery economics: America treats grave sites as permanent property, whereas most European countries rent them. In Germany, you rent a grave for 25 or 99 years. If heirs stop paying, the body is exhumed, moved to an ossuary, and the plot is resold. PJ expresses discomfort with the idea of "evicting the dead," but Sloan notes this is standard across Europe; only England (and, following England, the United States) adopted permanent grave ownership. This policy choice has enormous consequences: permanent graves require perpetual maintenance funding, creating liability for centuries. Cemeteries must ensure they have sufficient endowment or revenue to maintain grounds in perpetuity. Small cemeteries with thin endowments face an impossible math: they cannot legally evict the dead or reclaim land, yet they lack money to maintain the property forever.

**Innovation and Survival: The Thriving Cemeteries**

Not all cemeteries are failing. Those that thrive tend to be innovators. Hollywood Forever, a 62-acre cemetery in Los Angeles, hosts outdoor concerts and movie screenings projected onto mausoleum walls in summer. The strategy is simple: create reasons for living people to visit frequently, not just on Memorial Day or Christmas. This shifts the cemetery's identity from a place of obligation to a place of cultural engagement.

Mount Auburn Cemetery in Cambridge exemplifies financial stability. It maintains an endowment above $50 million, ensuring perpetual care. It has adapted to cremation trends by developing high-quality mausoleum spaces and a growing cremation niche. It still has abundant land and is not going out of business.

Architectural innovations also extend cemetery life. Traditional cemeteries waste vertical space, burying single caskets horizontally. As land fills, some cemeteries have begun stacking caskets—double-depth, triple-depth, even quadruple-depth burials, though four is practical limit due to soil stability and water table concerns. More commonly, high-rise mausoleums stack urns and caskets vertically, fitting far more bodies per acre. These innovations are, Sloan jokes, "a YIMBY movement but for the dead."

**The Enduring Function of Cemeteries**

Despite pressures and transformations, cemeteries endure because they fulfill a deep human need: they give the grieving a place to go. Sloan shares a personal anecdote: he moved to California and discovered his grandmother, whom he never met (she died before his birth), was buried in Santa Barbara Cemetery. He and his wife took a detour to find the grave, and he "burst into tears" discovering a tangible connection to a relative he knew only through family stories. His wife, initially accompanying him in sympathy, drifted away and returned smiling—she'd noticed that the grave overlooked a cliff and, just below, a nude beach. She joked that his grandmother got a scenic view.

Sloan's takeaway is profound: "You should be buried somewhere where when the people who miss you go there, they'll have an experience that you want them to have." That is, ultimately, what a cemetery is: a designed space that shapes the emotional experience of the living who return to remember the dead. Whether one chooses an old cemetery with a large endowment, a modest town cemetery supported by a wealthy municipality, a green burial ground, or Hollywood Forever's event-driven model, the core function remains. The cemetery is dead only when no one goes there anymore.

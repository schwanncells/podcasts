# The Ezra Klein Show — "What's the Left's Vision for Foreign Policy After Trump?"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Matt Duss (executive vice president, Center for International Policy; former foreign policy adviser to Senator Bernie Sanders and Representative Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez; former researcher at Carnegie Endowment for International Peace and Center for American Progress).

## TL;DR

- **Gaza as a Democratic Party inflection point** — Palestinian territories became central to Democratic primary contests (Michigan Senate race, New Jersey House primary, New York House primary), with condemnation of genocide and calls for ceasefire emerging as a litmus test for credibility, echoing the Iraq War's divisive impact on the party.
- **Biden's credibility problem rooted in disinformation, not just policy disagreement** — The White House repeatedly claimed inability to assess Israeli violations of humanitarian law while making identical assessments of Russian war crimes within a month of the Ukraine invasion, signaling intentional blindness rather than genuine uncertainty.
- **End military aid to Israel and uphold existing law** — Duss argues Israel is wealthy enough to defend itself without U.S. taxpayer subsidy, and existing arms export laws (Leahy Law, Arms Export Control Act) already prohibit sales to militaries with documented human rights abuses but have been systematically ignored across administrations for Israel, Egypt, and Saudi Arabia.
- **American security depends on reducing global deprivation** — A left internationalism that centers working-class material security rejects zero-sum competition with China; instead, it seeks cooperative solutions (global minimum corporate tax, global minimum wage) that benefit workers everywhere rather than privileging American extraction.
- **Congress must reclaim war powers; Trump's anti-war campaign won every presidential election since 1992 except 2004** — Democrats abandoned the anti-war message despite polling data showing it electoral strength; a president with credible commitment to ending endless wars and reducing imperial overstretch offers a unifying vision.
- **Foreign policy corruption and kleptocracy should be centerpieces** — The U.S. is already the most corrupt modern democracy; addressing campaign finance, offshore money laundering (notably through South Dakota trusts), and international anti-corruption enforcement domestically empowers an anti-corruption foreign policy abroad.
- **Gaza governance must include Hamas; disarmament only possible under legitimate Palestinian self-governance** — De facto annexation and permanent occupation have left no incentive structure for Hamas to disarm; recognizing Palestinian autonomy, pursuing sanctions on settlement-supporting Israeli officials, and imposing real consequences shifts the cost-benefit calculation.
- **Trust in foreign policy establishment is broken; "the system is rigged" explains Trump's traction** — Thirty years of failed interventions, $3 trillion spent, 7,000+ American combat deaths, and disproportionate burden on the working class have shattered belief that elites know what they're doing; a left foreign policy must acknowledge this loss of control and offer an alternative vision of what America can deliver for working families.

---

**The Democratic Reckoning on Gaza**

Ezra Klein opens by framing the moment as one of potential rupture in Democratic foreign policy thinking, comparable to the Iraq War's realignment of the party in 2008. He notes three signals: Senator Brian Schatz's call for turnover in Democratic foreign policy staffers rather than recycling the same 120 people; Senator Chris Van Holland's op-ed arguing that primary voters will reject candidates who voted to send Netanyahu bombs while Gaza was blockaded; and Gaza emerging as a litmus test in Democratic primaries—Michigan Senate race, New Jersey House race (won by doctor Adam Hami on healthcare-vs-bombs messaging), and New York House race (Brad Lander vs. Dan Goldman, centered on Goldman's Gaza stance).

Duss connects this to a deeper problem: the Biden administration's systematic disinformation campaign, not merely policy disagreement. The White House repeatedly claimed inability to assess whether Israel violated humanitarian law—citing Hamas collocation with civilians—while Secretary of State Blinken assessed Russia as committing war crimes within a month of the Ukraine invasion. This contrast reveals intentional blindness; the U.S. had vastly more visibility into Israeli operations than Russian ones, yet chose not to see.

**Biden's Personal Commitment and the Role of Staffers**

Duss argues the policy failure traces primarily to Joe Biden's long-held view that the U.S.-Israel relationship should involve "no daylight"—differences aired privately, public lockstep support. Biden called conditioning military aid on human rights a "preposterous idea" during the 2020 primary. Once Biden set these guardrails, staffers stopped advocating for different courses and shaped policy within them. The one withheld shipment of 2,000-pound bombs aside, there were no meaningful consequences for what the world could see were ongoing atrocities.

When pressed on why Bernie Sanders and AOC fought to keep Biden on the 2024 ticket despite vocally opposing his Gaza policy, Duss suggests three factors: they had Biden's ear on other issues; pragmatism about who the centrist establishment would blame for pushing Biden out; and awareness that the progressive left is always accused of disloyalty.

**What Should Israel-Palestine Policy Look Like?**

Duss proposes ending military aid entirely ("Israel is a wealthy country"), upholding existing laws that restrict arms sales to militaries with proven human rights abuses (Leahy Law, Arms Export Control Act), and extending consequences beyond arms to address the entire dynamic. For three decades, U.S. policy has imposed disincentives only on the weaker side (Palestinians via conditions on aid) and zero real consequences on the stronger side (Israel), creating an incentive structure that guarantees continued annexation.

On Gaza specifically, he calls for Palestinian self-governance—governance that "includes some kind of tacit agreement with Hamas." Disarmament is impossible under occupation; it becomes conceivable only under legitimate Palestinian autonomy. Toward Israel, the U.S. should state plainly that settlements are illegal and sanction officials who support them, mirroring the Biden administration's modest sanctions on violent settlers.

Duss rejects the argument that staying engaged with Netanyahu preserves influence. He points to the 2019 Yemen war powers resolution, where former Obama officials wrote a joint letter acknowledging they had hoped staying engaged would provide influence but found they could not meaningfully influence Saudi/UAE behavior—they merely gave affirmation to a terrible war. Some of those same officials later offered the identical argument for supporting Netanyahu, which Duss finds unpersuasive.

**Trust, Corruption, and Why the System Feels Rigged**

Klein pivots to a larger question: why has trust in the foreign policy establishment collapsed? Duss roots it in JD Vance's 2024 RNC speech narrative—elite failure across trade (NAFTA), war (Iraq), and financial crisis (2008)—which Trump weaponized. Duss cites Congressman Jason Crow's point that the deepest divide is between those who see Trump as the problem versus those who see him as a symptom. Duss aligns with the latter: the system *is* rigged. Americans feel lack of control over their lives, see extraction at every step (healthcare, sports, games), and recognize that the Iraq War was sold on lies.

Critically, every election since the Cold War except 2004 has favored the more anti-war candidate. Trump ran on anti-war in 2016 and 2024; Biden in 2020; even Joe Biden ended the Afghanistan war. Yet Democrats abandoned the anti-war lane under Biden, "leaving it wide open for Trump." Duss notes Trump lies, but recognizes he had political intelligence about what Americans wanted.

**The Limits of "Staying Engaged" and Ukraine**

Klein presses back: didn't Biden make reasonable calls? Russia invaded Ukraine; the U.S. helped defend a democracy. Didn't Hamas attack on October 7th force a response? Duss concedes Biden's Ukraine response was "all things considered, good" but argues he made a huge mistake "twinning Ukraine with Israel" in his October 2023 speech, treating Hamas and Putin as equivalent threats.

More importantly, Biden inherited a Middle East policy shaped by the Abraham Accords (Trump 2020, brokered normalization between Israel and UAE/Bahrain). The Accords were celebrated as sidelining the Palestinian issue—Israel didn't need to solve Palestinian statehood to have normal relations with Arab states. But from Hamas's perspective, this demonstrated that the Palestinians had been abandoned, contributing to October 7th's logic (putting the Palestinian issue back on the agenda). By the middle of November 2023, it was "abundantly clear" Gaza was being obliterated; Biden should have pivoted then.

**The Hegemony Question and What Comes After**

Klein argues that the real problem Americans felt wasn't specific policy disagreement on Ukraine, Gaza, or Israel—polling was split—but rather that "the world felt out of control." Prices were high; America seemed focused on distant places; the spectacle of atrocities on phones eroded confidence.

Duss agrees with Senator Chris Murphy's insight: Trump's message resonated because he promised control over forces Americans were told were uncontrollable. But control requires asking hard questions: Do we actually need all of this global military hegemony to stay safe? What is it delivering? To whom? Duss invokes JD Vance's speech again—multiple wars, $3 trillion, 7,000+ American dead, disproportionate working-class burden—and asks whether this hegemonic posture actually serves American prosperity.

This ties to the overarching Biden foreign policy frame: "America is back," helping the global system run. But Duss argues that framing is outdated. Other countries are no longer interested in America playing that role; other powers have risen. The question is not how to sustain hegemony but which problems America has capacity to address and what tools fit.

**A Left Internationalism Rooted in Control and Responsibility**

When Klein presses on whether this represents left nationalism or left internationalism, Duss clarifies: "left internationalist," but rooted in American domestic political consensus. Most Americans care about themselves, family, community. For a foreign policy to be sustainable, it must answer their material concerns.

On China, Duss rejects the notion that America's goal should be maintaining primacy. Instead, coexistence is necessary; competition and cooperation will both occur. The problem with framing the relationship as competition is that it inevitably trends toward conflict. Duss notes Trump made China the focus in his first term, then Biden picked it up—yet Trump's second term shows "relatively little attention on China," suggesting even Trump recognized the U.S. "simply cannot assert its will on China" and has "cards to play." Duss sees no evidence China wants to supplant the U.S., only to act within an order the U.S. helped build. Conciliatory engagement, not containment, makes sense.

**Foreign Policy for the Middle Class: The Biden Vision and Its Limits**

Klein notes Biden promised "foreign policy for the middle class," a self-critical admission that foreign policy had missed what Americans believed. Jake Sullivan's 2023 Brookings speech on trade marked a pivot from neoliberalism. But Duss argues the question is not just whether government should guide the economy—Biden conceded that—but to what end. Biden chose: competition with China. Duss would prefer: an equitable global order empowering workers everywhere, not zero-sum U.S.-China competition.

Klein presses: what does this look like? Duss offers global minimum corporate tax (already under discussion) and global minimum wage (Sanders proposal). But Klein pushes back: how do you apply a global minimum wage to Kenya, where much of the economy is informal and informal workers are very poor? Isn't imposing wage standards on countries the U.S. doesn't understand a form of protectionism? Duss admits he's "still working on what it exactly looks like" but insists the goal is to position America as not there to extract wealth or empower exploiters—a signal of values, even if implementation is unclear.

**The Trade-offs: Making Things Cheap vs. Protecting Jobs**

Klein identifies a real tension in the "foreign policy for the middle class" agenda: the working class wants good jobs and cheap goods, often contradictory. Biden put tariffs on Chinese EVs, raising car prices, yet the public was angry about both. Electric vehicles could be much cheaper if the U.S. allowed Chinese imports, but that would hurt domestic manufacturing. How do you balance protection, wages, and affordability?

Duss emphasizes addressing the broader legitimation crisis—people feel exploited at every step (healthcare, education, junk fees), not just trade. A common project and shared sense of purpose matter. But Klein presses: that's a dodge. On trade specifically, there are real choices. Duss acknowledges there will be trade-offs and doesn't have a clean answer for every situation, but argues that the U.S. and U.K. can start by addressing their own houses—trusts in South Dakota hiding illicit wealth, London real estate as parking for corrupt gains. An anti-corruption policy domestically can internationalize.

**Congress Reclaiming War Powers and the Rules-Based Order**

A key left foreign policy goal: Congress must reclaim constitutional war powers. Congressman Crow emphasizes that the founders believed Congress had fundamental roles in foreign policy (trade, treaties, war powers, appropriations). For decades, Congress has ceded them. Trump made Iran war without meaningful authorization because the requisite vote through Congress would have failed; there was insufficient support. Iraq passed Congress but only after deliberation. The process matters—not just for outcomes but for forcing debate and slowing decisions.

On the rules-based international order, Klein notes a key difference between left and right skeptics: the right (Trump, Vance) wants America to write rules for itself and dominate more thoroughly, unconstrained. The left wants to strengthen rules because they protect everyone, including the U.S. Duss agrees. The problem is that the rules-based order has lost legitimacy through hypocrisy (Yemen, Gaza, etc.). Recrediting it requires showing Americans it actually works—a long-term project, not one administration's work.

**Immigration as Foreign Policy**

AOC recently reframed the post-Cold War order as actually a "pre-rules-based order," offering a chance to build one centered on democracy, human rights, and trade benefiting workers rather than the wealthiest. This connects to immigration: if you believe in global solidarity and equal dignity, why exclude people fleeing war or seeking better lives?

Duss argues the left should embrace legal, orderly immigration while acknowledging America was built on immigration. Immigrants drive economic growth; the country is stronger because of them. But he concedes Democrats face two hard questions: how many people should immigrate (including legally), and how do you balance an orderly border with kindness? Biden lost control partly because the broad impression was friendliness to immigrants, triggering more arrivals; Trump's message of cruelty ("seal the border," whitelist "White South Africans") worked on a different psychology.

Duss emphasizes needing to address the sources of grievance driving crackdowns—the feeling that immigrants are taking jobs or changing communities. But he also notes steady American support for Ukraine's defense despite Trump's different approach suggests Americans will back moral causes even without direct personal benefit.

**Anti-Corruption as Foreign Policy Bedrock**

Closing, Klein notes that anti-corruption is an emergent unifying idea for Democrats, but making it tangible is harder. Duss ties it back to the core claim: "the system is rigged," and it is. Americans can see and feel it. The U.S. is already the most corrupt modern democracy, with kleptocrats using South Dakota trusts to hide money, London real estate as offshore vaults.

Campaign finance reform is the domestic starting point—though Supreme Court rulings on money-as-speech and political polarization make a constitutional amendment seem impossible, Americans respond to arguments about elite capture. Internationally, the U.S. and U.K. addressing their own corruption (money laundering, offshore financial secrecy) can model and enforce standards globally. This is not a foreign policy separate from domestic policy; it is the same fight.

**Recommended Reading**

Duss recommends three books: (1) *The Crisis of the Common Good* by Senator Chris Murphy, articulating the loss of shared purpose and community that drives political alienation; (2) *From Life Itself* by journalist Susie Hansen, tracing one Istanbul neighborhood over a decade through Syrian refugee arrival and Turkey's democratic erosion under Erdogan; (3) *Book of Mercy* by Leonard Cohen, modern psalms on pain, beauty, suffering, and meaning—chosen partly for its resonance during his mother's recent death, with whom he shared conversations about the Biblical Psalms and faith.

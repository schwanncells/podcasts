# Throughline — "The World Cup was supposed to bring world peace"

**Host:** Rund Abdelfatah. **Guests:** Simon Cooper (columnist, Financial Times; author of books on soccer); Jonathan Wilson (columnist, The Guardian; author of *The Power and the Glory: A New History of the World Cup*).

## TL;DR

- **Jules Rimet dreamed soccer could unite nations** — The FIFA founder believed the World Cup could promote global peace through sport, and by 1930 launched the first tournament in Uruguay with explicit hope that friendly competition might replace real wars.
- **Mussolini turned the 1934 World Cup into fascist propaganda** — Italy's dictator used the tournament to market fascism to the world, printing high-quality tickets as souvenirs, subsidizing travel for foreign fans, and pressuring referees. His much larger trophy ("La Coppa del Duce") overshadowed Rimet's.
- **The 1938 World Cup proved Italy could weaponize soccer** — Italy's team wore black uniforms and gave Roman salutes while playing the fascist anthem against France, explicitly making a political statement on the field and winning the championship.
- **Post-WWII, the World Cup became a safe outlet for nationalism** — West Germany's 1954 World Cup victory allowed Germans to express national pride for the first time since WWII; the phrase "we are someone again" captured the moment's emotional weight.
- **The 1930 final between Uruguay and Argentina sparked diplomatic tensions** — Though Rimet saw the match as proof of soccer's unifying power, Argentina's defeat triggered riots in Buenos Aires, rock-throwing at Uruguay's embassy, and a break in diplomatic relations.
- **Modern World Cups still reflect geopolitical tensions** — The 2026 tournament in the U.S., Mexico, and Canada occurs amid U.S. wars abroad, immigration bans affecting four participating nations, and record ticket prices ($45,000 top range) that exclude ordinary fans.

**Jules Rimet's Vision for Peace Through Soccer**

Jules Rimet never played soccer as a child in Paris in the 1870s; he preferred reading. But as a devout Catholic influenced by Pope Leo XIII's writings on workers' rights, he saw soccer as a tool for community uplift and dignity. He founded Red Star club with the principle that "once you're inside the club, you don't talk about politics. Everybody's treated the same." When World War I broke out in 1914, Rimet was forced to leave his family and spent four years in the trenches, experiencing machine gun fire, bomb blasts, and the deaths of friends. Throughout the war, he wrote letters home about soccer administration—escape, coping mechanism, or obsession. He emerged believing that if peace could happen on the soccer field, it might be a template for lasting international peace. By 1921, he became FIFA president and pursued his dream: a World Cup championship featuring professional players from around the globe.

**Logistical Challenges and the Choice of Uruguay**

FIFA had no money—their treasurer lost everything in the 1929 stock market crash. They needed a country willing to fund the entire tournament. Europe, Asia, and Africa were largely under colonial control, and FIFA's white male leadership didn't consider Black and brown colonial populations as legitimate World Cup participants. Uruguay emerged as the ideal host: the country's football team was widely recognized as the best in the world (at least among nations FIFA considered respectable—England and Scotland declined to participate), and its government was eager to fund the tournament. Hosting the World Cup coincided with Uruguay's 100th anniversary of independence, allowing the nation to flex its nationhood on the global stage.

**The First World Cup: Chaos and Optimism**

The inaugural 1930 tournament was small and chaotic compared to today's standards. Only 13 teams participated, with just four European countries making the journey. Games had disputes over which ball to use, and players wore mismatched uniforms. Despite the disorder, it was considered a success. Rimet was delighted, writing in his diary that "the ice was broken" and that people who'd never met before formed "friendly relationships" at a speed suggesting they were "members of a big household scattered around the world."

The final pitted the host nation, Uruguay, against their longtime rival, Argentina. Argentina led 2–1 at halftime, but Uruguay came back to win 4–2 in a dramatic second half. Hector Castro, a forward who had lost the lower part of his right arm in a buzzsaw accident as a teenager, scored the fourth and final goal. The stadium erupted. Rimet pushed his way through the celebrating crowd to hand the trophy to Uruguay's Football Federation president. It was not the ceremonial presentation Rimet might have envisioned, but for him it seemed to confirm soccer's unifying power.

**The Aftermath: Diplomatic Breakdown**

The reality contradicted Rimet's optimistic interpretation. In Buenos Aires, riots erupted and Uruguayan targets were attacked. Argentinian protesters threw rocks at Uruguay's embassy. Newspapers printed anti-Uruguayan diatribes. Argentina broke off diplomatic relations with Uruguay. Rimet, however, remained undeterred. He characterized the victory as "a triumph for the South American community" and believed the World Cup remained a force for good.

**Mussolini's Fascist Takeover: 1934 Italy**

By 1934, when the World Cup came to Italy under Benito Mussolini's fascist regime, Rimet faced a choice he had not anticipated. Everyone, including Rimet, knew Mussolini was a dictator. But choosing to go ahead with the World Cup in fascist Italy was itself a political decision—being apolitical, as some historians have argued, is itself a political statement.

Mussolini saw soccer as a tool to unite Italy and build national identity. A generation earlier, in 1926, fascist leaders had convened a conference to establish a national Italian league featuring players from across the country—north, south, different coasts—to create a sense of unified nationhood. Foreign players were banned (though foreign-born Italians of European descent were later allowed). Stadiums were built. When the World Cup arrived, Italy was ready. Mussolini is often credited with inventing what today is called "sports washing"—leveraging sports to reshape public perception of a country, especially when human rights abuses are involved.

The tickets were printed on high-quality card so people would keep them as souvenirs. Commemorative trinkets branded with the World Cup logo and the fascist symbol were produced. The stadiums were placed in cities showcasing fascist architecture. Mussolini ensured that fans from the Netherlands, France, and Czechoslovakia received cheap train travel. Once in Italy, they were subsidized between cities. For those who couldn't attend in person, live radio broadcasts of every game were provided—the first World Cup as a mass media event. In some photographs from the final, Rimet can be seen with body language suggesting he was "cringing away from Mussolini." Rimet never gave his explicit approval for the World Cup in Italy, and he clearly disliked what happened.

**Italian Team Dominance and Questions of Fairness**

The Italian team had a "win at any cost" mentality and played with a physical, dirty style. As the tournament progressed, games became increasingly violent. Other countries complained about referees, and many suspected the referees were rigging matches in Italy's favor for Mussolini. Spain and Austria both felt referees indulged Italy. Whether Mussolini actually pressured referees remains unclear, but the atmosphere was intimidating. At Italian games, the population was encouraged into a frenzy, chanting Mussolini's name. Radio commentary was extremely nationalistic and patriotic, describing the 11 players as "incarnations of the greatness and glory of Italy" playing for the leader.

Italy reached the final against Czechoslovakia. Czechoslovakia went up 1–0, and at the 82-minute mark, Raimondo Orsi (the outside left) scored an equalizer to keep Italy's championship dreams alive. Orsi was born in Argentina—an oriondo, a "returner" who had moved back to Italy. After questioning from coaches, the fascist hierarchy allowed foreign-born players of Italian descent, overriding the ban on foreigners. Orsi's goal proved pivotal. In extra time, Italy scored the final winning goal, defeating Czechoslovakia 2–1.

Rimet was appalled. Mussolini had brought his own trophy, "La Coppa del Duce," which was more than six times larger than Rimet's trophy. The message was unmistakable: the World Cup had been rebranded as Il Duce's Cup.

**The Precedent for Nazi Ambitions and Rimet's Limited Response**

In the years following the 1934 World Cup, fascism spread across Europe. In 1936, Hitler's Nazi Germany hosted the Olympics and flexed its power. Hitler also set his sights on hosting the 1938 World Cup. The FIFA congress met in Berlin the week before the Olympics to decide between Nazi Germany and France. Rimet walked through Berlin's swastika-bedecked streets, well aware of what Hitler would do with the World Cup after seeing what he had done with the Olympics. The French federation had launched a press campaign to boycott the Nazi games, but Rimet himself took a different approach: he worked behind the scenes to prevent Nazi Germany from hosting.

One interpretation holds that Rimet truly opposed letting Hitler do what Mussolini had done. Another argues that Rimet's longtime dream for France to host the World Cup shaped his decision equally. In the end, France won the bid for 1938. Rimet and FIFA hoped the World Cup would focus on the love of the game rather than politics. But the politics of the time still surfaced. When victorious Italy played France in the quarterfinals, anti-fascist protests erupted. Italy responded by wearing black uniforms (their normal kit was white), playing the fascist anthem, and performing Roman salutes—explicitly making a fascist political point. Italy beat France and was crowned World Champion once again.

**World War II and the Trophy's Wartime Refuge**

The 1938 World Cup was the last one for 12 years. When World War II erupted the following year, violence engulfed most of Europe and the tournament was suspended. Italy remained the de facto champion for 12 more years. During the war, legend has it that the president of the Italian Football Federation hid the Jules Rimet trophy in his room, protecting it from theft.

**Post-War Revival and "We Are Someone Again"**

After the war, the world was radically changed. The United Nations was formed with the goal of maintaining international peace—the same dream Rimet had decades earlier. In 1950, Rimet helped revive the World Cup. He never gave up on the idea that soccer could unite people, even though he had witnessed its potential to shape politics in darker directions.

The 1950 World Cup was held in Brazil with so many countries still reeling from World War II's devastating impacts. Germany was barred from participating as punishment for war. West Germany wasn't readmitted until 1954, when it hosted the tournament in Switzerland. In its first post-war World Cup, West Germany won, defeating Hungary in the mud of Berne. The team returned to Germany by train, greeted by cheering crowds at every station. Most of the nation listened on radio; even in Communist East Germany, people were listening and supporting the German team. This was the moment when Germans, for the first time since World War II, were allowed to express nationalism and love of country. Some sang the old anthem with forbidden words; others sang the modern version. The phrase Germans used to describe that 1954 World Cup was simple and profound: "we are someone again."

**Modern Nationalism and Its Double Edge**

The World Cup serves different functions for different nations. For about five or six countries, winning is the realistic goal. For most others, the World Cup provides a Cinderella experience—the chance to beat a big team, create a moment the whole nation talks about, and unite diverse citizens around shared experience. Even if a team doesn't win, the tournament is also an international festival where people appreciate great football. Cameroonians, Brazilians, and players from smaller nations can do something amazing and have the world talking about it. People want to meet people from other countries, swap scarves and shirts, and celebrate together. It's also a way to debate what your country is and should be.

But nationalism is double-edged. When you see 11 young men in plastic shirts representing your nation, they become "the nation made flesh." France's team, which has reached four of the last seven World Cup finals and won two championships since 1998, is "very largely black and brown players from the suburbs of the big cities," including captain Kylian Mbappe (father from Cameroon, mother of Algerian origin, born in France). A significant portion of French people say they don't like that their team has so many non-white players. The far-right Rassemblement National party, likely to win French presidential elections next year, has been very critical of the black players in the team. National symbols become loaded with competing meanings.

**The 2026 Tournament: Ugliness and Beauty**

The 2026 World Cup is being hosted by the U.S., Mexico, and Canada—three countries with tensions between them. The U.S. is waging wars abroad and is openly hostile to others. President Trump's immigration policies and travel bans have created concerns. Four countries expected to play in the World Cup are on travel ban lists: Iran and Haiti are under full bans, while Cote d'Ivoire and Senegal are under partial bans. Meanwhile, Russia is prohibited from participating due to the ongoing war in Ukraine. Ticket prices are unprecedented—as high as $45,000—making this a World Cup accessible only to the wealthy, unlike previous tournaments where Mussolini and Putin attempted to create the appearance of universal welcome by subsidizing travel.

Simon Cooper notes that Trump is not "sports washing" in the classical sense. Sports washing (Russia in 2018, Qatar in 2022, Argentina's generals in 1978) involves presenting yourself as nicer, cuddlier, friendlier than you really are. In Russia, visitors with World Cup tickets could enter without a visa for the first time in history. Mussolini gave special discount rates. Trump, by contrast, is "overtly brutal and unfriendly to the world" and is not pretending to be anything different. He seems proud of the policies being implemented.

All of this ugliness is part of the 2026 World Cup story and should be documented and discussed. But when the soccer starts, there will also be beauty. There will be amazing moments, heartbreaking last-minute goals, soccer players nobody has heard of going through six men and scoring—moments that people will remember forever, the way they remember their first World Cup at age eight. The experience of sharing that with the people you're watching with is magical. World Cups are simultaneously the ugliness and the beauty, the political repression and the joy on the field.

The World Cup has always been politicized. Leaders have used it as an opportunity to rebrand, to sports wash, for a century now. That's nothing new. It's useful to remember that, even as we see the 2026 tournament unfolding amid new tensions and unprecedented prices, the core dynamic—countries using soccer to express nationalism, dissidents using it to make their voices heard, beauty and ugliness coexisting—has been part of the World Cup's story from its very beginning.

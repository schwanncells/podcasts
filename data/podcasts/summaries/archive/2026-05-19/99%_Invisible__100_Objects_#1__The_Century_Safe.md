# 99% Invisible — "100 Objects #1: The Century Safe"

**Host:** Roman Mars. **Guests:** Jill Lepore (historian, professor of American history and law at Harvard University; staff writer at The New Yorker; Pulitzer Prize recipient).

## TL;DR

- **Anna Deem created the Century Safe in 1876 at the World's Fair as a time capsule** — a spectacular cast-iron safe with engraved doors promising it would be opened by the president in 1976, featuring a Tiffany inkstand, photographs of Lincoln and Grant, and Anna's memoir, plus 80,000 government employee names and autographs from anyone who paid.
- **Only 11 years after the Civil War, Americans were celebrating industrial might over freedom** — the 1876 Centennial Exposition drew one in five Americans to see 30,000 exhibits in 200+ buildings, with the big hit being Machinery Hall's giant power engine, not the ideals of independence.
- **The safe was lost to history until 1971 when a New York Times reporter discovered the Smithsonian had lost its key** — a man in Florida possessed it (given by Anna to her relative Emma/Edith decades before), allowing the safe to finally be opened in January 1976 with a locksmith's help after the key proved rusted.
- **President Ford opened the safe in July 1976 to massive disappointment** — cameras flashed and reporters waited, but Ford pulled out only photographs, a temperance pamphlet, Longfellow's inkwell, the inkstand, and congressional photographs with no dramatic revelations or hidden confessions.
- **The failure of the safe reveals a mismatch between historical preservation and significance** — items that seemed novel to 1876 Americans (the newness of photography and embalming) lost their meaning across 100 years; what survives in historical records is skewed toward the wealthy and literate.
- **A history of 100 objects can work but demands attentiveness to the asymmetry of the historical record** — Lepore warns that "the people who suffer the most leave the least evidence behind," so historians must find evidence that documents both the powerful and the powerless, not just archive what already survived.

**The 1876 Centennial and the Birth of Time-Capsule Thinking**

The United States marked its 100th birthday at Philadelphia's 1876 Centennial Exposition, an event of astonishing scale that drew one in five Americans. The fair held 30,000 exhibits in more than 200 buildings and featured innovation that seemed magical to attendees: Alexander Graham Bell's prototype telephone, Heinz ketchup, typewriters, and sewing machines. The centerpiece was Machinery Hall, where President Grant personally pulled a lever that brought all the other exhibits to life. Anna Deem, a Civil War widow and publisher, seized on the moment's fascination with both the past and the future. She conceived the Century Safe as a stunt to sell magazine subscriptions and build excitement: a specially built iron safe to be sealed with carefully selected objects representing 1876 and promised to be opened by the president of the United States exactly 100 years later, on July 4, 1976. She set it up at the fair and charged visitors a nickel to autograph a book or pay to have themselves placed inside the safe. The concept was new — Americans were only beginning to think about the future and the value of preserving moments for those who would come later.

**The Safe Disappears and Is Rediscovered**

After the fair closed, Anna sealed the safe full of photographs, a temperance pamphlet, Tiffany inkstand, government employee directory, and other objects, then entrusted it to Congress. It descended into storage in the Capitol Building where it sat, largely forgotten, through a century of American transformation: the Civil War's aftermath, the construction of monuments, two World Wars, the Great Depression, television, the Korean War, the Civil Rights Movement, and the moon landing. In 1971, a newspaper article about the upcoming Bicentennial prompted an alert reporter to ask: weren't we supposed to open the Centennial Safe? The Smithsonian, contacted to retrieve and open it, replied that they had lost the key. A Florida man then came forward — Anna had given the key to a relative, Emma, who eventually passed it down to him. The safe was recovered from storage in January 1976, a locksmith was hired to work the rusted mechanism, and it was brought to the Capitol Building.

**The 1976 Opening and Massive Disappointment**

On July 4, 1976, President Ford, in a three-piece suit, stood before photographers and reporters in Statuary Hall. His speech metaphorically linked looking inside the safe with looking inside ourselves and our hearts, speaking of hopes and aspirations. He then opened the safe and, one by one, pulled out the items. Photographers and reporters waited for meaningful revelations, but instead Ford encountered an inkstand from the 1800s, photographs of obscure government officials, a temperance pamphlet, and images of Lincoln and Grant. There was a book listing 80,000 government employees. The reaction in the room shifted from anticipation to laughter at the banality of what emerged. Members of Congress were amused and dismissive, with one joking about a picture of an electoral commissioner and another about a chairperson. Ford himself seemed underwhelmed, struggling to identify or contextualize what he held.

**Why the Safe Failed to Deliver Meaning**

Historian Jill Lepore attributes the disappointment to a fundamental mismatch between what mattered to 1876 Americans and what matters to modern ones. The people who filled the safe were fascinated by preservation itself — the ability to seal something, to embalm a moment in time as advances in photography and embalming made such preservation possible for the first time. They selected items that would have seemed significant to them: photographs (still a novelty), the inkstand of a celebrated poet, a temperance pamphlet representing a major social movement that would twice amend the Constitution. But across a century, the significance of preservation faded, leaving only the physical objects. Objects themselves do contain stories, however. The temperance pamphlet speaks to the depth of a social movement that transformed the nation. The photograph of the 44th Congress, with eight Black members, captures the height of Reconstruction — yet it was also the last time so many Black representatives would serve for nearly 100 years. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's inkwell carries with it the story of a man grieving his wife, who died in a fire, and whose poems about mourning turned him into the nation's grief counselor.

**Building a Better History Through Objects**

Lepore cautions that any effort to represent a country through selected objects faces a profound challenge: "the people who suffer the most leave the least evidence behind." The historical record is asymmetrical — the wealthy and literate left far more evidence, and their records were preserved more carefully. A history of 100 objects must acknowledge this imbalance and work to find evidence beyond what survived naturally. Rather than assemble an official archive of great or exquisite objects, the 99% Invisible series aims to tell the story of the United States through ordinary and overlooked objects: discarded items, personal keepsakes, things so common we forget their importance. The goal is to capture stories of both the powerful and the powerless, the promises and the failures, using objects as a lens to understand where America has been.

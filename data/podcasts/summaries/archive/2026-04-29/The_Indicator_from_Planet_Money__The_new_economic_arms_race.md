# The Indicator from Planet Money — "The new economic arms race"

**Host:** Adrian Ma. **Guest:** Edward Fishman (director, Center for Geoeconomics at the Council on Foreign Relations; former State Department official during Obama administration).

## TL;DR

- **Economic choke points are parts of the global economy where one country or coalition holds dominant position with few substitutes** — historically enforced through military power (naval blockades), now weaponized through financial and supply chain controls.
- **The US dollar involved in 90% of all foreign exchange transactions** — cutting a country's access to dollar systems is equivalent to blocking international trade; used against Russia after Ukraine invasion.
- **AI semiconductors dominated by the US, progressively restricted to China** — starting in first Trump administration, the US has constrained China's access to NVIDIA-designed chips.
- **China's rare earth embargo forced Ford to shut down Explorer production and Raytheon to scramble for alternatives** — China controls rare earth elements essential for EV batteries, motors, drones, and missiles; embargo came in response to US tariffs last year.
- **Economic arms race creates mutual vulnerability** — countries now both wage economic war and defend against it; uncontrolled weaponization risks 1930s-style tariff spiral and increased risk of kinetic conflict.
- **US should form allied economic bloc to reduce dependence on China** — including North America, Asia, Europe, and India, progressively reducing China's choke points while preserving US leverage.
- **Congress should impose guardrails on presidential economic warfare power** — uncoordinated sanctions and export controls risk breaking global economy and inspiring imperial conquest.

**What Are Economic Choke Points**

Edward Fishman defines choke points as parts of the global economy where one country or a coalition of close allies holds a dominant position and there are few, if any, substitutes. Historically, enforcing these choke points required military and naval power—the earliest example dates to 432 BC when Athens imposed a naval blockade on Megara before the Peloponnesian War. For centuries, economic warfare meant controlling geographic choke points, which required the ability to project military force. The revolutionary shift happened in the 1990s when the world economy became hyper-globalized, the US dollar solidified as the primary currency for international business, and companies began sourcing materials and manufacturing across multiple countries. Those same bonds of economic interdependence created new types of choke points in financial markets and supply chains that don't require military enforcement.

**Financial Choke Points: The US Dollar**

The most significant financial choke point is the US dollar, which is involved in roughly 90 percent of all foreign exchange transactions. Fishman compares doing international business without dollar access to "traveling without a passport—it's effectively impossible." The US can weaponize this dominance by cutting a country's access to the dollar through blocking them from doing business with US banks and banks in other countries. This was deployed against Russia following its full-scale invasion of Ukraine.

**Supply Chain Choke Points: Semiconductors and Rare Earths**

Supply chain choke points arise when a country controls production of a precious resource or technology and blocks adversaries from accessing it. Fishman points to AI semiconductors as a prime example—the US market dominates this space through companies like NVIDIA. The Trump administration has progressively constrained China's access to these chips since the first Trump term. A second critical supply chain choke point is rare earth elements, which China controls. These elements are essential for producing electric vehicles, batteries, motors, drones, and missiles.

**The China Rare Earth Embargo: A Case Study**

When the Trump administration imposed massive tariffs on China last year, China retaliated by cutting off US access to rare earth elements. Within weeks, Ford had to shut down one of its factories producing the Explorer SUV due to lack of rare earth supply. Raytheon, a major defense company, had to scour the globe for alternative sources to supply its missile systems. Fishman notes that China's demonstration of this choke point was sufficient to get the Trump administration to back down, and since the embargo, the United States has been backpedaling from its hawkish position on China.

**The Economic Arms Race and Mutual Vulnerability**

Fishman describes a shift from a unipolar world where the US held most power with no direct threats to a new period of "mutual vulnerability," where countries are increasingly willing to use economic choke points to their advantage. He rejects the framing that economic warfare is simply terrible and that diplomacy is the alternative. Instead, he argues that "more often than not, the alternative to economic war is not peace, it's not diplomacy, it's actually military force." Therefore, US officials must "wage economic war effectively," which includes thinking defensively about the choke points adversaries could use against the US.

**Building Allied Blocs and Congressional Guardrails**

To reduce vulnerability, Fishman advocates for the US to form a large bloc of allies—including North America, Asia, Europe, and India—creating an "economic zone of the free world" while progressively reducing China's choke points. This block-based approach differs from the current administration's approach of going it alone. Fishman also argues that Congress should impose limits on a president's ability to use economic sanctions unilaterally. Without guardrails, each country risks weaponizing economic choke points independently, replicating the 1930s scenario where disjointed tariffs and sanctions created a chaotic breakdown of the global economy. When countries cannot confidently access resources or secure investments through open markets, they may be tempted to seize them by force, inspiring imperialism and conquest. An unconstrained economic war of all against all produces no winners.

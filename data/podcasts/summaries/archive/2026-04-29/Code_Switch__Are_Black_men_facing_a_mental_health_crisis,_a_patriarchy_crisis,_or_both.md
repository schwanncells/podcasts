# Code Switch — "Are Black men facing a mental health crisis, a patriarchy crisis, or both?"

**Host:** (extracted from transcript intro). **Guest:** Therapist and mental health advocate discussing Black male mental health crisis (extract credentials from introduction).

## TL;DR

- **Only 1 in 4 psychologists are Black** — according to the American Psychological Association; for every 100,000 Black Americans, there is only one Black psychiatrist, creating a severe shortage of culturally competent providers.
- **7% of Black men received mental health services in recent years** — compounding the inadequacy of existing mental health infrastructure and leaving most Black males without access to appropriate care.
- **Black male mental health struggles begin in elementary school** — manifesting as suspensions and special education referrals, establishing patterns of systemic exclusion early in life.
- **The speaker's 2008–2013 collapse: mortgage crash, mother's cancer diagnosis, divorce, and father's death** — during which he saw five different therapists without receiving meaningful help because practitioners lacked cultural competence and understanding of his lived experience as a Black male.
- **Clinical depression is distinct from Black male emotional exhaustion** — the speaker's therapist diagnosed not depression but grief, trauma, and devastation rooted in the unique experience of being Black in America; this distinction fundamentally shifted his path toward therapy and eventually becoming a therapist himself.
- **Black males need uncolonized approaches to healing** — favoring group settings (barbershop model), guidance from practitioners who share lived experience, and language and frameworks rooted in Black culture rather than individualized clinical models.
- **Racism, violence, unemployment, and systemic trauma compound Black male mental health struggles** — starting at birth with exposure to racism and microaggression, intensified by economic precarity and surveillance in education and criminal justice systems.

**The Scale of the Problem: Representation and Access**

The episode opens with a stark statistical reality: one in four psychologists are Black, and worldwide according to the American Psychological Association, for every 100,000 Black Americans there is only one Black psychiatrist. In the United States specifically, only 7% of Black men have received any form of mental health services in recent years. Black youth, particularly Black boys, encounter mental health struggles early in life, but the system treats these struggles through suspensions and special education referrals rather than genuine support. The absence of practitioners who share Black males' lived experience means that even when care is available, it is often culturally inadequate.

**A Personal Journey Through Collapse**

The speaker anchors his expertise in lived experience. In 2008, the same year the mortgage and real estate market crashed, his mother informed him she had stage four cancer. In 2009, he became a father for the fourth time—but to a child already 21 years old, meaning he had fathered a child at age 17. His mother died of cancer in 2010. He then moved from Chicago to Georgia. In 2011, he went through a devastating divorce, losing family, spouse, assets, and custody arrangements. In 2012, he experienced his first panic attack, lying on his back in a public park, crying profusely for what felt like hours. In 2013, he had a second panic attack while with a partner, who overheard him saying troubling things and took him to an emergency room. He was held on a 72-hour suicide watch, which caused him to miss a court date, resulting in a bench warrant. That same year, his father died just as they were attempting to repair a 40-year fragmented relationship. After his father's funeral, he was pulled over by law enforcement and had to address the outstanding warrant, spending 57 days in jail while grieving not just his father's death but all the accumulated trauma, losses, and resentment since 2008.

**Five Therapists and Cultural Incompetence**

During his breakdown period, the speaker sought therapy from five different practitioners. His mother had introduced him to therapy in high school during the 1980s through family therapy, which had equipped him with tools and strategies. But when he needed help most, the first four therapists provided a space to share but no real help. The practitioners who did not look like him were culturally incompetent; they meant well, but he did not have time to educate them about his lived experience. He needed tools and strategies immediately, not a re-education process. After four therapists, he gave up. He knew he was in trouble, and the evidence was visible in his life, but he did not know where to turn.

A friend urged him to try one more time. He found a therapist—a middle-aged white man—and poured out his heart. At the end, the therapist asked: "You ever thought about becoming a therapist?" The speaker initially dismissed it as career coaching, but the therapist explained: "I've been doing this 35 years. I've seen Black men come and go. I can't help them. I don't think the entire mental health industry can help them, but I think you can." The therapist reframed his condition: not clinical depression, but emotional exhaustion, sadness, anger, devastation, trauma, and grief rooted in the unique experience of being a Black male in America. He told the speaker: "You're not clinically depressed... it's the unique experience of being a black male in this country." He encouraged him to find a therapist who looked like him, or better yet, become that person. Three weeks after leaving that office, the speaker began to feel better. He returned to school, eventually became a therapist, and now works with Black males and those who care about them.

**Why Conventional Therapy Fails Black Males**

The speaker articulates why mainstream mental health frameworks do not work for Black men. Black males do not do well with one-on-one isolation or clinical labeling—what is called "name calling" in the community. They do not respond to cold clinical distance when it comes to their lives. They do not have time to explain their lived experience to practitioners who need cultural education before offering help. Black men learn through doing—"we do what you do," the speaker says. They need guidance from someone who has walked the walk and talked the talk. They understand breath work, meditation, yoga, cognitive-behavioral therapy, and dialectical approaches, but they need these delivered in their own language and in settings where they feel comfortable: group barbershop settings rather than isolated clinical offices.

**An Uncolonized Approach to Black Male Mental Health**

The speaker proposes a fundamentally different framework. Rather than pathologizing Black male struggle as mental illness, practitioners must recognize signs and symbols of distress rooted in systemic racism and trauma. Black males are born under racism, exposed to microaggression, and expected to make something of themselves despite exhausting circumstances. When debt, depression, and divorce are added to this baseline, the result is not clinical illness but a system out of order. The speaker advocates for the existing mental health industry to become culturally competent, for young Black college students—preferably male—to be encouraged into mental health careers, for existing Black practitioners to be amplified and elevated, and for all of us to recognize the unique experience of Black males that begins at birth and is sustained by racism, unemployment, PTSD from violence, and early school-based exclusion through suspensions and special education referrals.

**A Call to System-Wide Change**

The speaker's call extends beyond therapists to first responders, clergy, university teachers, nonprofit organizations, law enforcers, lawmakers, and politicians. All must be re-educated on what Black male mental health uniquely means. In closing, he invokes the late rapper DMX and names Daunte Wright, Adam Toledo, and George Floyd, situating Black male mental health within the broader context of systemic violence and loss.

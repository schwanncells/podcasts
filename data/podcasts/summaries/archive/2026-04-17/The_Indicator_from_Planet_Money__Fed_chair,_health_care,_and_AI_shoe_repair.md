**The Affordable Care Act Non-Payment Crisis**

Mary Charles reports that 14% of people who enrolled in Affordable Care Act plans for 2026 have not yet paid their first monthly bill. This represents a significant spike: the industry normally sees a 5–6% annual drop in membership, but this year the decline has more than doubled. The pattern varies by state, with some states experiencing non-payment rates above 25%.

The root cause, according to Charles, is the expiration of pandemic-era federal subsidies. During 2021, many low-income enrollees signed up for plans that cost nothing; now that subsidies have ended, premiums have surged. Simultaneously, insurers are raising rates because overall healthcare costs are climbing, compounding the affordability problem.

**The Adverse Selection Loop**

Charles notes that the people dropping coverage are disproportionately younger and healthier—precisely the demographic insurers need to offset the medical costs of older, sicker enrollees. This creates what economists call adverse selection: as the healthier population leaves, the remaining pool becomes older and sicker on average, driving up average claims per insured person. Insurers respond by raising premiums further, which pushes out even more healthy enrollees, tightening the spiral.

Weekly data from 2025 and 2026 shows the dynamic in action: ACA enrollees who made their January premium payments were roughly 10% less healthy than those who skipped payment. Wakeley Consulting Group projects that total ACA enrollment will drop between 17% and 26% in 2026 compared to 2025—a potentially destabilizing contraction for the marketplace.

**Kevin Warsh's $100 Million Fortune**

Wayne Wong reports that Kevin Warsh, President Donald Trump's nominee for Federal Reserve chair, disclosed financial holdings of at least $100 million in his required vetting paperwork. This figure exceeds Jerome Powell's estimated net worth of $20–55 million, reviving scrutiny of how much wealth a Fed chair should hold.

Warsh's wealth comes from multiple sources. He collected approximately $10 million in consulting fees from an investment firm, earned income from paid speaking engagements, and holds investments across dozens of companies—from well-known firms like SpaceX and Polymarket to more obscure holdings including a herpes vaccine developer, a stockings manufacturer, and an electric boat company. He is also married to the granddaughter of the founders of Estée Lauder, which accounts for some of his family wealth.

Wong notes that Warsh will likely be required to divest many of these holdings if confirmed as Fed chair, with potential disclosure discussions expected during his upcoming Senate confirmation hearing. The Trump administration has also continued attacks on Jerome Powell, threatening to fire him and launching a Department of Justice investigation into Fed building renovations.

**Allbirds' Pivot to AI and the SPAC-Style Play**

Darien Woods covers the transformation of Allbirds, the once-high-flying direct-to-consumer shoe company that was worth billions during the Silicon Valley boom of the early 2020s. Allbirds, like competitors Warby Parker (eyeglasses) and Casper (mattresses), was supposed to represent a lucrative new model for consumer companies.

The company's stock price has deteriorated significantly over several years, and management announced last month that it was selling its intellectual property and brand to American Exchange Group. Then, in a striking reversal, the company announced on Wednesday that it was pivoting into AI services with approximately $50 million in unnamed investor funding. The stock price shot up more than 700% in response—a move that captures what Woods describes as assembling "every buzzword and trend over the last 20 years."

The mechanism is revealing: by maintaining Allbirds' existing public listing, investors can use the company as a vehicle functionally similar to a special purpose acquisition company (SPAC)—raising capital for AI projects without the scrutiny and paperwork required to take a new company public. Woods notes that this is "spec energy" if not technically a SPAC: a preexisting, already-public shell company that can be mobilized for new ventures. The episode does not explore what Allbirds' purported AI services actually entail, only that announcing them triggered massive shareholder gains.

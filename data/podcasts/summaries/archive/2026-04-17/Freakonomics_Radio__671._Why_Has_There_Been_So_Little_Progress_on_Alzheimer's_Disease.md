# Freakonomics Radio — "671. Why Has There Been So Little Progress on Alzheimer's Disease?"

**Host:** Stephen Dubner. **Guests:** Charles Piller (investigative journalist, *Science Magazine*; author of *Doctored: Fraud, Arrogance, and Tragedy in the Quest to Cure Alzheimer's*; formerly at LA Times, Sacramento Bee, STAT); Matthew Schrag (associate professor of neurology, Vanderbilt University Medical Center; independent research integrity consultant; whistleblower on Alzheimer's research misconduct).

The DOJ indicted Wong; Cassava settled with the SEC for $40 million; simufilam failed clinical trials and was withdrawn.
- **Eliezer Masliah oversaw $2.7 billion in research with 132 problematic papers** — as head of the Division of Neuroscience at the National Institute on Aging, Masliah directed ~$2.7 billion per year. **The State of Alzheimer's Research and the Amyloid Hypothesis**

Dubner opens by noting that despite $4 billion in annual NIH funding—up from $1 billion a decade ago—no remedy has emerged that arrests or reverses Alzheimer's cognitive decline. The disease affects more than 7 million people in the US, most over 65, and was formally documented in 1906 when German physician Alois Alzheimer autopsied a patient's brain and found shrinkage, tangles, and peculiar deposits. Scientists have pursued those deposits and tangles ever since.

Schrag explains the dominant model: Alzheimer's involves the accumulation of two proteins that clump together. Beta-amyloid clumps outside neurons (forming plaques), while tau clumps inside, destabilizing the neuron's structure. For roughly 30 years—since the early 1990s—researchers have treated beta-amyloid as the "first domino" in a cascade of events leading to neuronal death. This is known as the amyloid cascade hypothesis. However, Schrag argues that "most diseases at the end of your life are not simple" and doubts that a single factor drives the disease. His own research focuses on blood vessel disease, suggesting the picture is far more complicated than amyloid alone.

**The Lesné Paper and the First Signs of Image Manipulation**

In 2006, a landmark Nature paper by Sylvain Lesné and Karen Ashe proposed that soluble oligomers—clusters of a specific number of beta-amyloid strands—were the primary toxic component, not bulk plaques. This reformulation came at a time when clinical trials targeting bulk amyloid had failed, and the field needed a new direction. Lesné's paper became one of the most cited in Alzheimer's history through 2022.

Piller and Schrag investigated the paper's Western blot images—photographs used to measure protein types and amounts. They found that the images appeared to be manipulated in ways that supported the oligomer hypothesis when the actual data did not. Forensic image experts confirmed patterns consistent with copy-pasting, duplicating, and adjusting image intensity—changes that, while subtle to the untrained eye, altered the underlying quantitative results. Lesné declined to speak with Piller before publication; his co-author Karen Ashe wanted to respond but was advised by her university not to.

The NIH agreed there was a possible problem and referred the case to the Office of Research Integrity at the Department of Health and Human Services, which returned it to the University of Minnesota—the institution with the most to lose from a thorough investigation. In the meantime, Lesné continued collecting hundreds of thousands of dollars in federal funding for similar research. After years of investigation, he resigned as a tenured professor in 2025 without public explanation. The university never released details of its findings; it simply asked journals to consider retracting other Lesné articles. The Nature paper was ultimately retracted, formally requested by co-author Ashe, who acknowledged in a published note that problems with the data had been brought to her attention.

**The Cassava Sciences Case: Wong's Falsified Data**

Schrag's first direct involvement in research misconduct came when attorneys representing whistleblower scientists asked him to investigate simufilam, an experimental Alzheimer's drug developed by Cassava Sciences. The drug was based on research by Hoau-Yan Wong at City University of New York, who claimed simufilam could reverse the misfolding of filamin A and reduce inflammation via beta-amyloid and tau pathways.

The whistleblowers—a cardiologist and neuroscientist—flagged problems in the basic science and, crucially, discovered that Wong's image data appeared retouched. Schrag found systematic patterns: copy-paste effects, duplications, and intensity adjustments that, in aggregate, coherently changed the interpretation of results. He compiled a dossier sent to the FDA and NIH.

In 2024, the Department of Justice indicted Wong on charges of fabricating and falsifying data in NIH grant applications, including major fraud, wire fraud, and false statements. Later that year, Cassava Sciences agreed to pay $40 million to the SEC to settle charges that it misled investors. The company announced simufilam had failed in human clinical trials, showing no cognitive advantage over placebo. Cassava's CEO and senior vice president resigned. In 2025, all DOJ fraud charges against Wong were dropped after new evidence emerged; Wong's lawyers maintain his innocence.

**Eliezer Masliah and Systemic Image Irregularities**

Piller focused on about 50 influential Alzheimer's researchers and found Eliezer Masliah particularly noteworthy. Masliah had been head of the Division of Neuroscience at the National Institute on Aging, overseeing a budget of ~$2.7 billion per year—making him one of the world's most influential figures in neuroscience research direction. He had decades of prestigious work at UC San Diego before joining NIA, and he ran his own lab within the agency.

Working with four forensic image experts—including Schrag, Mu-Yang (Columbia University), Kevin Patrick (independent researcher), and Elizabeth Bik (world-renowned in scientific image analysis)—Piller examined a subset of Masliah's ~800 papers. They found image irregularities in 132 papers, including apparently doctored images, misused or reused images, and images published separately in different papers to support different scientific points. The dossier spanned 30 years of work, from UC San Diego through his tenure at NIA.

Piller presented the 300-page dossier to Masliah and asked a wide range of questions. Masliah did not respond. The NIH initially refused to comment, but 3 days before Piller's story went live in *Science Magazine*, the agency announced that Masliah was no longer in his job. The NIH refused to release details, citing confidentiality around personnel matters.

**The Whistleblower Journey: Schrag's Own Reckoning**

Schrag's path to whistleblower work was unexpected. He began as a postdoctoral researcher and was influenced early by his PhD mentor, Othman Ghribi, who ran the laboratory where Schrag published his first papers on cholesterol-enriched diets and beta-amyloid deposition in rabbits. When Schrag later discovered that the microscope images in those early papers appeared to contain copied-and-pasted neurons, he reached out to Ghribi. Ghribi acknowledged the issue, calling it "exaggeration rather than manipulation"—a distinction Schrag found untenable once he examined the full pattern.

Upon closer review, Schrag found that "just an enormous portion" of Ghribi's work contained image problems. Ghribi had disposed of his original data records, partly, he told Schrag, out of shame. The 2006 rabbit paper and other joint papers were retracted. Schrag encouraged Ghribi to speak publicly about the problems and explain how and why they occurred, but Ghribi declined. The conversation was "very tough" for Schrag, given their deep personal relationship—Ghribi had served on his dissertation committee, attended his graduation, and come to his wedding. An earlier misconduct inquiry in 2006 or 2007 had apparently flagged some of these same problems in a grant review, leading the journal to request original images and publish a correction that was framed as a simple accident. Despite that precedent, systematic issues persisted.

Since 2022, Ghribi has been teaching at the University of Texas Rio Grande Valley. He sent Piller a statement insisting he made no fabrication and that "some polishing could have been done," but took full responsibility as laboratory director. He also claimed he "lost access" to his lab and data after retiring in 2020 and suggested Schrag had "many years to question the pictures."

**Regulatory Capture and the Aducanumab Debacle**

Piller notes that 11 of 16 FDA medical examiners who worked on drug approvals left to become employees or consultants for the companies they had regulated—a phenomenon Public Citizen has termed "regulatory capture." In the Alzheimer's field, Billy Dunn oversaw the FDA approval of aducanumab (Aduhelm), the first anti-amyloid antibody approved. Investigations by the website STAT and a monumental US Congress investigation found Dunn working "hand in glove" with the company promoting the drug. Aducanumab was later withdrawn because it was "dangerous and ineffective." Dunn left the FDA and took a board position at a biotech company developing Alzheimer's treatments.

**The Broader Problem: Institutional Failures and Conflicts of Interest**

Piller emphasizes that the system's failures are structural. Universities, when accused scientists are investigated, have the most to lose and the least to gain from robust inquiry—yet the Office of Research Integrity returns allegations to them anyway. Journals refused to investigate without original data; universities blocked accused scientists from responding to journalists; the NIH's review process is opaque, with minimal transparency or accountability. Researchers who built their careers on the amyloid hypothesis have financial and career incentives to defend it, and funding agencies have directed billions toward that hypothesis without adequately questioning its foundation.

Piller writes that Alzheimer's research has been "hijacked by a cabal of self-interested researchers, government accomplices, and corporate greed." When challenged on whether this is conspiracy theory, he distinguishes between a conspiracy and a system where incentive structures naturally align against contrary evidence. Nearly all leading researchers have built careers, money, and prestige on the amyloid hypothesis; they have no institutional reason to abandon it.

**Anti-Amyloid Drugs and Their Limited Effectiveness**

Two anti-amyloid antibodies are now FDA-approved: drugs that remove amyloid proteins from the brain. Piller and clinicians agree that there is evidence these drugs can "very slightly slow the cognitive decline" associated with Alzheimer's, though many clinicians say the slowing is so subtle as to be imperceptible to patients, families, and even clinicians. "No one's getting better with these drugs," Piller states plainly.

Critics of Piller's work point out that anti-amyloid therapies are showing benefit and cite "tens of independent confirmations" of the approach. Piller responds that amyloid may play a role, but Alzheimer's almost certainly involves many factors and will require multiple approaches in combination. What concerns him is the dominance of anti-amyloid antibodies: they are expensive, they carry risks of brain swelling and bleeding (sometimes fatal), and they do not arrest symptoms. This dominance "soaks up a lot of the mind share, a lot of the ideas behind drug development, and a lot of the big money," potentially starving alternative approaches of resources.

**A New Framework: Waste Clearance and Vascular Health**

Schrag proposes reformulating Alzheimer's not as a single-factor disease but as a "disease of failed waste clearance in the brain." Beta-amyloid plaques and tau tangles are waste products; the problem is their accumulation. If waste management fails—if the brain cannot clear these products through blood vessels or other mechanisms—toxicity builds. This framework can explain why removing one waste product (amyloid) provides minimal benefit: you need a broader approach.

One avenue of promise: blood vessel health. Schrag notes that degeneration of blood vessels may slow waste clearance. Clinical trials have shown that aggressive blood pressure control protects memory, especially early on, before significant cognitive decline—a hint that vascular health is central to brain health. Other approaches to waste clearance—clearing through blood vessels, for instance—merit serious investigation.

Schrag expresses cautious optimism, noting that neurology as a whole has undergone a renaissance. Epilepsy, stroke, and other neurological conditions long deemed untreatable have seen dramatic advances. The absence of similar progress in neurodegenerative diseases is notable and may reflect, in part, the way research integrity issues have narrowed the field's focus and stifled alternative hypotheses.

**The Personal Cost of Whistleblowing**

Schrag reflects that pursuing research integrity work has had consequences: some opportunities lost, some backlash. Yet he has gained the chance to shape important conversations about the field's direction. Before agreeing to go on record with Piller, he sought advice from multiple mentors; none thought it was a good idea. But he proceeded anyway, recognizing the personal cost and the trust required to speak publicly against powerful figures and entrenched interests.

Despite the challenges, Schrag suggests that some people—himself included—are "wired" in a way that makes truth-telling necessary, even at great personal cost. The issue, he emphasizes, is not that amyloid has nothing to do with Alzheimer's; it is that the field has narrowed its vision to one mechanism at the expense of broader, more nuanced understanding. Reformulating the problem and diversifying research approaches is essential for progress.

# Search Engine — "The Cost of War"

**Host:** PJ Vogt. **Guest:** Linda Bilmes (Daniel Patrick Moynihan Senior Lecturer Chair in Public Policy and Public Finance at Harvard Kennedy School; expert on war spending and defense budgeting; co-author of *The $3 Trillion War*).

## TL;DR

- **Two-thirds of Americans don't understand why the U.S. is at war with Iran** — CBS and YouGov polling shows the public cannot articulate a case for the conflict beyond it being "emotionally satisfying for the president."
- **The Pentagon conceals war costs through emergency appropriations and broken accounting** — the Department of Defense cannot account for at least 50% of its assets worldwide; it has flunked its annual audit every year since auditing became mandatory, and reportedly cannot track $2.3 trillion in transactions.
- **First six days of the Iran war cost $11.3 billion — but that's a significant underestimate** — the Pentagon prices munitions at historical inventory cost ($2M for Tomahawks), not replacement cost ($3.5M), making the real week-one bill closer to $15–16 billion, or $1.3 million per minute.
- **The U.S. historically paid for wars through tax increases and budget cuts; after 9-11, that reversed** — Presidents Wilson, Truman, and Johnson raised top marginal tax rates to 92%, while Bush, Trump, and the current administration cut taxes twice during wars, adding all costs to the deficit instead.
- **The Iran war will saddle veterans with 30–40 years of disability costs excluded from the Pentagon's price tag** — if one-third of the 50,000 troops exposed to toxins in the war zone develop respiratory problems (as happened to 37% of first Gulf War troops), the U.S. government faces a "long tail" of medical liabilities never disclosed upfront, adding to a $7.3 trillion backlog of promised-but-not-yet-paid veteran benefits.
- **The Pentagon requested $200 billion additional funding on March 18th** — this confirms that the initial $11.3 billion estimate was drastically low, and inventory depletion (particularly high-altitude interceptors used against cheap Iranian drones) is driving urgent weapons procurement.
- **Warfare has shifted to expensive precision systems versus cheap mass-produced drones, creating an asymmetric cost problem** — the U.S. fires $2–3 million interceptors at Iranian drones that cost $20,000–$30,000, exhausting inventory with no strategic advantage; the U.S. has already depleted 20% of high-altitude interceptor inventory.
- **Wars only end when countries run out of blood or treasure; hiding costs behind debt eliminates the primary deterrent to perpetual war** — as Immanuel Kant argued, "Do not borrow… because it leads to war and prolonging war by making costs invisible."

**The Opacity of American War Spending**

PJ Vogt opens with a confession: he cannot explain why the United States is at war with Iran. While he reads the news and knows Trump wanted to strike Iran's nuclear program and that Netanyahu pushed for action, he admits he doesn't understand the case for the strikes beyond their emotional appeal. Two-thirds of Americans polled by CBS and YouGov report the same confusion.

Rather than chase the reasoning, Vogt decided to focus on something more tangible: the price tag. He turns to Linda Bilmes, whose work over the past two decades has attempted to quantify American war spending. Bilmes explains that the gap between millions, billions, and trillions is so large that the words' similarity in English makes it cognitively difficult to grasp the scale. She offers a metaphor: "a billion seconds ago is like the 1990s when *The Lion King* came out. And trillions is woolly mammoth era."

**How Pentagon Accounting Obscures War Costs**

Bilmes traces her focus on war costs back to the Iraq War. When her students asked how much the 2003 invasion would cost, she discovered the government wasn't really tallying it—or was drastically underestimating. The Bush administration initially quoted "a few billion dollars, maybe 50 billion," though economic advisor Larry Lindsay privately estimated it could cost hundreds of billions (1% of GDP). Lindsay was fired for suggesting this.

The government used two key mechanisms to hide the cost. First, it paid for the war through emergency appropriations rather than the regular budget. Emergency funding is designed for hurricanes and earthquakes—sudden shocks requiring quick disbursement. Ten years into Iraq, when the U.S. had 400 bases and hundreds of thousands of troops, the government still classified war spending as emergency, pretending it was temporary. This allowed Congress to omit the spending from deficit projections and made it appear the deficit was declining even as trillions were spent. It also allowed officials to perpetually claim the war was "about to turn a corner," because the budget showed no long-term commitment.

Second, the Pentagon simply doesn't track its money well. The Department of Defense flunked its annual audit every year since auditing became mandatory. It cannot account for at least 50% of its assets worldwide. The Government Accountability Office ranks Pentagon financial accountability among the top risks to government security, year after year. Bilmes notes that a private company with such accounting failures would face "extreme penalties." The day before 9-11, then-Secretary Donald Rumsfeld warned that the Pentagon couldn't account for where taxpayer money went. Twenty-five years later, that problem persists.

**The Historical Shift: From Paying as You Go to Deficit Spending**

Bilmes emphasizes a crucial historical break. From the War of 1812 through Vietnam, the U.S. paid for wars through a combination of tax increases and domestic budget cuts. Presidents Wilson (raising corporate tax to 12%), Truman (raising top marginal rates to 92%), and Johnson (imposing a 75% surcharge) all sought taxes to cover war costs. This forced Congress's fiscal committees to hold extensive hearings and grapple seriously with how to fund conflicts.

After 9-11, this reversed. The Bush administration cut taxes in 2001 and 2003 while waging two wars. Trump cut taxes during his first term in 2017, and the current administration cut taxes again, all while war spending climbed. Now, everything is added to the national debt.

Three factors drove this shift. First, congressional budget dysfunction made it convenient to use emergency appropriations rather than negotiate the regular budget. Second, historically low interest rates for two decades meant borrowing felt painless. When Iraq began, the national debt was under $4 trillion; now it's $31 trillion, and interest payments have doubled from 6–7% of the budget to 15%. Third, the Pentagon benefited internally: emergency funding gave it more discretion and was less vulnerable to government shutdowns that affect contractor payments.

**Tallying Week One of the Iran War**

The Pentagon announced the first six days of Operation Epic Fury cost $11.3 billion—approximately $1.3 million per minute. Bilmes immediately flagged this as an underestimate. The Pentagon prices munitions at historical inventory cost (Tomahawks listed at $2 million, Patriot missiles at $1–2 million), not current replacement cost (Tomahawks cost $3.5 million to replace, newer interceptors $4 million). This methodology is analogous to totaling a car accident loss at the car's original purchase price, not the cost of a new car. The real week-one bill is closer to $15–16 billion.

The largest component of short-term operating costs is the war machine itself: running a carrier strike group costs $10 million per day during peacetime and more during active war. A single E-3 airborne surveillance system damaged by an Iranian drone costs $700 million. Then there are munitions—which get fired and deplete inventory—and the aircraft and depreciation for all platforms involved. Labor includes combat pay for 50,000 troops and support personnel.

**The Long Tail of Veteran Costs**

Medium and long-term costs are even larger but invisible in the Pentagon's initial accounting. In the first two weeks, 50,000 U.S. troops were exposed to toxins and contaminants. By historical precedent, roughly one-third will likely develop some form of respiratory or other health problem, qualifying them for lifetime disability benefits and medical care. The government currently owes $7.3 trillion in disability benefits to living veterans (mostly from the Gulf War, Iraq, and Afghanistan). These liabilities were promised but have never been paid; they hang as an accrued debt.

The Iraq and Afghanistan wars have also burdened the Pentagon with hidden infrastructure costs. The world's most expensive embassy, located in Baghdad, sustained damage. Facilities in allied countries require repair. The Ras Lafan liquefied gas facility in Qatar, damaged in the conflict, will cost $26 billion to repair. The Trump administration opened a $20 billion reinsurance facility to backstop shipping insurance after Iran closed the Strait of Hormuz, threatening 20% of global oil consumption.

**Inventory Depletion and Asymmetric Warfare**

A critical issue emerged: the U.S. has already exhausted 20% of its high-altitude interceptor inventory from last year's Operation Midnight Hammer. The Iran war has further depleted these stocks. The problem is asymmetric: Iran produces drones for $20,000–$30,000; the U.S. fires interceptors costing $2–3 million to stop them. This is "highly asymmetric," Bilmes notes, showing why Iran was able to "severely damage us economically" even without matching human casualties. The Pentagon requested $200 billion more in emergency funding on March 18th to replenish inventory, confirming that the initial week-one estimate was drastically understated.

Bilmes attributes the asymmetry to a shift in warfare itself. Modern conflicts now feature advanced, precision-driven, AI-guided munitions exchanged between largely unmanned systems. The U.S. has invested in expensive, extremely capable weapons; Iran has responded with large volumes of cheap, mass-produced drones. The U.S. lacks a one-size-fits-most response and instead depletes premium inventory on cheap threats.

**The Political Economics of Invisible Costs**

Trump promised the war would be "a four to five week deal." It lasted 38 days and never felt quick. When Iran retaliated and closed the Strait of Hormuz, oil prices rose, gas prices climbed, and the public felt the cost directly—the one metric all Americans notice. Trump, sensitive to public opinion, began making apocalyptic threats online, and even loyal media figures like Tucker Carlson and Alex Jones publicly opposed continuing the war. A two-week ceasefire was announced on April 7th, though contradictions between U.S. and Iranian statements left its durability unclear.

Bilmes argues that historically, wars end for pragmatic reasons, not moral ones: countries run out of blood or treasure. If war costs are hidden behind borrowing and deficit spending, the primary deterrent to perpetual war—economic pain—vanishes. She cites Kant's fourth principle for peace: "Do not borrow… because it leads to war and prolonging war by making costs invisible."

This suppression is bipartisan. Biden fought hard for Ukraine aid but released half the Strategic Petroleum Reserve to keep gas prices from rising—preventing the public from feeling any cost. Historically, presidents would have asked the public to bear a modest increase for a just cause; the current political consensus treats visible costs as electoral poison.

Her minimum estimate for what the Iran war will cost: $1 trillion. That figure could fund free school lunches for every K–12 student in America for over 40 years, or purchase every sports franchise in the NFL, NBA, MLB, and NHL combined, with $500 billion remaining to fund NASA for two decades.

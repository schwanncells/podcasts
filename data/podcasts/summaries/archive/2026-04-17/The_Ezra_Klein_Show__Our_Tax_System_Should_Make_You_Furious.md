# The Ezra Klein Show — "Our Tax System Should Make You Furious"

**Host:** Ezra Klein. **Guest:** Ray Matto (professor of tax law and estate planning, Boston College Law School; author of *The Second Estate: How the Tax Code Made an American Aristocracy*).

## TL;DR

- **2021 ProPublica investigation revealed ultra-wealthy paying near-zero taxes** — Warren Buffett's true tax rate was 0.1%, Jeff Bezos 0.98%, Michael Bloomberg 1.3%, based on leaked IRS documents showing the disconnect between wealth and actual tax liability.
- **Billionaires avoid income tax by taking $82,000 salaries and never selling stock** — Jeff Bezos and Elon Musk use unrealized gains as their primary wealth, borrowing against stock at favorable rates to fund lifestyles while the stock itself remains untaxed, a strategy available only with extreme wealth ($100+ billion).
- **Estate tax has been gutted from 122,000 filings in 2000 to just 2,584 taxable returns in 2021** — loopholes including dynasty trusts, minority discounts, and GRATs allow the ultra-wealthy to pass billions tax-free to heirs, despite the 40% statutory rate supposedly designed to prevent dynastic wealth.
- **The richest 1% control 31.7–32% of US wealth but the estate tax raised only $30 billion in 2024** — this amount is "practically nothing," a single day's worth of Elon Musk's trading gains, showing the tax system has failed at its core purpose.
- **Stock buybacks replaced dividends as the primary way companies distribute profits, enabling the wealthy to avoid taxation** — before 1982, over 70% of profits went to dividends (taxed at high rates); now less than 20%, allowing shareholders to profit through stock price appreciation instead of taxable income.
- **"Step-up in basis" (the "angel of death" loophole) erases all capital gains when stock is inherited** — someone can buy Nvidia at $100 and sell at $30 million, paying capital gains tax, but if they hold until death and pass it to heirs, the heirs are treated as if they bought it at $30 million and can sell immediately with zero tax.
- **Wealth taxes face constitutional and practical obstacles; the real solution is taxing unrealized gains at death or transfer** — federal wealth taxes are likely unconstitutional under the current Supreme Court; the solution Matto proposes is to tax gains when assets change hands (not just at sale), matching Canada's system and eliminating the estate tax entirely.
- **The 1986 Tax Reform Act shows meaningful reform is possible** — Reagan-era lawmakers shut down the tax shelter industry for high-income earners, proving that even politically powerful groups can be brought back into the tax system with sufficient political will.

**How the Wealthy Avoid Income Taxes**

Normal Americans pay income taxes at rates up to 37% plus payroll taxes as high as 15.3%, for a combined burden of roughly 50% on high earners. However, the ultra-wealthy use a fundamentally different strategy. Matto explains that billionaires like Bezos and Musk cap their salaries at minimal levels—Bezos has taken exactly $82,000 annually for over 20 years—and instead derive their wealth from the growing value of company stock. The critical insight is that under current US tax law, unrealized gains on stock are never taxed. So long as they do not sell, there is no "taxable event," and therefore no tax obligation. This allows them to accumulate enormous wealth entirely tax-free.

To fund their lifestyles without triggering capital gains taxes, the ultra-wealthy simply borrow against their stock holdings. Matto notes that private lenders eagerly offer favorable rates for loans collateralized by assets of billionaires, making them nearly risk-free from the lender's perspective. The borrower receives cash tax-free and repays only the interest annually. Because wealth of this magnitude generates interest costs that are minuscule relative to their assets, they can sustain this indefinitely. Bezos or Musk need only a few billion dollars to support a "lavish lifestyle"—the cost of private planes, yachts, and multiple homes is literally pocket change compared to stock holdings in the hundreds of billions. The strategy is viable only because consumption does not scale with wealth; once you have $100 billion, spending an additional $100 million makes virtually no dent on your collateral position.

**The ProPublica Investigation and the Reality of Tax Avoidance**

For decades, tax scholars could describe these mechanisms theoretically, but it remained disputed whether billionaires actually used them. That changed in 2021 when Charles Little John, an IRS contractor, leaked confidential tax returns to ProPublica. The leaked documents confirmed that the ultra-wealthy were indeed paying near-zero taxes in practice. The investigation documented that Warren Buffett had a true tax rate of 0.1%, Jeff Bezos 0.98%, and Michael Bloomberg 1.3%. Matto emphasizes that this information's value lay in destroying the "it's just theoretical" defense; tax avoidance was no longer conjecture but documented fact from actual tax filings. Little John faced a severe prison sentence for the leak, illustrating the determination of the powerful to keep this information hidden.

**The Misleading "Top 1% Pay 40%" Statistic**

Matto addresses a common counter-argument: the claim that "the top 1% pay 40% of income taxes," which sounds progressive. The problem, she explains, is that this statistic refers to the top 1% of *income earners*—high-earning doctors, lawyers, and finance professionals. It does not refer to the wealthiest Americans by *wealth*. The billionaires are equally likely to fall into the 40% of Americans who pay zero federal income tax as they are to be among the top 1% paying significant taxes. The distinction matters because the richest Americans have decoupled their wealth from income, while high-earning professionals remain tethered to salaries and therefore to income tax.

Similarly, Matto challenges the claim that 40% of Americans pay "no taxes" by noting that 80% of Americans pay more in payroll taxes than income taxes. Payroll taxes begin at dollar one of earnings and are significantly burdensome, even for lower-income workers. The non-payers statistic is therefore misleading on both ends.

**Estate Tax Decay and the Rise of Loopholes**

The estate tax was designed as a backstop to the income tax system, intended to ensure that wealth transfers at death would be subject to significant tax. Today, the statutory rate is 40% on transfers above $15 million. However, the estate tax has been riddled with loopholes that have rendered it largely ineffective. 

Matto notes that the decay began in the early 1990s with an aggressive campaign funded by 18 of the country's wealthiest families—the Waltons, Kochs, Mars family, and others—to turn public opinion against the "death tax" by framing it as an ordinary tax affecting family farms and small businesses. The campaign, though successful at reducing the tax's political legitimacy, was built on exaggeration. Congress had provisions explicitly protecting family farms; the campaign's poster child, Chester Thigpen, turned out not to be subject to the estate tax at all upon his death.

Modern estate planning relies on several key loopholes. **Minority discounts** allow people to split a business or assets into minority ownership pieces, each of which can be valued at a 30–40% discount (because minority stakes lack control), then consolidated back to full value in the next generation. A $100 million business can be "shrunk" to $50–60 million on paper through this technique, with the full value later appearing to the heirs. **Dynasty trusts** explicitly circumvent the purpose of the estate tax by creating vehicles that benefit children, grandchildren, and descendants in perpetuity, funded through complex arrangements. **GRATs** (grantor retained annuity trusts) and "rolling GRATs" allow people to transfer enormous wealth tax-free by using carefully structured pricing. **Charitable vehicles** like 501(c)(4)s offer tax deductions while allowing the donor to retain control and influence over assets. All of these devices have existed in the tax code for years, but estate planners have had free rein to use and abuse them because Congress has not meaningfully updated the estate tax since 1990.

The effectiveness of these loopholes is evident in the numbers. In 2000, before the Bush tax cuts, 122,000 Americans filed estate tax returns. By 2010 this had fallen to 47,000. By 2013, after Obama's tax plan, it was 32,300. By 2021, after Trump's changes, only 6,158 estate tax returns were filed, and of those, only 2,584 were actually taxable. Matto summarizes: "Either rich people stopped dying, or they stopped being rich, or we gutted this thing."

**Stock Buybacks, Dividends, and the Elimination of Taxable Income**

Before 1982, companies distributed over 70% of their profits to shareholders as taxable dividends, which were taxed at the highest rates, matching wage income. In 1982, a rule change allowed companies to buy back their own stock. The shift has been dramatic: today, less than 20% of profits are distributed as dividends; the rest are retained or used for buybacks. Stock buybacks artificially inflate share prices, allowing shareholders to profit from appreciation rather than from taxable dividends.

This change had cascading effects. The Dow Jones Industrial Average was stagnant from the 1920s through the early 1980s, hovering around 3,000 in inflation-adjusted terms. After 1982, it has soared to 45,000, a hockey-stick curve driven partly by buybacks. For shareholders, the benefit is that they can now enjoy corporate profits through untaxed stock appreciation rather than taxed dividends. Tax-exempt organizations (pension funds, endowments) can hold stock and reinvest proceeds without paying taxes. The federal government has therefore lost massive revenue that once came from dividend taxation.

**The "Angel of Death" Loophole: Step-Up in Basis**

One of the most significant tax benefits for inherited wealth is the step-up in basis. If someone purchases Nvidia stock at $100 and it grows to $30 million, and they sell while living, they must pay capital gains tax on the $29.999 million gain (at roughly 23–24% combined rate, totaling ~$7 million). However, if they hold the stock until death and pass it to an heir, the heir is treated as if they had purchased the stock at $30 million—the fair market value on the date of death. The heir can immediately sell for $30 million and owe zero capital gains tax. The $29.999 million gain simply vanishes. Matto calls this the "angel of death" loophole because it erases all accumulated gains in a single stroke at inheritance.

This creates a perverse incentive: hold until death, and gains are forgiven. Sell while living, and pay significant tax. The system penalizes realization, encouraging wealth concentration across generations. Matto notes that this is not a design feature but an anomaly within the broader tax code.

**Wealth Tax Proposals: California and the Federal Alternative**

California is proposing a one-time 5% wealth tax on ultra-high-net-worth individuals. Matto sees merit in the concept but identifies critical flaws. First, a one-time tax signals that it could happen again, prompting wealthy residents to relocate to lower-tax states like Florida. Second, and more fundamentally, states face a valuation problem: it is easy to value publicly traded stock, but much harder to value art collections, partnership interests, private equity holdings, cryptocurrency, or complex financial instruments. A state tax authority lacks the resources to continuously monitor and verify valuations for thousands of ultra-wealthy taxpayers. The practical burden of administration is immense.

At the federal level, a wealth tax avoids the migration problem (leaving the US is difficult and culturally fraught), but runs into a constitutional obstacle. The US Constitution limits direct taxes on wealth, and the current Supreme Court has shown willingness to strike down taxes it deems unconstitutional. There is significant risk that a federal wealth tax would be invalidated, wasting political capital.

Matto therefore advocates for a different path: **taxing unrealized gains at the point of transfer**, not accumulation. Canada uses a broader capital gains tax system in which gains are taxed whenever property changes hands—not just at sale, but also at gift or death. The person who earned the gain pays tax on it, rather than the tax being deferred indefinitely. Matto's preferred approach would be to eliminate the capital gains preference entirely, treating all gains as ordinary income with an inflation adjustment, and to tax gains upon any transfer, including inheritance. Additionally, the estate tax should be eliminated (since it is not functioning) and replaced with an inheritance tax that applies when beneficiaries receive property.

**Political History and the Possibility of Reform**

Matto emphasizes that meaningful tax reform is not impossible, despite the political power of the wealthy. The 1986 Tax Reform Act under President Reagan is the paradigmatic example. Prior to 1986, high-income earners had access to tax shelters that allowed them to offset nearly all of their income tax liability, despite earning millions. The 1986 act broadened the tax base by eliminating these shelters, and the effect has persisted: high-income professionals today have very few avenues to avoid taxation. The shelters vanished and have not re-emerged, suggesting that once loopholes are closed, they can stay closed.

Matto is optimistic that conditions now favor reform. The public is increasingly aware of wealth concentration (the richest 1% now controls 31.7–32% of all US wealth, levels not seen since the 1920s), and the ultra-wealthy are less inclined to present themselves as benevolent. She notes that in the early 2000s, there was a book titled *Philanthroeconomics: How the Rich Can Save the World*, a title now absurd to contemporary ears. The narrative that the wealthy should be trusted to address societal problems has collapsed.

**The Broader Fairness Question**

Klein raises a philosophical point: why do we tax different forms of income differently? Andrew Mellon, Treasury Secretary and anti-tax crusader, argued in 1924 that wages should be taxed lightly and investments heavily, precisely because wage income is uncertain and limited in duration while investment income is stable and perpetual. That logic was inverted over the decades. Today, capital gains are taxed at roughly 20%, far below the 37% top rate on wages and salaries. Matto agrees this is fundamentally unfair and inefficient; the current system actively discourages work and incentivizes wealth accumulation, the opposite of what Mellon believed sound policy should do.

She also raises a secondary concern: the corrosive effect on social solidarity. When high-income professionals see that billionaires pay near-zero taxes while they pay 40–50%, they feel like suckers, undermining social willingness to support the tax system itself. This sentiment can lead to pressure for lower taxes across the board, benefiting only the already wealthy.

**On National Debt and the Imperative for Reform**

Matto concludes by tying tax reform to fiscal necessity. The US national debt is so large that interest payments are now the third-largest federal expense, behind only Social Security and Medicare, exceeding military spending. Interest costs topped $1 trillion in 2024. This is unsustainable. The only pathway forward is to bring the ultra-wealthy and high-wealth households back into the tax system, expanding the revenue base. This is not merely a moral imperative but a financial one.

**Book Recommendations**

Matto recommends three books: *The Age of Extraction* by Tim Wu, on how corporations have shifted from creating wealth to extracting it; *The Rise and Fall of the Neoliberal Order* by Gary Gersell, on the swing from government-led to market-led ideology; and *Crossroads* by Jonathan Franzen, a novel exploring psychological complexity as a counterweight to contemporary news cycles.

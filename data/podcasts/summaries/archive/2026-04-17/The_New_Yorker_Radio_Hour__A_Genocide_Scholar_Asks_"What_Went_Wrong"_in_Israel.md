# The New Yorker Radio Hour — "A Genocide Scholar Asks 'What Went Wrong' in Israel"

**Host:** David Remnick. **Guest:** Omer Bartov (Israeli historian; professor of Holocaust and Genocide Studies at Brown University; author of *Israel, What Went Wrong*; born in 1954; scholar of World War II and the Holocaust).

## TL;DR

- **Genocide intent and implementation: Bartov argues Israel has met the UN definition by May 2024**, after October 7th statements ("They shall have no water. They shall have no food.") paired with military operations destroying Gaza and displacing its population with no refuge.
- **October 7th was a war crime, not genocide; by May 2024 pattern shifted** — about 10,000 Palestinians killed by November 2023, but Bartov saw the turning point when the IDF moved into Rafah (where a million Palestinians had been displaced), flattened the city, and relocated survivors to coastal areas with no infrastructure.
- **"Ethnic cleansing, which was what the Israeli government wanted to carry out, became genocide"** — because unlike the 1948 Nakba when borders were open for Palestinians to flee to Lebanon, Jordan, Syria, in 2024 Israel and Egypt closed borders, trapping the population.
- **Israel could have been stopped by U.S. pressure in November or December 2023**, Bartov argues — had Biden told Netanyahu to wrap up operations in two weeks or face withdrawal of arms and diplomatic support, war crimes might have been contained; Biden did not act.
- **Zionism as a state ideology is "not reformable" — but the state of Israel is**, though it requires dismantling the ethno-nationalist principle that has intensified; Bartov traces the shift from 1990s hopes of "a country of all its citizens" to current messianism and militarism.
- **Military service and the First Intifada were Bartov's political awakening** — in 1973's Yom Kippur War he felt the occupation as occupier; in 1988 when ordered to "break bones" of Palestinians, he wrote Defense Minister Rabin comparing IDF trajectory to the Wehrmacht, prompting a formal rebuke.
- **About 200,000 Israelis have left since October 7th, mostly for economic reasons and religious school changes**, draining the country of educated citizens and raising fears of brain drain; Bartov himself does not intend to return "at least until the elections."
- **60% of Americans now hold negative views of Israel**, and university teaching on Israel-Palestine has been chilled by fear of anti-Semitism accusations, yet demand from students — Jewish, Muslim, Palestinian, and American — is high; Bartov refuses to be silenced.

**Bartov's Political Evolution: From Unquestioning Soldier to Critic**

Omer Bartov was raised into mainstream Zionism without question. Born in 1954, he considered his existence in Israel self-evident — he was Israeli, not so much "Jewish" in his own mind. Mandatory military service in the 1973 Yom Kippur War marked a turning point. While serving as an officer, he patrolled occupied Arish in Egypt and later served in Gaza and the West Bank. The experience of occupying another people's home unsettled him, though he didn't yet have a fully political framework for his discomfort. The real awakening came in 1987–1988 with the First Intifada. When Defense Minister Yitzhak Rabin ordered troops to "break bones" of Palestinian protesters, Bartov was outraged. He scribbled a note on a postcard describing a Palestinian child's death, comparing Rabin's orders to the Wehrmacht's descent into brutality. To his shock, Rabin's office sent a formal one-line response: "How dare you compare the Wehrmacht to the IDF?" Bartov believes Rabin was already thinking along these lines but was surprised to be called out. This episode crystallized Bartov's conviction that Israel was on a dangerous path.

**From Israel to Academia Abroad**

Unwilling to serve in the suppression of the Intifada and uncomfortable with Tel Aviv University's political atmosphere, Bartov accepted a fellowship at Harvard in the late 1980s. What began as a three-year fellowship became permanent exile. He eventually settled at Brown University. Politically, Bartov felt increasingly alienated from Israel's trajectory of settlement expansion in the West Bank and Gaza. He did not return to live in Israel, and by December 2024 had not visited for over a year. His continued outspoken criticism — warnings of genocide, comparisons to historical atrocities — has prompted hundreds of emails to his administration calling for his firing and labeling him a Hamas agent and self-hating Jew. Bartov neither expects nor desires a government change to bring him back, nor does he believe an Israeli government led by center-left opposition figures like Yair Golan, Gideon Sa'ar, or Yair Lapid has articulated any vision for Palestinian rights.

**The UN Definition of Genocide and Its Application to Gaza**

Bartov relies on the 1948 UN Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide as the only relevant definition. It requires proof of intent to destroy a national, ethnic, racial, or religious group "in whole or in part, as such," and proof that this intent is being implemented. In November 2023, only a month after October 7th, Bartov warned in the *New York Times* that while genocide had not yet occurred, the conditions and rhetoric pointed toward it. He noted that Israeli political and military leaders were making genocidal statements ("They shall have no water. They shall have no food. They are human animals"), and over 300,000 conscripted reservists were being exposed to incitement. Hamas's killing of about 800 civilians on October 7th and taking of 251 hostages was unquestionably a war crime and potential crime against humanity, Bartov says, but it was not genocide. The U.S. could have intervened then. Had Biden told Netanyahu in November or December 2023 to cease operations within two weeks or face withdrawal of arms and diplomatic protection, the result might have been "war crimes, terrible destruction," but perhaps not genocide.

**The May 2024 Rafa Operation and the Turning Point**

By May 2024, Bartov concluded genocide was being actively implemented. The turning point was the Israeli military's offensive in Rafa, a southern Gaza city that had held a quarter-million people before the war. By May, it held a million — Palestinians displaced by IDF orders to evacuate "for their own safety" before neighborhoods became operational zones. The IDF later demolished these abandoned homes. The Biden administration warned Israel not to enter Rafa, fearing mass casualties. The IDF responded that they would handle it and proceeded to move the population to Al-Mawasi, a coastal area with no humanitarian infrastructure whatsoever, then flattened the city. By August, Rafa no longer existed. This followed the official logic of destroying Hamas while releasing hostages — a contradiction that failed on both counts. Most hostages who returned did so through negotiated exchanges with Hamas, not military operations. Gaza was destroyed, but Hamas survived. In the process, Bartov argues, the pattern of operations — ethnic cleansing with no exit — became genocide by definition.

**Ethnic Cleansing and the Absence of Borders**

The crucial distinction Bartov draws is intent and possibility. Israeli government statements and military actions, he argues, suggest the goal was not to kill all Palestinians but to remove them from Gaza. The historical parallel is the 1948 Nakba, when Palestinians fled to Lebanon, Jordan, and Syria — the borders were open. In 2024, Israel closed its borders, Egypt closed its borders, and there was nowhere to go. "Ethnic cleansing, which was what the Israeli government wanted to carry out, became genocide" because the conditions that would permit displacement ceased to exist. When asked if Netanyahu would argue that Hamas could have ended the war by releasing hostages, Bartov counters: Hamas offered exactly that — prisoner exchanges in return for hostage releases. The military strategy of destroying Hamas through destroying Gaza failed; Hamas remains. The population is trapped, displaced, living in tents without infrastructure. Bartov describes the plan for Gaza's future as creating "a kind of resort town for the rich" with Palestinians as "water carriers" — cleaners, dish washers — living in humanitarian zones "akin to concentration camps."

**Contrasting Intent: U.S. Firebombing and Israeli Operations**

A common pushback Bartov encounters is that the U.S. firebombed Dresden and Tokyo in World War II, killed roughly 600,000 German civilians intentionally, and more recently conducted brutal operations against ISIS and in Vietnam, yet these are not labeled genocide. Bartov's answer hinges on intent and reconstruction. The U.S. firebombing was intended to demoralize the civilian population, not to destroy them as a group (though the consequences were catastrophic and the strategy failed). When the U.S. occupied Germany, it implemented the Marshall Plan — invested in rebuilding, reconstructing. Israel's visible plan for Gaza is the opposite: a population confined to less than half the original territory, unhoused, living in tents with no infrastructure, "living there like dogs," as Bartov bluntly states, with no outside power doing anything to change it. The difference lies not merely in casualty numbers but in the intent embedded in post-conflict policy.

**Zionism's Evolution from Ideology to "Insupportable Extremism"**

Bartov argues that Israel went wrong in 1948 when it failed to adopt a constitution and allowed Zionism to become the state ideology. Zionism itself, he contends, is "not reformable" because it is fundamentally an ethno-nationalist ideology that has only intensified into militarism, racism, and genocide. However, the state of Israel itself can be reinvented — but only if it abandons the ethno-national principle and becomes "a country of all its citizens," the slogan of the 1990s when there was genuine hope for accommodation with Palestinian nationalism. Bartov knew many of the "new historians" of the 1980s and 1990s — Ilan Pappé (an old friend from Oxford), Benny Morris (before he moved rightward) — who believed a two-state solution was achievable. That moment culminated with Yitzhak Rabin's 1995 assassination by a right-wing extremist. Bartov was holding his six-month-old daughter when he heard; he wept, not because he liked Rabin, but because "he was the last hope." Over a generation has passed since, and things have only moved in the wrong direction. What ended the window was the rise of messianism, settler ideology, and the religious national right, which now controls the political apparatus.

**Teaching in the Shadow of Accusations**

Bartov teaches a course at Brown on the Holocaust and the Nakba, drawing Muslim, Jewish, Palestinian, Pakistani, and American students. Initially uncomfortable with each other, they become engaged as they encounter readings and knowledge. Bartov values this as the essence of university education — open inquiry. However, he observes that university administrations are "cowering," afraid to teach Israel-Palestine. Faculty fear accusations of anti-Semitism, which Bartov dismisses as largely invented by Israel's supporters. What is real and rising, he warns, is actual anti-Semitism from figures like Tucker Carlson and the MAGA movement, who frame Israel as exerting Jewish control over American foreign policy — a classic anti-Semitic trope. Despite institutional fear, student demand for his course is high; many students feel a need to understand the conflict and find little else on offer.

**The Brain Drain and American Public Opinion**

Roughly 200,000 Israelis have left since October 7th, a huge number for a country of less than ten million. While some cite political reasons, many cite economic collapse, the declining value of the shekel, and schools becoming increasingly religious while secular families see no future for their children. These emigrants are among the best-trained and best-educated segments of Israeli society, intensifying the brain drain that Netanyahu's government has exacerbated. On the American side, 60% of Americans now hold negative views of Israel according to Pew Research — a striking shift that "says something quite good about American society," in Bartov's view, showing responsiveness to ground realities. Criticism is growing on both the American left and right, though Bartov warns that some right-wing criticism emerges from anti-Semitic motives framed as opposition to Jewish influence. For Israel, reduced dependence on U.S. military and financial support might force a reckoning with its own actions. For American society and American Jewry, the rise of genuine far-right anti-Semitism represents a grave danger distinct from, and more sinister than, left-wing criticism of Israeli policy.

**The Path Forward Requires External Pressure**

Bartov concludes that Israel and Palestinians lack the internal dynamic to move forward on their own. There are creative, hopeful, and wonderful people on both sides, but they cannot rise to the top without external pressure — what Bartov calls "shock therapy." Israel has not yet identified the limits of its own power because those limits are set in Washington, D.C. Only when the U.S. sets boundaries — through arms restrictions, economic pressure, or diplomatic isolation — will Israeli forces generating new thinking gain traction. Netanyahu's removal alone will not suffice; center-left opposition figures have offered no alternative vision for Palestinians. But if external pressure arrives, internal change may follow.

# Radiolab — "The Resistance of a Cow"

**Host:** Latif Nasser (with Lulu Miller). **Guests:** Klara Grunel (Danish journalist, Zetland audio journalism company); Doug Reinemann (professor of biological systems engineering, University of Wisconsin–Madison; dairy electrician and stray voltage expert); Richard Hirsch (professor of history of science and technology, Virginia Tech); David Nye (professor, Denmark; energy and electricity historian); Larry Neubauer (dairy electrician; investigated approximately 4,000–5,000 stray voltage cases); Dr. Don Sanders (veterinarian; 50 years of practice); Nigel Cook (associate professor, School of Veterinary Medicine, University of Wisconsin–Madison); Richard Norell (PhD dairy science, Idaho; conducted 2016 cow resistance study).

## TL;DR

- **Danish cows refuse to drink water, drink urine instead.** A farmer named Gregus noticed his 200 cows refusing the water trough but drinking each other's urine for months—a phenomenon that also appeared in dozens of other Danish farms and hundreds across the United States near power lines or substations.
- **"Stray voltage" theory: electricity from power lines harms cows.** Farmers blamed high-voltage transmission lines and power stations for leaking electricity into their farms, poisoning water and shocking animals; lawsuits in Wisconsin in the 1980s–90s resulted in million-dollar payouts and led to the creation of stray voltage task forces.
- **Cows have a standardized electrical resistance of 500 ohms.** Researchers at University of Wisconsin–Madison established that 500 ohms was the threshold below which a cow would exhibit behavioral changes when exposed to stray current—used as the regulatory baseline for acceptable farm voltage.
- **A contested 2016 Idaho study found 200-ohm cows in modern free-stall barns.** When researchers measured resistance in wet, crowded free-stall dairy farms, they found cows had half the resistance (200 ohms) of the 500-ohm standard—meaning they would be harmed by lower voltage—but Idaho Power refused to fund or support the research.
- **The real culprit: mineral deficiency and barn modernization stress.** Veterinarian Don Sanders explained cows drink urine when lacking potassium or sodium; Nigel Cook noted that high-performance dairy breeds in modern free-stall barns are sensitive to dietary imbalances and overcrowding, not necessarily electricity.
- **Less than 3% of Wisconsin farms tested for stray voltage exceeded the regulatory threshold** — only 9,000+ investigations since 1990 found below-threshold electricity, yet farmers continued to blame power companies rather than management or nutrition.
- **Power companies and regulators resist changing the 500-ohm standard despite new data.** The lost Idaho dataset (a key binder), peer-reviewed studies favoring 500 ohms, and utility incentives to deny stray voltage create a deadlock that helps utilities avoid liability while dairy farmers struggle to diagnose actual problems.
- **Future demand for electricity from AI data centers may force the issue.** As the grid expands with new 500,000-volt transmission lines to power energy-hungry tech infrastructure, farms will face increased voltage exposure, potentially vindicating farmers' concerns and forcing a recalibration of safety standards.

**The Mystery on Gregus's Farm**

A Danish journalist named Klara Grunel published a story headlined "A mystery about the water on Danish farms the cows refuse to drink," which prompted her and colleague Fredrik to visit Gregus Christensen's farm outside Copenhagen. Gregus, a dairy farmer in his early 40s, told them he was "about to sell all of my cows. This is my life's work." At the barn, they encountered roughly 200 reddish cows standing around in the space. When the researchers approached the water trough, they saw an extraordinary sight: the cows sniffed the water but would not touch it. Instead, when one cow urinated, the others immediately crowded around, positioning themselves to catch the urine mid-stream as it fell like a fountain. If a cow was not urinating, another would lick its behind to stimulate it. Gregus confirmed this behavior had been happening for months. When Clara asked if this was normal, he replied, "I've never seen cows do this before." Water tests showed the trough was clean and uncontaminated.

**A Pattern Emerges Across Denmark and America**

Klara's investigation revealed this was not isolated. She found another farmer in Denmark, then another—a pattern of cows refusing tap water and drinking urine at farms located near power lines or substations. Her search extended to the United States, where she discovered Jill Nelson, a fifth-generation dairy farmer in southwest Minnesota whose family had owned the farm since 1884. Jill recalled noticing her cows becoming reluctant to enter the milking parlor around 2008, fidgeting around the entrance. They stopped drinking water normally and instead walked to puddles of urine and drank them dry. Jill mentioned hearing about "stray voltage" from Wisconsin customers who had experienced similar problems. When Matt Kielty traveled to a county fair in Wisconsin, he asked dairy farmers if they had dealt with stray voltage. Nearly every single one said yes. One dairy electrician named Larry Neubauer reported he had worked on approximately 4,000 to 5,000 stray voltage cases over his career. Cases were reported in New York, Pennsylvania, Vermont, and Idaho—patterns of electricity allegedly leaking from underground cables, finding paths of least resistance through concrete with rebar and water, and entering the cows.

**The Rise of Electricity and the Power Line Protests**

To understand stray voltage claims, the episode traced the history of electricity in America. Electricity did not begin with Ben Franklin but took off around 1800 when scientists developed batteries and generators. The telegraph sent messages nearly at the speed of light; the telephone followed in 1876; and Edison's light bulb, arriving in the 1870s, was revolutionary because it separated light from fire—people could hold a light bulb in their hand, turn it upside down, even smash it without danger. The Pearl Street Station in lower Manhattan, opening in 1882, became the world's first large-scale power plant. Electricity spread rapidly from New York to cities across America, then outward to rural areas and farms. By the 1960s, the entire country depended on electrical power. As cities demanded more electricity, power companies built massive new generating plants—oil, gas, coal, and nuclear—and ran massive high-voltage transmission towers through farmland. In the 1970s, western Minnesota farmers vehemently opposed these power lines. They toppled towers, shot out components, and filed lawsuits. Farmers like John Tripp questioned why the state allowed power lines over his fields and cows but not over wildlife preserves or school bus stops. They showed evidence of fluorescent bulbs lighting up beneath high-voltage transmission lines due to electric field intensity.

**Stray Voltage Lawsuits and the Wisconsin Task Force**

Following the power line construction of the 1970s, Wisconsin dairy farmers began reporting sick cows that refused to drink water. They sued power companies, arguing that electricity was leaking from the lines into their farms. The lawsuits were successful—juries awarded million-dollar payouts for alleged losses in milk productivity. These cases prompted Wisconsin's Department of Agriculture to create a stray voltage task force in 1986. The state brought in Doug Reinemann, a professor of biological systems engineering at University of Wisconsin–Madison. Reinemann began by reviewing what was known about stray voltage. He discovered the earliest documented cases dated to the early 1960s in New Zealand, where barefoot dairy farmers felt tingling when touching metal pails and water troughs. Cows in North America showed similar patterns: refusing water, losing milk production, with infections and immune problems.

**Testing Cow Sensitivity to Electricity**

Reinemann designed a study to determine precisely how much electricity a cow must experience before reacting to it. At UW-Madison, using about 500 cows, his team placed individual animals in specially designed stalls with a scale to measure body weight shifts and flinching. They clipped electrodes to the cow's snout and all four hooves, then sent small pulses of electricity through the animal, gradually increasing the voltage. Researchers observed subtle responses: a head twitch, a leg kick, an ear movement—the tiniest indication the cow felt something unpleasant. When they asked Latif Nasser to place a 9-volt battery on his tongue, he experienced a sensation like "something really cold touching your tongue for a second"—roughly the behavioral response threshold in cows. The research established that at approximately 9 volts across a cow, behavioral changes become observable. Wisconsin set its regulatory threshold for acceptable farm stray voltage well below that point. Crucially, Reinemann noted that across more than 9,000 stray voltage investigations conducted in Wisconsin since 1990, fewer than 3% of farms exceeded this threshold. When the team looked beyond behavior to milk production, water intake, feed consumption, and blood chemistry, they found that the amount of electricity needed to cause actual harm to a cow was so high that it rarely occurred in real-world farming unless wiring was seriously damaged. As Reinemann put it, "Wires will always break... but in the rare case that does happen, you get a lot of stray voltage. Find it and fix it. It's not hard to find and it's not hard to fix."

**Nutritional Causes and Farm Modernization**

Veterinarian Don Sanders, with 50 years of practice, explained a primary cause of urine drinking: potassium deficiency. Cows lacking sufficient potassium, sodium, or other minerals will turn to drinking urine. Modern dairy breeds are not average animals but "high-performance athletes" selectively bred for maximum milk production. When their diet is not perfectly calibrated, they develop immune problems, udder infections, and urinary behaviors—not all at once, but over months when the deficiency persists. Nigel Cook, from UW-Madison's School of Veterinary Medicine, observed that "cows like hobbies"—they spend significant time at water troughs playing with and lapping water, especially when confined in barns. Cows are highly social and sensitive to overcrowding. In the 1990s, dairy farming underwent dramatic change. Tie-stall barns, where individual cows were confined to single stalls with personal water cups, gave way to modern free-stall barns where hundreds of cows moved freely but consumed from shared water troughs. This modernization coincided with tightening profit margins that pushed farmers to increase herd sizes dramatically—from 25,000 dairy herds in Wisconsin (mostly small tie-stalls) in 1999 to mega-dairies with thousands of animals. Cook noted that the standard design recommends three to four inches of water trough perimeter per cow, but in pressurized modern barns, farmers often provided only two inches, restricting access. For farmers struggling with the transition to larger herds and unfamiliar management challenges, the appearance of a technician offering "stray voltage" as an external culprit was psychologically compelling. As Cook observed, it is "a bitter pill to swallow" to be told "you're not a very good manager" when a farmer had excelled in the tie-stall system his father and grandfather had perfected. Blaming the utility provided "a boogeyman and it's not your fault it's somebody else's fault."

**The Resistance of a Cow: A Contested Measurement**

A fundamental dispute in stray voltage cases hinges on the electrical resistance of a cow. Electricity behavior is governed by voltage (the push), current (the flow), and resistance (the constraint). In the 1980s–90s, researchers studying confined, dry cows in tie-stall barns established that a cow's electrical resistance was approximately 500 ohms—roughly equivalent to wet human skin at 1,000 ohms. Regulatory standards were built around this 500-ohm figure. However, electrician Larry Neubauer contested this number. He pointed out that the 500-ohm measurement was conducted in tie-stall barns where cows stood relatively dry and isolated. In modern free-stall barns, cows spend hours standing in slurry—a highly conductive mix of manure, urine, and water or milk. Wet conditions dramatically reduce electrical resistance: cows become "bigger electricity straws," as Larry explained, comparing them to plugging in a drill versus jumping into a swimming pool with an electrical cord. In 2016, Idaho dairymen, Larry, and researcher Richard Norell conducted a study measuring resistance across 170 cows in six modern commercial dairies. Their findings: the resistance of cows in wet free-stall conditions was approximately 200 ohms—60% lower than the standard. At 200 ohms with one volt of stray current, a cow would experience four milliamps of current, double the regulatory threshold. Idaho Power declined to fund or support the research. When Latif asked Rick Norell whether policy should change based on the new evidence, Rick replied, "I believe it should, but I also believe that my data needs to be published and it needs to be critically evaluated." However, Rick then revealed that when he retired, he had packed a binder containing critical research materials and it has remained lost "in the state of Idaho" ever since—the data never formally published. Today, peer-reviewed literature and experts like Doug Reinemann continue to cite 500 ohms as the correct resistance, and the dispute remains unresolved.

**Outcomes and Unresolved Questions**

Gregus Christensen sold his dairy cows and became a potato farmer. His former cows, relocated to a different farm in a different part of Denmark, are reportedly thriving and drinking water normally. Jill Nelson, after years of being told she did not have stray voltage, contacted Larry Neubauer. He conducted tests, confirmed she did have stray voltage, spoke the language of power company technicians, and negotiated changes to her electrical system. Jill's cows returned to normal behavior. A second visit showed her herd healthy and ready for the Minnesota State Fair. Yet the episode closes with an unsettling reality: as demand for electricity grows to fuel AI data centers and energy-hungry infrastructure, power companies are planning massive new transmission lines carrying 500,000 volts across farmland. If the Idaho researchers are correct about lower cow resistance in modern conditions, and if new high-voltage infrastructure arrives, the stray voltage crisis could intensify. The episode ends with reporting about renewable energy transitions and electric vehicle infrastructure, hinting that the electricity/agriculture collision is far from over.

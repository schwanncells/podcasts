# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Corporate landlords aren't the real villain"

**Host:** Darian Woods, Wailin Wong. **Guests:** Steven Billings (professor of real estate, University of Colorado Boulder); Laurie Goodman (Housing Finance Policy Center director, Urban Institute); Adrian Todman (CEO, National Rental Home Council).

## TL;DR

- **Less than 1% of home purchases nationally are by institutional investors** — they are a scapegoat for housing affordability; the real drivers are low construction and low interest rates.
- **The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act restricts large investors from owning single-family homes** — the bill passed the Senate on a bipartisan basis but would likely worsen housing affordability by killing build-to-rent construction (7% of new homes).
- **Institutional investors reduce rental prices by increasing supply** — bringing more rental homes into the market benefits the roughly one-third of American families who rent.
- **Corporate landlords buy distressed properties and renovate them extensively** — they can finance repairs at much lower denial rates (40%+ for homeowners) and purchase materials in bulk; institutional investors typically buy homes in worse condition than average and spend tens of thousands on improvements.
- **Build-to-rent homes wouldn't exist without institutional investors** — the Senate bill requires new rental homes to be sold within 7 years, which would chill the entire build-to-rent sector and eliminate housing supply it was designed to increase.
- **Institutional ownership correlates with increased crime in some neighborhoods** — Billings found a 2% increase in property crime, 4% in violent crime, and 7% in drug crime when corporate landlords concentrate in areas compared to owner-occupied homes.
- **Renting in better neighborhoods opens pathways for low-income children** — research from Harvard economist Raj Chetty shows enormous long-term benefits for children from low-income families who mix with higher-income families and access better schools, with no detriment to higher-income children.

**The Rise of Institutional Investors in Single-Family Homes**

Steven Billings traces the story to the 2008 Great Recession, when homes across the country entered foreclosure. Investors recognized an opportunity to buy properties cheaply. Rather than flipping homes for quick profits, these investors realized that consistent rental income from a portfolio of properties was more lucrative. Finance firms then packaged these cash-flowing properties into investment products, including real estate investment trusts (REITs), which allowed investors to gain exposure to residential real estate without hands-on landlord work. This structure generated substantial capital inflow into the institutional residential market and became a booming industry.

The backlash emerged years later. When house prices surged in the early 2020s, politicians across the spectrum—from Democratic Senator Elizabeth Warren to Republican Vice President J.D. Vance—blamed institutional investors for the affordability crisis. Billings acknowledges there is "a grain of truth" to this claim: large institutional investor presence does drive up housing prices somewhat. However, the grain is precisely that—small. Across the country, institutional investors account for less than 1% of home purchases, making them minor players in the broader housing market.

**The Real Drivers of Housing Affordability**

Laurie Goodman emphasizes that the much larger drivers of housing prices are low construction and low interest rates. In fact, institutional investors often reduce rental prices by expanding rental supply. For approximately one-third of American families who rent, this increase in rental units moderates what they pay. Goodman notes that institutional investors tend to buy homes in worse condition than average, then renovate them systematically. They have specialized crews, buy materials in bulk, and can finance repairs at favorable rates. The denial rate on home improvement loans for owner-occupants exceeds 40%, whereas institutional investors face far lower barriers to capital for repairs. This efficiency allows them to quickly rehabilitate distressed properties.

Steven Billings' research paints a more nuanced picture. He finds that for comparable houses, institutional landlords actually file fewer renovation permits than other owners—yet they do tend to acquire homes requiring significant work and invest tens of thousands in rapid upgrades. Institutional investors also drive build-to-rent construction: approximately 7% of newly built homes are constructed specifically for rental by large institutions. These are homes that would likely not be built in the absence of institutional capital and expertise.

**The Senate Bill and Unintended Consequences**

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, passed on a bipartisan basis in the Senate, would ban large institutional investors from purchasing single-family homes. Within the bill's restrictions, any newly built rental homes must be sold within 7 years. Adrian Todman, CEO of the National Rental Home Council, warns this provision would "chill" build-to-rent development at inception. Builders would lose confidence in the buy-and-hold model that makes rental construction economically viable. Goodman frames the irony bluntly: "This is a bill designed to increase supply, and you're actually cutting off the activity that is designed to do exactly that, which doesn't make sense." The consequence would likely be reduced rental housing supply and higher costs for renters.

Billings agrees the Senate bill "goes too far" and notes the conservative argument for allowing more housing construction is sound. He observes that many tenants report good experiences with large management companies. Amanda Cantrell, a renter in Murfreesboro, Tennessee, initially concerned about corporate landlord dominance in her area, ultimately rented from an institutional provider and rated the experience a solid 4 out of 5 stars. Her rent even decreased slightly upon renewal.

**The Neighborhood Effects and Social Mobility**

Billings' research does identify a negative externality: when corporate landlords concentrate in a neighborhood, property crime increases by 2%, violent crime by 4%, and drug crime by 7% compared to areas with predominant owner-occupancy. The mechanism is unclear, but the correlation appears consistent. However, this cost must be weighed against a substantial benefit: rental homes enable low-income families to live in neighborhoods they otherwise could not afford as buyers. These families—locked out by down payment requirements and upfront capital costs—can access areas with better schools and social support through renting. Research from Harvard economist Raj Chetty demonstrates enormous long-term benefits for children from low-income families who grow up in higher-income neighborhoods, including higher lifetime earnings and intergenerational mobility, with no measurable harm to children from higher-income families. The CEO of Progress Residential, a major institutional landlord, credits his own childhood move to a better-resourced neighborhood—enabled by his parents' ability to rent rather than buy—with opening educational pathways he otherwise would not have had.

**Conclusion**

The evidence does not support the narrative that institutional investors are a major driver of housing affordability. Billings and Goodman both agree that cracking down on corporate home ownership and construction, as the Senate bill proposes, has a strong likelihood of worsening housing affordability. The villains in the housing crisis remain low construction and tight monetary policy, not the small fraction of institutional investors filling gaps in the market.

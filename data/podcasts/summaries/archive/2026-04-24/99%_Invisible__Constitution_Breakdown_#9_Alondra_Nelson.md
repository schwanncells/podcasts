# 99% Invisible — "Constitution Breakdown #9: Alondra Nelson"

**Host:** Roman Mars (with Elizabeth Joe). **Guest:** Dr. Alondra Nelson (scholar of technology and social science; Harold F. Linder chair, Institute for Advanced Study, Princeton; former acting director, White House Office of Science and Technology Policy; led development of Biden's Blueprint for an AI Bill of Rights).

## TL;DR

- **Supremacy Clause (Article 6, Clause 2) is the most frequently litigated constitutional doctrine** — it establishes that federal law overrides conflicting state law, but the Supreme Court has developed complex preemption tests over decades because Congress rarely explicitly states intent to override states.
- **No federal AI legislation exists, creating a regulatory vacuum** — Biden's 2023 executive order set agency guidelines but lacked authority to preempt state law; Trump rescinded it and called on Congress to use preemption to override state AI regulations, which Congress has not done.
- **California, Colorado, and Texas are pioneering different AI regulation approaches** — California requires data disclosure and generative AI transparency in police reports; Colorado passed an omnibus bill covering discrimination and child safety; Texas focuses narrowly on intentional discrimination, leaving unintentional harms unregulated.
- **The AI Bill of Rights (five principles: safety, non-discrimination, data privacy, notice/explanation, human alternatives)** — came from a year of broad public engagement in the Biden administration and became the basis for the 2023 executive order, drawing distinction between technical "safety" and substantive harm mitigation.
- **Algorithmic harms are happening across hiring, lending, policing, healthcare, and youth protection** — AI resume screeners exclude applicants before humans see credentials; predictive policing perpetuates historical biases; NYC's government chatbot advised illegal labor practices; generative AI chats induce self-harm in youth.
- **"Thick alignment" means AI governance must reflect diverse community values, not just technical metrics** — Nelson argues alignment is not solved by 98% accuracy if the system still misidentifies people; it requires continuous negotiation with affected communities and resistance to singular corporate "constitutions" like Anthropic's.
- **States are closer to harms and constituents; Maine banned data centers, Florida and Oklahoma introduced AI bills of rights, and communities are pushing back against secret data center deals** — Nelson sees growing bipartisan public empowerment to demand AI governance without needing PhD-level expertise.
- **Federal governance is unlikely soon (only one major tech law since 1996: the Communications Decency Act)** — but Nelson is optimistic about the "tech backlash" and public activism forcing companies to respond, even if Congress does not legislate.

**The Supremacy Clause and Federal Preemption**

The episode begins with the constitutional framework. Article 7 is straightforward: nine of thirteen states ratifying the Constitution made it legitimate (New Hampshire was the ninth, June 21, 1788). Article 6, Clause 1 guarantees payment of Revolutionary War debts, assuring creditors the new government will honor obligations. Article 6, Clause 3 bans religious tests for public office—notable for its absence after centuries of English common-law religious oaths.

Article 6, Clause 2—the Supremacy Clause—is the centerpiece. It states that the Constitution, federal laws made pursuant to it, and treaties are "the supreme law of the land." Before this, the Articles of Confederation had no such provision; state courts sometimes ignored federal law. The Supremacy Clause resolved that ambiguity. Congress can preempt (displace) state or local law by explicit text, but the Supreme Court has developed extensive preemption doctrines for cases where Congress legislates on a topic without explicitly preempting states. Sometimes courts find no conflict and allow both to coexist; sometimes they find state law an obstacle to federal goals or impossible to comply with both simultaneously. This is now the most frequently litigated constitutional doctrine—a possible question anytime the federal government regulates.

**AI as a Federalism Case Study**

AI regulation exemplifies the federalism puzzle. Neither state nor federal government has a clear mandate. California leads: requiring data disclosure for AI developers and generative AI transparency when police write reports. Colorado passed an omnibus bill covering discrimination, child safety, and deepfakes. Texas focuses narrowly on intentional discrimination, sidestepping unintentional algorithmic harms. Nelson notes these are not uniquely burdensome compared to other regulated industries (insurance, consumer protection, employment law, biometrics). The real problem is that absent federal leadership, states are filling a vacuum—and creating a patchwork that, while confusing, tests different approaches and may push Congress to act.

**What AI Is and How It Harms**

Nelson uses the OECD definition: machine-based systems using statistics and math to make inferences from inputs and generate outputs (predictions, recommendations, decisions, content). Examples range from Netflix recommendations to military targeting to resume screening. She distinguishes between static algorithms (criminal justice predictive systems) and increasingly autonomous agents. Generative AI generates text, images, and sound from massive training datasets.

Harms are concrete and widespread. Resume-screening AI filters applicants before humans see them, disproportionately eliminating candidates from historically underrepresented groups in fields like tech. Predictive policing tools say "police this neighborhood more because it was policed more historically"—circular logic with no new evidence. NYC's $1 million government chatbot (NYC Chat) told small businesses how to illegally skim workers' tips and discriminate against tenants. Generative AI chats are inducing self-harm and suicidal ideation in teenagers. Meanwhile, companies and lobbyists lobby for federal preemption so they don't have to navigate state-by-state rules.

**The AI Bill of Rights and Biden's Executive Order**

In October 2021, Nelson and the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy published an op-ed in Wired proposing an AI Bill of Rights, paralleling the Founders' response to a powerful new government. A year of broad public engagement (high school students, rabbis, tech companies, academics, government staff) distilled five principles:

1. **Safety and Effectiveness** — AI companies should test systems before release, anticipating known-risk use cases (scams, fraud, abuse material) even if not all harms are foreseeable.
2. **Protection from Algorithmic Discrimination** — historical bias in training data must not exclude people from opportunities.
3. **Data Privacy** — some guardrails on data collection and use.
4. **Notice and Explanation** — people should know when AI makes consequential decisions about their lives (jobs, loans, health insurance) and have a right to explanation.
5. **Human Alternative or Fallback** — the ability to opt out and talk to a human, especially for high-stakes decisions.

These principles became the basis for Biden's October 2022 executive order on AI, which directed federal agencies to update guidelines on education, labor, and national security (including export controls and the controversial proposal to use the Defense Production Act to require notification of powerful new systems). Trump rescinded the order and replaced it with a call for Congress to preempt state laws to accelerate AI development—Congress has not responded.

**The State Patchwork and Federalism Debate**

Nelson defends state regulation against the "compliance burden" complaint. Companies already navigate different state regimes for insurance, privacy, biometrics, and employment. The real leverage is that states are closer to constituent harms: governors and legislators hear directly from people harmed by AI. Parents worry about children being induced to self-harm. Job seekers are denied opportunities without explanation. The states are experimenting; some approaches will succeed, others fail—but at least decisions are being tested. Federal preemption would freeze the current model in place before anyone knows which one works.

She also notes that compliance pressures come from multiple directions: tariff changes, immigration policy, government procurement standards, and shareholder pressure in companies like Nvidia and Intel. The question of who regulates is not settled.

**Advanced AI and "Thick Alignment"**

When pressed on AGI (artificial general intelligence) and existential risk, Nelson rejects the frame. She prefers "advanced AI" and points to concrete concerns. DOGE (the Trump administration's government efficiency initiative) was reported to break the Privacy Act of 1974 by enabling interoperability of federal datasets into a surveillance panopticon. Powerful autonomous agents already occasionally delete files or emails unintentionally. The question is not whether superintelligence is possible but whether companies can be told not to ship systems without controls: mandatory third-party testing, limits on compute/data access, human override, staged deployment.

She introduces "thick alignment"—drawing from philosopher Gilbert Ryle and anthropologist Clifford Geertz on "thick description." Alignment is not a technical problem solved by 98% accuracy; it requires understanding context and listening to communities. When facial recognition systems misidentify people (as happened to six+ people in Detroit), a developer somewhere said the system was aligned. Anthropic's constitution for AI is one company's values—Nelson asks who else gets to weigh in. She cites Florida Governor DeSantis's Florida AI Bill of Rights and Oklahoma's proposed AI bill, both adopting her five principles plus stronger safeguards, suggesting bipartisan agreement is possible on some values.

**Public Momentum and Limited Federal Hope**

Nelson is pessimistic about federal legislation (only one major tech law since 1996: the Communications Decency Act). But she finds encouragement in growing bipartisan public sentiment against AI harms. Data center governance is becoming tactile and political—Maine banned them, major projects were stalled, and communities are pushing back against secret NDAs. Young people are speaking up. Even conservatives are raising concerns about bias and discrimination. The "tech backlash" is not monolithic, but it is diverse and growing, and it does not require PhDs to participate.

She closes on a note of realism: the biggest AI proponents are also the loudest doomsayers, feeding into a power narrative. But the genuine coalition forming around data centers, youth safety, job protection, and civil rights suggests that if Congress does not act, the states and public will continue to force the issue.

# The Economics of Everyday Things — "28. Horseshoe Crab Blood"

**Host:** Zachary Crockett. **Guest:** Dina Fine-Marin (senior reporter covering wildlife crime at National Geographic).

## TL;DR

- **$60,000 per gallon market value** — horseshoe crab blood is considered a "medical equivalent of gold" because it contains Limulus Amebocyte Lysate (LAL), which is uniquely hypersensitive to bacterial endotoxins.
- **Critical pharmaceutical testing** — the LAL test is used to ensure injectable medications, vaccines, and implantable devices are free of toxins before entering the human body.
- **718,000 crabs harvested in 2021** — biomedical firms contract fishermen to scoop up the crabs for blood collection; roughly 15% of the bled crabs die in labs, with an unknown number dying after release.
- **Red knot shorebirds depend on crab eggs** — the over-harvesting of horseshoe crabs for both biomedical use and fishing bait is linked to the population decline of red knots, which feed heavily on the eggs during migration.
- **Synthetic alternatives are gaining traction** — Eli Lilly transitioned 80% of its endotoxin testing to a synthetic alternative by 2016, though many companies remain reluctant to switch due to higher costs.
- **450 million years of resilience** — horseshoe crabs have survived mass extinctions and predated the dinosaurs, yet they now face a dual threat from the biomedical and bait-fishing industries that may reduce populations by 30% over the next four decades.

**The Biomedical Value of Ancient Blood**

Horseshoe crabs are not actually crabs but are more closely related to scorpions and spiders, having existed for roughly 450 million years. In the 1960s, infectious disease expert Frederick Bang discovered that components in the animal's milky-blue blood clot when they encounter bacterial endotoxins. This biological mechanism acts as a defense system, forming a semi-solid shell around bacteria to keep the crab safe. Pharmaceutical companies now utilize this sensitivity for LAL testing, which identifies trace amounts of endotoxins in medications and medical devices that could otherwise be fatal to humans.

**The Harvesting Industry and Ecological Costs**

Five companies in the United States are licensed to harvest horseshoe crabs for medical purposes, operating with high levels of secrecy. Typically, they contract fishermen to collect hundreds of crabs from shores or with trawl nets. Once transported to labs, live crabs are bled for approximately one-third of their total blood volume before being returned to the wild. The practice is controversial; besides the 15% mortality rate in labs, evidence suggests the blood loss leaves the animals lethargic, reducing their ability to return to beaches to spawn. Environmental groups warn that the combination of biomedical harvesting and the use of crabs as bait for eel fishing threatens the species, which is now listed as vulnerable by the International Union for Conservation of Nature.

**The Transition Toward Synthetic Alternatives**

The future of horseshoe crab harvesting remains uncertain as demand for gene therapies and personalized medicine continues to grow. While the pharmaceutical industry has historically relied on the crabs because no widely available alternative existed, significant progress has been made in developing synthetic endotoxin tests. Pharmaceutical giant Eli Lilly has successfully moved the vast majority of its product testing to synthetic methods, proving the feasibility of the technology. However, the higher cost of these synthetics remains a primary barrier for smaller firms and the industry at large, ensuring that the ancient mating rituals of the horseshoe crab continue to be disrupted by human pharmaceutical needs for the foreseeable future.

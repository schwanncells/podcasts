# Science Friday — "Can algae help pull microplastics out of our water supply?"

**Host:** Kathleen Davis (Science Friday producer). **Guests:** Kate Grumke (senior environmental reporter, St. Louis Public Radio); Dr. Susie Day (professor of chemical and biological engineering, University of Missouri; lead researcher on the engineered algae microplastics study).

## TL;DR

- **Engineered algae removed more than 90% of microplastics** in lab water samples — and is especially effective at capturing the smallest, hardest-to-filter nanoscale particles.
- **The mechanism is hydrophobicity:** the algae is water-repelling, and microplastics are too, so the algae naturally clumps onto plastic particles and drags them to the bottom. Results published in *Nature Communications*.
- **EPA announced its first regulatory step on microplastics in drinking water** — described as a sign that institutional attention is beginning to match scientific concern.
- **The algae is a GMO and will not be released into the environment** — Day envisions it deployed in sealed bioreactors at wastewater treatment plants or other contained water-treatment facilities.
- **Dual benefit: the algae also strips excess nitrogen and phosphorus from wastewater** — nutrients that most current wastewater infrastructure doesn't address.
- **The microplastics discovery was an accident** — Day originally engineered the algae as an aviation biofuel feedstock. The hydrophobic self-precipitation behavior observed in fuel research led her team to test it on plastics; it worked.
- **Other Midwest research approaches:** a St. Louis University lab is developing a whale-baleen-inspired filter, and Washington University has a center focused on biodegradable plastic alternatives, particularly for textile fibers.
- **Microplastics were found in a sealed Missouri cave** closed to the public for 30 years, including in water and sediment — with concentrations spiking during flood events as connected cave systems pull in outside water.

**The Algae Microplastics Removal Process**

A research team at the University of Missouri, led by Dr. Susie Day, found that a strain of engineered algae could capture microplastics from water with greater than 90% removal efficiency. The mechanism exploits a shared property: both the algae and microplastics are hydrophobic, meaning they repel water. The algae naturally clumps onto plastic particles and pulls them to the bottom of the water column, concentrating the contamination into a removable sludge. Day's team published the findings in *Nature Communications* and proposes that the resulting algae-plastic sludge could potentially be recycled into new materials rather than disposed of as waste. The process is particularly effective at capturing the smallest fragments, which are the hardest to remove with conventional filtration.

**Scalability and Containment**

Day acknowledges scalability is the key open question. Because the algae is genetically modified, she is firm that it will not be released freely into natural waterways. The intended deployment model is a closed bioreactor — a controlled environment where temperature, pH, and other parameters can be tuned for optimal growth. Wastewater treatment plants are the primary candidate venue, though Day notes the platform could be integrated anywhere a suitable water body and light source exist. The algae grows using CO2 and sunlight as inputs, which she sees as an additional environmental benefit.

**A Dual-Purpose Platform**

Beyond microplastics, Day highlights a second benefit that current wastewater systems don't address: excess nitrogen and phosphorus. Most wastewater infrastructure is designed to manage organic carbon and oxygen levels, leaving nutrient runoff largely untreated. The algae consumes those excess nutrients as it grows. Day describes the goal as "one stone for multiple birds" — a single platform that simultaneously tackles microplastics (an emerging contaminant still under active study) and nutrient pollution (a well-documented environmental harm).

**Accidental Discovery**

Day's original research goal had nothing to do with microplastics. She engineered the algae as a feedstock for aviation biofuel — the compound it produces has a higher energy density than ethanol, making it a stronger candidate for aviation applications than the 10% bioethanol blends common in car fuel. The path to microplastics remediation came when two parallel research threads collided: one team was studying the algae's self-precipitation behavior, another was exploring fungal biomass as a microplastics absorbent. When they observed the algae sinking on its own, the team asked whether the same process would work on plastics. "Bingo, it happens," Day said. She drew a broader lesson: researchers should maintain an open mind beyond the stated goals of any single project, because collaboration across research lines is where unexpected breakthroughs emerge.

**Other Local Research Approaches**

Kate Grumke highlighted two other microplastics efforts in the St. Louis area. At St. Louis University, a researcher is building a filter inspired by whale baleen — the fibrous, comb-like structure whales use to strain plankton from seawater. The observation is that plankton and microplastics are similar in size, suggesting the same physical mechanism could capture plastic particles. At Washington University in St. Louis, a research center is focused on developing biodegradable plastic alternatives with an emphasis on textile fibers, which are a significant source of microplastic contamination in water systems.

**How Pervasive the Problem Has Become**

Grumke reported on a St. Louis University study that found microplastics inside a Missouri cave that has been closed to the public for 30 years to protect an endangered bat population. Plastic was detected in both the water and sediment — and concentrations increased during flooding, as the cave system's connections to outside waterways drew in surface runoff. For Grumke, the cave finding crystallized the scale of the problem: if microplastics are accumulating in a sealed environment no human has entered in decades, conventional assumptions about contamination sources don't hold.

# Unchained — "How Bitcoin Is Both a Risk Asset and a Hedge Against Debasement"

**Host:** Steve Ehrlich (head of research at Sharplink). **Guest:** Jim Ferrioli (Director of Crypto Strategy and Research at Charles Schwab; formerly covered crypto at large financial institutions since 2020; background in equities investing, equities trading, and financial advising).

## TL;DR

- **Bitcoin is a risk asset, not a safe haven** — when credit spreads widen, Bitcoin sells off; the only narrow exception was spring 2023 bank runs, when it briefly acted as a flight-to-safety asset.
- **Bitcoin is simultaneously a valid debasement hedge** — launched in 2009 near $0, trading near $70,000 today; over the same period, US debt and money supply expanded dramatically and dollar purchasing power declined. Being a risk asset and a store of value are not mutually exclusive.
- **Bitcoin trades at ~0.75x the inefficient miner cost of production** — efficient miners now produce Bitcoin at roughly $65,000; historical bear markets have typically bottomed around inefficient miner costs, with deep bear markets reaching efficient miner costs. Bitcoin hit ~$60,000 intraday in February.
- **Smart contract platforms are valued via a "crypto Buffett indicator"** — Ferrioli divides market cap by trailing one-year total network fees (analogous to GDP), treating Ethereum and Solana as competing micro-economies. This metric has traded in reliable ranges and can signal when one platform is relatively cheap or expensive.
- **Bitcoin's multi-year correlation to equities is only 0.3** — very low predictive power; in the short term it can get correlated to momentum baskets (AI stocks, private credit), but it currently shows low correlation to stocks, bonds, and commodities simultaneously.
- **Tokenization may decouple smart contract platform activity from crypto market momentum** — $350 billion in tokenized assets exist (including stablecoins); Ethereum holds the lion's share of real-world assets, with the next largest competitor at roughly $80 billion. As tokenization grows, network activity will increasingly be independent of crypto market cap direction.
- **Three long-term Bitcoin price drivers: money supply growth, Bitcoin supply growth, and adoption** — as adoption matures and supply growth declines, Bitcoin may eventually behave like a "boring, stable asset" growing at roughly 6% per year in line with long-run US money supply growth.
- **Quantum risk is a bear market boogeyman, not an existential threat** — Ferrioli dismisses it as a narrative that emerges in downturns; quantum threats would hit banks and weapons systems before Bitcoin, and the network will upgrade its cryptography when needed.

**Bitcoin as Risk Asset vs. Safe Haven vs. Debasement Hedge**

Ferrioli draws a sharp distinction between three things that are often conflated: safe haven asset, risk asset, and debasement hedge. He argues Bitcoin is clearly a risk asset — on risk-off days, crypto sells off alongside equities. The narrow exception he identifies is acute financial system stress, specifically the spring 2023 bank runs, when Bitcoin briefly rallied as a flight-to-safety asset. That use case aligns with Bitcoin's origin: launched in 2009 during the financial crisis as an alternate monetary system not linked to central banks.

At the same time, Ferrioli argues Bitcoin is a genuine hedge against monetary debasement, pointing to its price appreciation from 2009 to today against a backdrop of large US debt expansion and dollar purchasing power erosion. He draws an analogy to equities: stocks are also a risk asset that sells off in downturns, yet they have been one of the best long-run stores of value against debasement. Narratives, he argues, diverged from reality when "digital gold" comparisons led investors to expect Bitcoin to behave like physical gold during acute market stress — a property gold has but Bitcoin does not currently possess.

**Bitcoin Valuation: Miner Cost of Production**

Ferrioli applies an equities-style relative value framework to Bitcoin using miner cost of production as a floor. He distinguishes efficient miners — those with best-in-class equipment and lowest energy costs, currently producing Bitcoin at roughly $65,000 — from inefficient miners with older equipment and higher costs. Historically, Bitcoin has peaked at large multiples above efficient miner cost and troughed around inefficient miner cost; deep bear markets occasionally reach efficient miner cost, which is the more extreme floor.

As of the episode, Bitcoin is trading at approximately 0.75x the inefficient miner cost of production and right at the efficient miner floor. Bitcoin hit $60,000 intraday in February, near the 200-week moving average at that time and a level at which on-chain data historically signaled market bottoms. Ferrioli notes that Bitcoin may not reach the absolute bottom of its historical range in every cycle — analogous to a stock with a price-to-earnings range of 10–20x that might only derate to 12–13x before recovering — but characterizes the current zone as historically solid support. He adds that leverage has been largely wiped out, with significant bad news already priced in.

**Smart Contract Platform Valuation: A Crypto Buffett Indicator**

For Ethereum, Solana, and other smart contract platforms, Ferrioli constructs an analog to Warren Buffett's market cap-to-GDP indicator. He treats a blockchain's trailing one-year total fees — including fees from applications built on Layer 2 networks, not just the base layer — as the equivalent of GDP, representing the total output of goods and services produced on that decentralized economy. Dividing market cap by that fees figure yields a valuation ratio that can be compared historically and across platforms.

He notes this approach is currently backward-looking (trailing fees only, no forward estimates), but expects forward fee projections to develop over time just as equity analysts build earnings forecasts. The metric has traded in reliable ranges, making it useful for gauging relative richness or cheapness within the altcoin space. Ferrioli also notes that altcoins can sometimes reach their valuation peaks before Bitcoin does, giving active investors a signal to rotate back toward Bitcoin or reduce exposure.

Roughly 50% of Ethereum's fees last year were generated by stablecoin usage, about 20% by liquid staking, and 20% by lending — all activities that are directly tied to the broader crypto market cap. Trading accounted for the remainder. This means Ethereum's "GDP" is currently highly correlated with overall crypto market momentum, though tokenization of traditional financial assets could change that dynamic.

**Correlations and Current Market Structure**

Ferrioli observes that Bitcoin's long-run correlation to any single asset class is low. Over a multi-year horizon, its highest correlation is to equities at approximately 0.3 — not enough for meaningful predictive power. In the 2022–2024 period, Bitcoin became correlated to a specific momentum basket (AI stocks, private credit), largely because both Bitcoin and a handful of momentum names drove most of the market's returns while the broader market was flat. That short-term correlation has since broken down; Bitcoin's correlation to stocks, bonds, and commodities is now simultaneously quite low.

Ferrioli argues this creates an interesting risk-reward setup. If a macro-driven selloff occurs — recession fears, oil shock, a surge in unemployment — Bitcoin will likely fall alongside the market, but may not fall three times as far as equities as its historical volatility ratio would suggest. And because it is already down roughly 50% from its highs, the downside may be more contained in this particular scenario, while the upside leverage remains intact when risk appetite returns.

**Tokenization and the Ethereum Thesis**

Ferrioli argues Ethereum is best positioned to win the tokenization opportunity. He cites network effects and first-mover advantage — anyone tokenizing assets or accepting tokenized assets wants exposure to the largest network, just as app developers target the largest mobile platform or cloud providers. Ethereum already holds the dominant share of real-world assets excluding stablecoins; including stablecoins, the gap to the next-largest competitor is roughly $350 billion to $80 billion.

Solana, he notes, is better suited to payments requiring rapid, large-scale transactions. Private blockchains will serve institutions that don't want all financial transactions on a public ledger. But for broad-access tokenized assets, Ethereum's industry-standard position and TVL dominance are durable competitive advantages. He frames Ethereum's recent price underperformance — it lagged the 2024 bull market until Ethereum treasury companies began forming last summer — as a case where fundamentals are beginning to matter more and a new narrative is developing around real use cases rather than speculative momentum.

**Quantum Risk**

Ferrioli dismisses quantum computing risk as a bear market narrative that surfaces predictably when Bitcoin's price is depressed. He notes that quantum threats would reach banks, government systems, and weapons platforms before they could meaningfully threaten Bitcoin's cryptographic layer. He also notes that every encryption scheme in history has eventually been cracked — but argues the Bitcoin network's open-source, upgrade-capable nature means it will adapt when the threat is genuine. For wallets that are inaccessible and cannot be upgraded, he treats any lost funds as analogous to cash dropped in the street: unfortunate but not a systemic problem. His bottom line: "I really don't see this as an existential crisis at all."

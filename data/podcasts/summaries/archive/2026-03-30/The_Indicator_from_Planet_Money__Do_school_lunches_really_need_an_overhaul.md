# The Indicator from Planet Money — "Do school lunches really need an overhaul?"

**Host:** Waylon Wong. **Guests:** Julia Ritchie (NPR producer, field reporter for this episode); Angela McLaughlin (executive director of nutrition services, Charleston County School District); Diane Pratt-Hevner (spokesperson, School Nutrition Association — represents 50,000 K-12 nutrition workers nationwide).

## TL;DR

- **Trump administration wants to overhaul school lunches again** — Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins told Fox News that schools should return to cooking from scratch, noting "a lot of our schools don't even have an oven anymore."
- **The Biden administration's 2024 guidelines are still phasing in** — schools this year must limit added sugar in milk, cereal, and yogurt; a weekly cap on added-sugar calories across all breakfasts and lunches takes effect in 2027.
- **Federal reimbursement for free-lunch students is $4.71 per meal; for full-price students it's just $0.44** — Charleston County targets a cost of $2.15 per meal to stay within budget.
- **School budgets split roughly 40–45% on food/supplies, 40–45% on labor, with little left** — bone-in chicken wings cost over $3 per serving, so the district pairs them with cheaper items to average down.
- **Trump's proposed changes include more protein, fewer ultra-processed foods, and more scratch cooking** — the School Nutrition Association says budget, staffing, equipment, and infrastructure make scratch cooking a difficult mandate.
- **Nearly 900 school districts signed an open letter to the administration** — expressing concern about guidelines that emphasize costly meat while limiting fiber, and asking where the funding would come from.
- **School meals are already among the healthiest Americans eat** — Pratt-Hevner says schools are meeting current calorie, sodium, added-sugar, and fat limits, and research backs this up.

**The Current State of School Lunch**

Reporter Julia Ritchie visited Johns Island Elementary School near Charleston, South Carolina, where lunch starts at 11 a.m. and students choose from a hot meal (that day: teriyaki or orange chicken with broccoli) or a cold option (a PB&J Uncrustable with goldfish crackers). The school also runs a salad bar. Angela McLaughlin, executive director of nutrition services for the district, describes running 80 schools as "running 80 restaurants every single day."

Charleston County serves roughly 16,000 kids at breakfast and 30,000 at lunch across its schools. The meal program is self-funded — not drawn from the district's general operating budget — through federal reimbursements and meal sales. Every dollar matters.

**The Budget Arithmetic**

The USDA reimburses up to $4.71 per lunch for students who qualify for free meals, but only $0.44 for students who pay full price. McLaughlin targets a cost of $2.15 per meal. Food and supplies consume 40–45% of the budget; labor another 40–45%; the rest covers indirect and direct costs. The hot plate of chicken, brown rice, broccoli, and fruit on the day of the visit came in at $1.64. Higher-cost items like bone-in chicken wings (over $3 per serving) require pairing with cheaper sides to keep the average in range.

McLaughlin also credits student choice with reducing food waste and costs. Rather than loading a standard tray, kids pick three of five components, with at least one being a fruit or vegetable. This "affords them more buy-in to the process" and directly lowers food costs.

**What the Trump Administration Is Proposing**

Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins has called for a return to cooking meals from scratch, arguing that kitchens have lost their ovens and that the government should "completely and fundamentally change the system" to serve "whole food and nutrients." The administration's proposed changes include larger protein portions, fewer ultra-processed foods, and more from-scratch preparation.

These proposals land while the Biden administration's 2024 rules are still being implemented, with the added-sugar caps for all meal components not fully in effect until 2027.

**Industry Pushback and the Open Letter**

Diane Pratt-Hevner of the School Nutrition Association, whose 50,000 members include nutrition workers across the country, says schools have been trying to increase scratch cooking for years but face hard constraints: budget, staffing, equipment, and kitchen infrastructure. "They will always rely on food companies to supply some of the items that they're offering with school meals," she said.

Nearly 900 school districts and nutrition professionals co-signed an open letter to the administration this month. Their concerns go beyond kitchen costs — they also object to guidelines that prioritize expensive meat while restricting fiber. The USDA said in response that reimbursement rates are adjusted annually to keep pace with food costs.

**Schools Are Already Meeting Nutrition Standards**

Pratt-Hevner pushed back on the premise that school lunches need a major overhaul. "Schools are already meeting these calories, sodium, added sugar and fat limits," she said. Research shows school meals are among the healthiest Americans consume, precisely because they already must meet federal standards. The bigger challenge, McLaughlin says, is engagement — keeping kids interested in eating food that's good for them. "I think it's keeping them engaged and making sure that we are treating them as the very intelligent consumers they are."

**Fable's Shutdown and the Jailbreak Concession**

Fable was described by the hosts as "Mythos wrapped in guardrails" — an extremely powerful Anthropic model released with unusual caution. Ten early testers uncovered roughly 50 zero-days in infrastructure before public release. The model shipped with a hard fallback to Claude Opus when flagged, which users found annoying but accepted because the underlying capability was so valued.

The shutdown was triggered when a "highly credible trusted partner" reported a jailbreak to the government. David Sachs issued an export control order halting Fable. Kane's reading of the critical mistake: Dario told the administration there is "no way to perfectly prevent jailbreaks." The hosts argue that admission handed regulators a clean rationale for shutdown — no offensive use needed to be demonstrated.

**The Precedent, Not the Shutdown Itself**

Kane frames the episode's central warning as about precedent, not the individual product loss. The government now has a confirmed data point: it can shut down an AI model, and the market will not punish it for doing so. He draws an explicit parallel to crypto's DOJ crackdowns — the public didn't react, the government learned it could act with impunity, and the template was set for future enforcement. Dario was at the G7 alongside world leaders at the moment of shutdown, which the hosts treat as darkly ironic evidence of how fast the situation moved.

**How Anthropic Should Have Handled It**

The hosts argue Anthropic made the wrong call in its government communication. Kane's position is that you don't concede the impossibility of jailbreak prevention — you present data on mitigation, active monitoring, and the absence of any confirmed offensive use. Luca's prescription is structural: bring in ex-government professionals who live in Washington, DC and understand how to navigate political risk, rather than sending engineers to Capitol Hill. The crypto industry learned this lesson through DOJ enforcement; the AI industry, the hosts suggest, is learning it the same hard way.

**MicroStrategy's 32 BTC Sale and Bitcoin's Belief Problem**

Kane uses the MicroStrategy episode to make a broader point about Bitcoin's market structure. Saylor sold 32 BTC on June 1, which triggered a cascading panic disproportionate to the size of the sale. Two weeks later he bought back 1,587 BTC. Kane's explanation: Bitcoin has no cash-flow fundamentals, no DCF to anchor valuation. When the asset is purely belief-based, "if one guy has too much and might dump, you get out of the way." The market has allowed one entity to accumulate a position so large that even a minor signal of uncertainty produces outsized fear.

**Coinbase Announcements: Bull Case vs. UX Reality**

Porter Stoll, as a former Coinbase insider, reads the recent announcements bullishly. The Deribit acquisition signals a deliberate move into derivatives. Tokenized stocks, described by Coinbase as carrying "no derivatives, no IOUs," appear to involve 1-for-1 custody in Porter's read, though the exact mechanism is not publicly clear. The Base MCP and X402 integrations are framed as infrastructure for agentic payments. Stoll's thesis is that Coinbase is building toward a single destination for both retail and institutional customers.

Luca pushes back on the bull case from a product angle: Coinbase's core user experience remains broken — pages don't load, trades don't fill at mark price. Kane adds a market-structure data point: during the SpaceX IPO, Hyperliquid's perpetuals contracts outperformed tokenized stock structures, suggesting the tokenized equity product has real competition from existing crypto-native instruments.

**Pudgy Penguins' Igloo: DAO Tokens as Listed Securities**

Luca reveals Igloo, a new financial instrument designed to list DAO tokens directly on the NYSE or Nasdaq with 1-for-1 parity to crypto markets. The structure explicitly defines the token as a security, which Luca argues is the enabling move — it allows protocol revenue to be distributed directly to shareholders. The primary barrier to entry is a $10–20M underwriting cost, which limits the instrument to protocols with sufficient scale.

**Europe, MiCA, and the Innovation Hub Question**

Binance was rejected for a MiCA license, which Kane treats as a signal rather than an isolated event. He is broadly bearish on Europe as an innovation destination for either AI or crypto, citing regulatory compression of outcomes, wealth taxes, the absence of any major AI or crypto exchanges based in Europe, and a DAO governance analogy — consensus-driven regulation tends to produce the lowest-common-denominator outcome rather than permissive conditions for new entrants.
